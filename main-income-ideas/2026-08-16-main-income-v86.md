### 1. Tattoo & Body Piercing Studio State Body-Art Licensure & Bloodborne Pathogen/Sterilization Compliance
**Problem:** Tattoo and body piercing studios operate under state or county body-art licensure — California's Safe Body Art Act (Health & Safety Code § 119300 et seq.) and Texas Health & Safety Code Chapter 146's DSHS-administered body art studio license among them — which requires every studio to maintain a written OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens exposure control plan (29 CFR 1910.1030), keep every artist current on annual BBP training, and prove every autoclave in service is functioning correctly through a weekly biological-indicator (spore) test with the result documented and retained — a studio unable to produce a passing spore-test result for a specific week during a health-department inspection is exactly the finding that triggers an immediate operations suspension, since an unverified autoclave puts every client tattooed or pierced since the last passing test at risk of an infection-control citation. Most states also require each individual artist to hold their own registration or certification, renewed annually and often tied to a specific number of documented infection-control CE hours, and an artist working on a lapsed individual registration exposes the studio to the same violation as operating with no license at all. A small multi-chair studio running several artists and multiple autoclaves manages per-artist registration currency, weekly spore-test documentation across every sterilizer, and BBP training renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a paper logbook taped to the autoclave.
**What to build:** A weekly per-autoclave spore-test log with a "cleared to sterilize" gate blocking equipment from being logged as in-service once a test is missed or fails; a per-artist state registration and BBP-training renewal tracker with a "cleared to work" gate; and a health-department inspection-readiness export bundling both records.
**Skill fit:** State body-art licensure renewal cycles and the weekly spore-test cadence are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-artist and per-equipment record requirement, and the sterilization-verification log plus the work-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "require the record before the action proceeds / block the action until the credential clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a skin-penetration trade most small studios currently track on a paper logbook with no systematic cross-check between a specific autoclave's weekly test result and its continued use.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the spore-test log, the artist registration/BBP tracker, and the inspection-readiness export; land the first 5-8 studios through Alliance of Professional Tattooists (APT) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Alliance of Professional Tattooists (APT) member directory and state body-art licensing board public license-holder lists, where small studios already juggle weekly spore-test documentation and per-artist registration renewal with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $15/mo per artist tracked. Average client (5 artists) = $199 + $75 = $274/mo. 32-40 studios × $274/mo = **$8,768-10,960/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every autoclave generates its own weekly spore-test event, every artist's registration and BBP training renews on its own independent cycle — a continuous, per-equipment and per-artist compliance stream tied directly to the studio's chair count.
**Biggest risk:** A studio unable to produce a passing spore-test result for a specific week, or an artist working on a lapsed registration, is exactly the finding a health-department inspection is built to catch and can trigger an immediate suspension — the sterilization log and the work-eligibility gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → spore-test log, artist tracker, and inspection-export deployed, first 5-8 studio retainers through APT outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 studios. Replaces-full-time-salary → 32-40 studios, pushing past $8.77-10.96K/mo.

### 2. Medical Spa (Non-Physician-Owned Aesthetic Practice) State Medical Board Delegation & Supervision Compliance
**Problem:** Medical spas offering laser hair removal, IPL, chemical peels, and neuromodulator or dermal-filler injections performed by RNs, PAs, or NPs operate under state medical board physician-delegation rules — the Medical Board of California's framework under Business & Professions Code §§ 2052 and 2725, and Florida's Board of Medicine delegation rules under Fla. Admin. Code 64B8-9.009 among them — that require a currently valid, provider-specific standing order or protocol from a supervising physician before any delegated procedure can be performed, plus a documented physician-availability standard (commonly requiring the physician be reachable or periodically on-site), and a treatment performed under a lapsed or missing standing order is exactly the finding a state medical board complaint investigation targets first, with unlicensed-practice-of-medicine exposure falling on both the treating provider and the supervising physician. Every laser and energy-based device used must also carry current FDA clearance documentation and, in most states, a device-specific operator training record for each provider who touches it, and a spa unable to produce that training record during a board inspection faces the same exposure as an untrained provider operating without any delegation at all. A small multi-provider med spa running several RNs and PAs across a rotating client schedule manages per-provider standing-order currency, physician-availability documentation, and per-device training-record tracking with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-provider standing-order and delegation-agreement renewal tracker with a "cleared to treat" gate blocking a procedure booking once a standing order lapses; a physician-availability log documenting the supervising physician's required presence or reachability; and a per-device FDA-clearance and operator-training-record tracker.
**Skill fit:** State medical board delegation-rule renewal cycles and FDA device-clearance records are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-provider and per-device record requirement, and the treat-eligibility gate plus the training-record tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fast-growing aesthetic-medicine trade most small med spas currently track on a shared drive folder with no systematic cross-check between a specific provider's standing-order status and the procedure they're about to book.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the standing-order tracker with the treat-eligibility gate, the physician-availability log, and the device/training-record tracker; land the first 5-8 med spas through American Med Spa Association (AmSpa) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Med Spa Association (AmSpa) member directory and regional med spa conference networks, where small practices already juggle per-provider delegation-agreement renewal and device-training documentation with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $20/mo per provider tracked. Average client (6 providers) = $299 + $120 = $419/mo. 22-28 med spas × $419/mo = **$9,218-11,732/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every provider's standing order renews on its own independent cycle, every device requires its own ongoing training-record currency, and every new hire opens a fresh delegation-agreement compliance event — a continuous, per-provider compliance stream tied directly to the spa's staffing.
**Biggest risk:** A procedure performed under a lapsed standing order, or by a provider with no documented device-training record, is exactly the finding a state medical board investigation is built to catch — the treat-eligibility gate and the training-record tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → standing-order tracker, availability log, and device-training tracker deployed, first 5-8 med spa retainers through AmSpa outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-15 med spas. Replaces-full-time-salary → 22-28 med spas, pushing past $9.22-11.73K/mo.

### 3. Locksmith & Electronic Security/Burglar Alarm Installation Company State Licensure & Technician Registration Compliance
**Problem:** Locksmith and electronic security/burglar alarm installation companies operate under state locksmith licensure — Texas's PSB locksmith license under Occupations Code Chapter 1702 among them — and a separate state alarm-company/alarm-agent registration such as California BSIS's Alarm Company Operator license, both of which require every individual technician to hold their own registration card tied to a fingerprint-based background check, renewed on a fixed cycle, and a technician dispatched to a client's home or business on a lapsed registration is exactly the finding a state licensing-board complaint investigation targets first, since both trades give employees unsupervised access to a client's physical security. Alarm installations must additionally follow the UL 681 installation standard, and many municipalities layer on their own alarm-permit requirement per installed system with a separate false-alarm-fee schedule — a system installed without the required local alarm permit on file is exactly the gap a municipal police-department audit is built to expose. A small locksmith-and-alarm company running several field technicians across a mixed residential/commercial book manages per-technician registration and background-check currency, company license renewal, and per-installation municipal permit tracking with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-technician state registration and background-check renewal tracker with a "cleared to dispatch" gate blocking a job assignment once a registration lapses; a company locksmith/alarm-license renewal tracker; and a per-installation municipal alarm-permit tracker flagging any system installed without a permit on file.
**Skill fit:** State locksmith and alarm-company licensing cycles and municipal alarm-permit requirements are public, structured state and local regulatory data with a well-defined per-technician and per-installation record requirement, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the permit tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the credential clears / confirm the required record is on file" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a private-security trade most small companies currently track on a paper technician-card binder with no systematic cross-check between a technician's registration status and today's dispatch schedule.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the technician registration tracker with the dispatch-eligibility gate, the company license tracker, and the municipal permit tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through Electronic Security Association (ESA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Electronic Security Association (ESA) member directory and Associated Locksmiths of America (ALOA) chapter meetings, where small locksmith-and-alarm companies already juggle per-technician registration renewal and municipal alarm-permit tracking with no dedicated compliance staff.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every technician's state registration renews on its own independent cycle, every new installation opens its own municipal permit event, and the company license renews on its own schedule — a continuous, per-technician and per-installation compliance stream tied directly to the company's field-crew size.
**Biggest risk:** A technician dispatched on a lapsed registration, or an alarm system installed without the required municipal permit, is exactly the finding a state licensing-board or municipal police-department audit is built to catch — the dispatch-eligibility gate and the permit tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → technician tracker, company-license tracker, and permit tracker deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through ESA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 28-35 companies, pushing past $8.99-11.24K/mo.

### 4. Secondhand Dealer & Scrap/Precious Metals Recycler State Licensure & Law-Enforcement Reporting Compliance
**Problem:** Secondhand dealers and scrap/precious-metals recyclers operate under state secondhand-dealer and secondary-metals-recycler statutes — Florida's Secondhand Dealers and Secondary Metals Recyclers Act (Fla. Stat. Chapter 538) and Georgia's O.C.G.A. § 44-12-130 et seq. among them — that require every purchase transaction to be electronically reported to a law-enforcement database (commonly within 24 hours) and every purchased item held for a mandatory hold period, commonly 10-15 days, before it can be resold or, for scrap metal, melted down — an item resold or melted before its statutory hold period expires is exactly what a police property-recovery sweep is built to catch, and a pattern of late or missing law-enforcement reports is grounds for license suspension in most states. A small secondhand shop or scrap yard processing dozens of purchase transactions a week across multiple counter staff manages per-transaction hold-period tracking, per-transaction law-enforcement reporting-deadline currency, and business license renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a paper ledger.
**What to build:** A per-transaction hold-period countdown gate blocking an item from being marked resold or scheduled for melt before its statutory hold period expires; a law-enforcement reporting-deadline tracker flagging any transaction not yet submitted within its required window; and a business license renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** State secondhand-dealer and secondary-metals-recycler hold-period and reporting-deadline requirements are public, structured state regulatory data with a well-defined per-transaction record requirement, and the hold-period gate plus the reporting-deadline tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance window clears / compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a resale trade most small dealers currently track on a paper ledger with no systematic cross-check between a specific item's purchase date and its statutory hold-period expiration.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the hold-period gate, the reporting-deadline tracker, and the license renewal tracker; land the first 5-8 dealers through Institute of Scrap Recycling Industries (ISRI) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Institute of Scrap Recycling Industries (ISRI) member directory and state secondhand-dealer association meetings, where small dealers already juggle per-transaction hold periods and law-enforcement reporting deadlines with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per counter employee tracked. Average client (4 employees) = $249 + $60 = $309/mo. 30-36 dealers × $309/mo = **$9,270-11,124/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every purchase transaction opens its own hold-period clock and its own law-enforcement reporting deadline — a continuous, per-transaction compliance stream tied directly to the dealer's purchase volume.
**Biggest risk:** An item resold or melted before its statutory hold period expires, or a transaction reported past its law-enforcement filing deadline, is exactly the finding a police property-recovery sweep or a licensing audit is built to catch — the hold-period gate and the reporting-deadline tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → hold-period gate, reporting tracker, and license tracker deployed, first 5-8 dealer retainers through ISRI outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 17-21 dealers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 30-36 dealers, pushing past $9.27-11.12K/mo.

### 5. Professional Employer Organization (PEO) & Staffing Agency State Registration, Workers' Comp & CPEO Compliance
**Problem:** Professional employer organizations and staffing agencies that co-employ workers on behalf of client businesses operate under state PEO registration — Florida's Employee Leasing Companies license under Fla. Stat. Chapter 468, Part XI, administered by DBPR, among them — and, for PEOs pursuing IRS Certified PEO (CPEO) status under IRC § 7705, an annual independent CPA financial-assurance audit plus a Form 8973 filing for every new client relationship, which the client needs on file to claim successor-employer treatment of the wage base for FICA and FUTA purposes; a Form 8973 filed late, or a CPEO financial-assurance bond that lapses below the required coverage, doesn't just create a PEO compliance gap — it can strip every one of that PEO's clients of the wage-base protection they were counting on, turning one filing miss into a liability event across the entire client roster simultaneously. Every co-employed worksite employee must also be covered under a current workers'-compensation policy, and a PEO unable to produce a valid certificate of coverage for a specific client during a state audit is exactly the finding that triggers a broader compliance review of every other client relationship. A small PEO or staffing agency running several dozen client companies manages per-client Form 8973 filing currency, per-client workers'-comp certificate tracking, and CPEO/state registration renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a shared spreadsheet.
**What to build:** A per-client Form 8973 filing tracker flagging any new client relationship without a filed form on record; a per-client workers'-comp certificate-of-coverage tracker with a "cleared to co-employ" gate; and a CPEO financial-assurance/state-registration renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** State PEO registration cycles and IRS CPEO's Form 8973 filing requirement are public, structured federal and state regulatory data with a well-defined per-client record requirement, and the filing tracker plus the co-employment-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a co-employment trade most small PEOs and staffing agencies currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a specific client's onboarding date and its Form 8973 filing status.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the Form 8973 tracker, the workers'-comp certificate tracker with the co-employment gate, and the CPEO/state registration tracker; land the first 5-8 PEOs through National Association of Professional Employer Organizations (NAPEO) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Association of Professional Employer Organizations (NAPEO) member directory and state PEO association meetings, where small PEOs and staffing agencies already juggle per-client Form 8973 filings and workers'-comp certificate tracking with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base + $15/mo per client company tracked. Average client (15 client companies) = $349 + $225 = $574/mo. 16-20 PEOs × $574/mo = **$9,184-11,480/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new client relationship opens its own Form 8973 filing event and its own workers'-comp certificate obligation, and the PEO's own CPEO/state registration renews on its own independent cycle — a continuous, per-client compliance stream tied directly to the PEO's client roster.
**Biggest risk:** A Form 8973 filed late, or a workers'-comp certificate that lapses for a specific client, is exactly the finding a state audit or an IRS CPEO compliance review is built to catch — and either can jeopardize the wage-base protection every other client is relying on — the filing tracker and the co-employment gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → Form 8973 tracker, workers'-comp tracker, and CPEO/state registration tracker deployed, first 5-8 PEO retainers through NAPEO outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 9-11 PEOs. Replaces-full-time-salary → 16-20 PEOs, pushing past $9.18-11.48K/mo.
