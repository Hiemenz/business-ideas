### 1. Vapor Intrusion Assessment & Sub-Slab Depressurization System (SSDS) O&M Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Environmental consulting firms and specialty contractors performing vapor intrusion investigations — determining whether volatile chemical vapors from contaminated soil or groundwater are migrating into a building's indoor air — operate under EPA's OSWER vapor intrusion technical guidance and the state-specific investigation protocols built on top of it (most states now require a vapor intrusion evaluation as a standard step in Phase II site assessments and brownfield redevelopment), using ASTM E2600-15's Standard Practice for Vapor Intrusion Assessment to structure the sub-slab soil-gas and indoor-air sampling itself, with results reviewed and sign-off provided by a state-licensed Professional Geologist or Professional Engineer. Where sampling confirms an unacceptable vapor pathway, the contractor installs a sub-slab depressurization system (SSDS) — a fan-driven system that pulls soil gas from beneath the slab and vents it above the roofline — and that installation is only the beginning of the compliance obligation: the state agency's approval typically requires an indefinite, recurring operation-and-maintenance program for as long as the building is occupied, including periodic fan-vacuum-gauge readings confirming the system is still pulling negative pressure, a functioning system-failure alarm check, and post-mitigation indoor-air resampling on a schedule the agency sets (commonly annual for the first several years, then less frequent once the system's performance is established). A system that goes unmonitored between the contractor's periodic visits, or a resampling event missed past its due date, is exactly the gap a state environmental agency's file review or a property-transaction environmental due-diligence request is built to catch — and for a commercial or multifamily property, an SSDS failure discovered after the fact can reopen a "closed" contamination case the property owner believed was behind them. A small environmental firm or specialty SSDS contractor managing dozens of active mitigation systems across a portfolio of client properties tracks per-system O&M visit scheduling, per-property resampling due dates set by different state agencies on different cycles, and PG/PE sign-off documentation with a mix of a field technician's paper log and a spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific system's monitoring is actually current before the file goes quiet for another year.

**What to build:** A per-property vapor intrusion investigation tracker recording sampling dates, ASTM E2600 methodology documentation, and PG/PE sign-off status; a per-SSDS operation-and-maintenance scheduler computing each system's next-due fan-vacuum check and alarm test from its install date and the state agency's specified interval; and a post-mitigation resampling tracker flagging each property's next-due indoor-air sampling event against the state-specific schedule, with a "compliance current" status the contractor can hand a property owner or their real estate attorney on request.

**Skill fit:** EPA's vapor intrusion guidance, ASTM E2600's sampling methodology, and state-specific SSDS O&M interval requirements are public, structured technical and regulatory sources with well-defined recurring-check cadences, and the per-system O&M scheduler plus the resampling-due tracker are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an environmental sub-discipline distinct from every Phase I screening or general remediation-milestone idea already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the investigation tracker, the SSDS O&M scheduler, and the resampling-due tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to environmental consulting firms and specialty SSDS installation contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Environmental Health Association's state environmental consultant networks and the AEHS (Association for Environmental Health and Sciences) Foundation's annual conference reach exactly the small environmental firms currently tracking staggered per-property O&M visits and resampling deadlines across a growing mitigation-system portfolio by hand.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-449/mo per firm depending on active SSDS portfolio size. 28-36 firms × $339/mo avg = $9,492-12,204/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every installed SSDS generates a recurring O&M and resampling obligation for the entire life of the building's occupancy — there is no natural end date the way a one-time remediation project has — and every new vapor intrusion investigation the firm takes on adds another property to the monitoring portfolio, a continuous, portfolio-driven compliance stream that only grows as the firm wins new investigation work.

**Biggest risk:** A firm running only a handful of active SSDS installations has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need a spreadsheet can still handle. Mitigate by qualifying leads for firms managing 15 or more active mitigation systems across multiple state jurisdictions with different resampling cadences, where the staggered monitoring calendar genuinely outgrows manual tracking.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 firms ($4.1K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 24 firms ($8.1K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 28-36 firms plus a property-owner-facing "compliance current" export firms can attach to a real estate transaction's environmental due-diligence package.

### 2. Custom Combine Harvesting & Mobile Grain-Harvest Crew FMCSA Harvest-Exemption & Multi-State Oversize-Load Permit Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Custom combine harvesting outfits — crews that own their own combines, headers, and grain carts and follow the wheat harvest north from Texas through Oklahoma, Kansas, Nebraska, and into the Dakotas across a compressed spring-to-fall season, harvesting other farmers' crops under contract — operate their equipment moves under FMCSA's agricultural "harvest" hours-of-service exemption (49 CFR §395.1(k)), which relaxes standard driving-time limits specifically for equipment traveling to, from, or between fields during planting and harvesting periods, but only if the operator can document that the exemption's specific seasonal-window and mileage conditions actually applied to a given move — a crew that can't produce that documentation during a roadside inspection has lost the exemption's protection retroactively for that trip. Because combine headers routinely exceed standard road-width limits, every state line the crew crosses as it chases the harvest north requires a fresh oversize/overweight load permit tied to that state's specific route, width and length limits, and approved travel window — a permit that expires the moment the crew's actual travel schedule slips behind a weather delay, and a header moved on an expired or unobtained permit is exactly the finding a state DOT roadside inspection targets, with the crew's entire harvest-season schedule at risk if equipment is held at a scale house waiting on paperwork during a narrow harvest window. A mid-size custom cutting operation running several combine-and-cart units across a multi-state harvest route manages HOS-exemption documentation, staggered per-state oversize-load permit applications and expiration dates, and each unit's actual travel-window compliance on a mix of a crew boss's paper log and a folder of state permit printouts in the truck, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific unit is actually cleared to cross the next state line before it rolls.

**What to build:** A per-move HOS harvest-exemption documentation log capturing the seasonal window and mileage conditions that justified the exemption for that specific trip; a per-state oversize/overweight load permit tracker computing each permit's approved travel window and flagging any unit scheduled to cross a state line without a current permit on file; and a route-and-schedule board sequencing the crew's harvest-season progression against each state's permit lead time so applications go in before a weather delay turns a planned crossing into an unpermitted one.

**Skill fit:** FMCSA's harvest HOS exemption conditions and state-by-state oversize-load permit requirements are public, structured federal and state regulatory data with concrete per-trip and per-permit deadlines, and the permit-eligibility gate plus the exemption-documentation log are the same deterministic "block the move until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a seasonal agricultural-logistics trade distinct from the farm labor contractor idea already in this repo, whose MSPA/H-2A worker-recruitment-and-housing framework never touches a combine crew's own equipment-movement permitting.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the HOS-exemption documentation log, the per-state permit tracker with the crossing-eligibility gate, and the route-and-schedule board. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to custom harvesting outfits.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the U.S. Custom Harvesters, Inc. member directory and annual convention reach exactly the small independent custom cutting crews currently juggling staggered state permit deadlines against a weather-driven harvest schedule with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-399/mo per outfit depending on unit count and multi-state route length. 32-42 outfits × $289/mo avg = $9,248-12,138/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every state-line crossing opens a fresh permit-application cycle regardless of how many times the crew has made the same route before, every harvest season resets the entire multi-state permitting calendar from scratch, and every additional combine-and-cart unit the outfit adds multiplies the number of permits and exemption records that must stay current simultaneously — a continuous, route-and-fleet-driven compliance stream tied directly to the outfit's ability to keep moving legally from field to field.

**Biggest risk:** A very small outfit running a single unit on a short, familiar route within one or two states has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for outfits running multiple units across four or more states in a single season, where the staggered permit-expiration calendar genuinely outgrows a folder of printouts in the truck.

**Growth path:** Side project → 14 outfits ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 28 outfits ($8.1K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 32-42 outfits plus a landowner-facing move-schedule export outfits can share with the farms waiting on them along the route.

### 3. Non-Petroleum Chemical Aboveground Storage Tank (AST) Inspection & Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Contractors who inspect aboveground storage tanks holding industrial and process chemicals — acids, caustics, and other non-petroleum chemicals used in manufacturing, water treatment, and industrial processing — operate under the Steel Tank Institute's SP001 Standard for Inspection of Aboveground Storage Tanks, which sets periodic external, internal, and integrity-testing intervals keyed to a tank's age, construction material, and prior inspection findings, layered with state Chemical Bulk Storage programs (such as New York's 6 NYCRR Parts 596-599, with comparable chemical-tank registration and inspection regimes in other states) that require each covered tank to be registered with the state, inspected on a state-defined cycle, and certified by a designated Qualified Individual — an entirely separate regulatory track from EPA's oil-only SPCC program, since Part 112's jurisdiction never reaches a tank that has never held petroleum. A facility operating a chemical AST past its SP001-recommended inspection interval, or one whose state chemical-tank registration has lapsed, is exactly the finding a state environmental agency inspection targets, and for facilities storing threshold quantities of specific hazardous chemicals, EPA's Risk Management Program (40 CFR Part 68) additionally requires a five-year accident-history record and a worst-case-release scenario analysis kept current — a recordkeeping obligation with its own review cycle layered on top of the tank-integrity inspection schedule. A small inspection contractor running SP001-certified inspectors across dozens of client facilities and hundreds of tanks manages per-tank inspection-interval computation by age and construction, per-facility state chemical-tank registration renewal, and RMP five-year-review currency for larger clients with a mix of a paper inspection-tag system and a spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific tank is actually due, current, or overdue before a crew is dispatched.

**What to build:** A per-tank SP001 inspection-interval tracker computing each tank's next-due external/internal inspection from its age, construction type, and last-inspection finding; a per-facility state Chemical Bulk Storage registration renewal tracker with a Qualified Individual certification-currency check; and an RMP five-year accident-history and worst-case-scenario review tracker for facilities storing threshold chemical quantities.

**Skill fit:** STI SP001's age-and-construction-based inspection-interval table and state chemical-tank registration cycles are public, structured standards-body and regulatory data with a well-defined per-tank record structure, and the inspection-due computation plus the registration-renewal tracker are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a non-petroleum chemical-storage niche explicitly distinct from the Bulk Fuel & Chemical Storage Facility EPA SPCC idea already in this repo, whose entire compliance core is Part 112's oil-only jurisdiction and never reaches a tank that has never stored petroleum.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the SP001 inspection-interval tracker, the state chemical-tank registration tracker, and the RMP review tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent AST inspection contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Steel Tank Institute's own certified-inspector directory and state environmental-consultant association meetings reach exactly the small independent inspection contractors currently computing SP001 due-dates and state registration renewals by hand across a growing multi-facility client base.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $229/mo base + $15/mo per tank tracked. Average client (10 tanks) = $229 + $150 = $379/mo. 26-34 contractors × $379/mo = $9,854-12,886/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every tank's SP001 inspection interval recurs on its own age-and-construction-driven clock indefinitely, every facility's state chemical-tank registration renews independently, and every new client facility or additional tank added to a contractor's book multiplies the number of interval clocks running simultaneously — a continuous, tank-count-driven compliance stream tied directly to the contractor's client roster.

**Biggest risk:** A contractor inspecting only a handful of tanks for one or two long-standing clients has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for contractors running inspection programs across ten or more client facilities with mixed tank ages and construction types, where the staggered inspection-interval calendar genuinely outgrows a paper tag system.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 contractors ($4.5K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 22 contractors ($8.3K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 26-34 contractors plus a facility-owner-facing compliance-status export contractors can hand a client ahead of a state inspection.

### 4. AZA-Accredited Zoo & Public Aquarium USDA APHIS License, AZA Accreditation & ESA/CITES Permit Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Zoos and public aquariums operating as fixed facilities hold a USDA APHIS Animal Welfare Act exhibitor license (commonly Class C) subject to unannounced facility inspection, and — the credential that drives most of the trade's day-to-day compliance calendar — Association of Zoos & Aquariums (AZA) accreditation, a five-year cycle requiring a comprehensive on-site inspection covering animal-care standards, veterinary-program documentation, staffing ratios, and physical-plant condition, with a mandatory interim annual report due in the years between full inspections; a facility whose AZA accreditation lapses immediately loses eligibility to participate in AZA's Species Survival Plan cooperative breeding-loan program, cutting the facility off from receiving or placing animals with any other accredited institution — a consequence severe enough that most facilities treat accreditation currency as an existential priority. Layered on top, any facility holding or breeding a species listed under the Endangered Species Act or CITES Appendix I/II must separately maintain U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service permits specific to that species, and every specimen transferred between institutions — a breeding loan, a rescue intake, a Species Survival Plan placement — must be logged against the sending and receiving facility's respective permits before the animal moves, since a transfer completed without current permits on file at both ends is exactly the finding a USFWS or APHIS inspection is built to catch. A small to mid-size zoo or aquarium running dozens of species across a facility with several curatorial departments manages APHIS license renewal, the AZA five-year accreditation cycle with its interim annual reports, and per-species ESA/CITES permit currency tied to a constantly shifting collection plan on a mix of a registrar's binder and a spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific animal is actually cleared to transfer before a truck leaves the loading dock.

**What to build:** A USDA APHIS exhibitor license renewal tracker; an AZA accreditation five-year-cycle tracker with interim annual-report deadlines and a facility-readiness checklist ahead of the on-site inspection; and a per-species ESA/CITES permit tracker with a transfer-eligibility gate blocking any specimen movement from being logged as authorized until both the sending and receiving facility's permits are confirmed current.

**Skill fit:** AZA's five-year accreditation cycle, APHIS's exhibitor license renewal, and USFWS's per-species ESA/CITES permit structure are public, structured accrediting-body and federal regulatory data, and the transfer-eligibility gate plus the accreditation-readiness checklist are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fixed-facility animal-collection niche explicitly distinct from the USDA APHIS Class C Mobile & Traveling Animal Exhibitor idea already in this repo, whose entire compliance core is an interstate travel itinerary and per-state CVI clock that a stationary zoo or aquarium's animals never trigger.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the APHIS license tracker, the AZA accreditation-cycle tracker with the readiness checklist, and the per-species permit transfer-eligibility gate. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small and mid-size independent zoos and aquariums.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Association of Zoos & Aquariums (AZA) itself runs an active member directory and annual conference, alongside the Zoological Association of America's smaller-facility network, reaching exactly the small independent zoos and aquariums currently tracking a five-year accreditation cycle and per-species transfer permits on a registrar's binder.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per facility depending on species count and accreditation status. 24-30 facilities × $429/mo avg = $10,296-12,870/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** The AZA accreditation cycle recurs every five years without exception, every specimen transfer opens a fresh per-species permit check regardless of how routine the loan, and every new species the facility adds to its collection plan opens its own ESA/CITES permit obligation — a continuous, collection-and-accreditation-driven compliance stream tied directly to the facility's standing in the accredited-institution network its breeding programs depend on.

**Biggest risk:** A very small municipal petting-zoo-scale exhibitor with no AZA accreditation ambitions and few regulated species has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for AZA-accredited or accreditation-seeking facilities running a collection that includes ESA/CITES-listed species, where the accreditation-cycle-plus-transfer-permit calendar genuinely outgrows a registrar's binder.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 facilities ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 18 facilities ($7.7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 24-30 facilities plus an AZA-accreditation-readiness export facilities can hand their site-visit team ahead of the five-year inspection.

### 5. Leather Tanning & Finishing Facility EPA Effluent Guidelines & OSHA Hexavalent Chromium Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Leather tanneries and finishing facilities that convert raw or pickled hides into finished leather using chrome or vegetable tanning processes operate under EPA's Effluent Limitations Guidelines for the Leather Tanning and Finishing Point Source Category (40 CFR Part 425), which sets numeric discharge limits for chromium, sulfide, ammonia, and other tanning-process pollutants in the facility's wastewater, requiring a periodic self-monitoring sampling and discharge-reporting cycle filed with the local publicly owned treatment works or under the facility's NPDES permit — a facility whose discharge sampling shows an exceedance, or that misses a required reporting deadline, is exactly the finding a POTW pretreatment inspection or an EPA audit is built to catch, with penalties and potential discharge-permit suspension that can halt production entirely. Chrome-tanning facilities specifically must separately comply with OSHA's hexavalent chromium standard (29 CFR 1910.1026), requiring periodic airborne-exposure monitoring for any worker handling chrome tanning liquors or dry chrome-tanned hides, a written exposure-control plan, and mandatory medical surveillance (including specific respiratory and skin examinations) for any employee whose exposure meets the standard's action level — a chrome-specific worker-health surveillance obligation vegetable-tanning-only operations never trigger. The chrome-laden sludge generated by the tanning drums must also be evaluated under RCRA hazardous-waste-determination rules, and if it tests as characteristic hazardous waste, manifested to a permitted disposal facility with the manifest reconciled against the facility's own generator records. A small independent tannery running several tanning drums and a finishing line manages Part 425 discharge-sampling schedules, hexavalent-chromium exposure-monitoring and medical-surveillance currency for its chrome-tanning crew, and RCRA waste-determination/manifest reconciliation for its sludge stream on a mix of a plant supervisor's clipboard and a filing cabinet, with no systematic cross-check confirming the current batch's discharge sample and the crew's exposure-monitoring status are both actually up to date before production continues.

**What to build:** A Part 425 discharge-sampling and reporting-deadline tracker computing each required sampling event and flagging any submission approaching its due date; a per-employee hexavalent chromium exposure-monitoring and medical-surveillance tracker with an action-level alert triggering the written exposure-control-plan review; and an RCRA waste-determination and manifest tracker reconciling each shipment of tanning sludge against the facility's own generator records.

**Skill fit:** 40 CFR Part 425's numeric discharge limits and OSHA's hexavalent chromium action-level and medical-surveillance triggers are public, structured federal regulatory data with well-defined sampling and monitoring cadences, and the discharge-reporting tracker plus the exposure-surveillance tracker are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a leather-manufacturing niche whose numeric-discharge-limit-plus-chrome-exposure-surveillance structure shares no statute or certifying body with any textile, apparel, or general industrial-hygiene idea already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the Part 425 discharge tracker, the hexavalent chromium exposure/medical-surveillance tracker, and the RCRA waste-manifest reconciliation tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent tanneries and finishing facilities.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Leather and Hide Council of America's member directory and the annual International Tanners' Forum reach exactly the small independent tanneries currently reconciling discharge sampling and chrome-exposure monitoring against production schedules with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-549/mo per facility depending on tanning-drum count and chrome-crew headcount. 22-28 facilities × $409/mo avg = $9,000-11,452/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every production run generates a fresh Part 425 discharge-sampling obligation, hexavalent chromium exposure monitoring recurs on its own cycle for the life of the chrome-tanning operation, and every new hire on the tanning floor adds a fresh medical-surveillance enrollment — a continuous, production-and-headcount-driven compliance stream tied directly to the tannery's ability to keep discharging and keep its crew certified fit for chrome exposure.

**Biggest risk:** A very small vegetable-tanning-only operation with no chrome process and minimal discharge volume has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for chrome-tanning facilities running multiple drums and a discharge permit subject to regular POTW sampling, where the dual discharge-and-exposure calendar genuinely outgrows a plant supervisor's clipboard.

## This Session's Pick: #3 (Non-Petroleum Chemical AST Inspection Contractor) for the cleanest wedge against a well-known but easily misread near-miss; #4 (AZA-Accredited Zoo & Public Aquarium) for the highest-stakes accreditation consequence

Non-petroleum chemical AST inspection stands out because it sits directly next to one of this repo's own prior ideas (the SPCC-based bulk fuel/chemical storage idea in Session 97) in a way a prospective customer could easily conflate at a glance — and demonstrating the crisp jurisdictional line between "this tank held oil" and "this tank never held oil" is exactly the kind of regulatory precision that makes a compliance-tracking pitch land credibly with an inspection contractor who lives in that distinction every day.

AZA-accredited zoo and public aquarium compliance has the sharpest institutional-consequence pitch in this session: losing AZA accreditation doesn't just risk a fine, it cuts a facility off entirely from the Species Survival Plan cooperative breeding network every accredited zoo depends on to responsibly manage its collection — a consequence far more existential to the institution than a routine penalty, giving the sales conversation unusual urgency, and AZA's own concentrated member directory makes the first 5-8 clients a well-targeted outreach.
