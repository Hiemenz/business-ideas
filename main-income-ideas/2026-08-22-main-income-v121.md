# Main Income Ideas - 2026-08-22 (Session 121)

## Who this is for
You're 29, you write software and know your way around data — pipelines, scrapers, structured reference data, rules engines. You want to replace a full-time salary ($8-15K+/mo) within 12-24 months with something you build solo in evenings and weekends, no outside funding, no co-founder, and you want the first paying customer inside 90 days. The wedge in this series is always the same: small operators in a regulated industry who have non-optional government or accreditation-body obligations — licenses, permits, certifications, filings, inspections — and no compliance department to track them. You sell a monthly-retainer SaaS that turns their compliance binder into a living calendar with alerts and hard gates. The regulator (or accrediting body) creates the deadline; you sell the peace of mind of never missing it.

- Realistic path to $8-15K+/mo

## Ideas

### 1. Mold Assessment & Remediation Contractor Dual-License & Certificate-of-Remediation Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** States with dedicated mold-industry licensing — most notably Texas, where the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) administers the Mold Assessment and Remediation Rules — require four separate license types (Mold Assessment Consultant, Mold Assessment Technician, Mold Remediation Contractor, Mold Remediation Worker), each with its own continuing-education renewal cycle, minimum $1 million general liability and errors-and-omissions insurance requirement, and a strict rule that the same company (in most cases) cannot both assess and remediate the same property without a documented disclosure to the client. Florida's Statute Ch. 468 Part XVI runs a parallel structure through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation, and New York City's Local Law 55 imposes its own mold assessment/abatement licensing for larger jobs. After a remediation job completes, Texas law requires the contractor to file a Certificate of Mold Remediation (CMR) within 10 days documenting the clearance test results — a filing that real estate closings and property managers rely on, and that TDLR audits directly. A small remediation company running two to ten technicians across a mix of MAC/MAT/MRC/MRW licenses, each with its own renewal date and CE-hour requirement, currently tracks license status, insurance certificate expiration, and per-job CMR filing deadlines on a spreadsheet the office manager updates inconsistently, and a missed CMR filing or a lapsed technician license discovered during a TDLR complaint investigation can halt a pending real estate transaction and trigger license discipline against the firm.

**What to build:** A per-technician license tracker covering all four Texas license types (or the equivalent Florida/NYC categories) with CE-hour accumulation against each renewal cycle; an insurance certificate vault with expiration alerts tied to the state's minimum coverage requirements; a per-job Certificate of Mold Remediation deadline tracker computing the filing due date from job-completion date and flagging any job approaching its window unfiled; and a dual-role conflict-of-interest disclosure generator for jobs where the same firm both assessed and remediated a property.

**Skill fit:** TDLR's license lookup database and Florida DBPR license search are public, structured, queryable data sources; the CE-hour accumulation and filing-deadline math is a straightforward data-modeling problem for an engineer, and the CMR deadline computation is the same "compute due date from a trigger event and alert before it lapses" pattern that recurs across this whole series.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license/CE tracker, the insurance-certificate vault, and the CMR filing-deadline tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to TDLR-licensed remediation firms and IICRC S520-certified companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Indoor Air Quality Association (IAQA) and the National Association of Mold Remediators and Inspectors (NAMRI) both run member directories reaching small remediation firms directly, and the IICRC's certified-firm locator gives a second cold-outreach list of S520-trained companies already managing exactly this compliance burden by hand.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $149-249/mo per remediation company depending on technician-license count. 65-75 companies × $199/mo avg = $12,935-14,925/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every job triggers a CMR filing deadline, every technician's license and CE cycle renews on its own schedule, and insurance certificates lapse annually — a continuous, job-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the firm's ability to keep operating and to close real estate transactions its clients depend on.

**Biggest risk:** Small firms often believe an informal spreadsheet is "good enough" until a TDLR complaint or a stalled closing makes the cost of a missed CMR filing concrete. Mitigate by leading sales conversations with the real estate-closing-delay scenario, which resonates immediately with owners who've had a deal held up before.

**Growth path:** Side project → 20 firms in Texas ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 45 firms across TX/FL ($9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 65-75 firms plus a NYC Local Law 55 add-on for firms expanding into that market.

### 2. Crime Scene & Biohazard/Trauma Cleanup Company Bloodborne Pathogen & Medical Waste Transporter Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Crime scene, trauma, and biohazard cleanup companies operate under OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030), which requires a written exposure control plan, documented offers of hepatitis B vaccination to every employee with occupational exposure, annual bloodborne-pathogen training with individual certificates, and PPE-use recordkeeping — all subject to OSHA inspection. Separately, states like Texas (25 TAC §325) and California (under the Medical Waste Management Act) require a regulated medical waste transporter permit or registration for any company hauling biohazardous waste, renewed annually, plus DOT hazmat shipping-paper requirements under 49 CFR Part 173 for UN3291 regulated medical waste, and state-mandated waste manifest or chain-of-custody retention (often three years). A small cleanup company running a handful of technicians tracks each employee's annual BBP training-certificate expiration and hepatitis B vaccination-offer documentation, the company's state medical waste transporter registration renewal, and a manifest or chain-of-custody record for every biohazard load hauled — usually on paper training logs and a folder of manifests in a filing cabinet, and a technician working past their training-certificate expiration or a missing manifest discovered during a state health department audit is exactly the finding that can suspend the company's transporter registration and expose it to OSHA citation.

**What to build:** An employee bloodborne-pathogen training-certificate tracker with annual renewal alerts and a hepatitis B vaccination-offer documentation log; a state medical waste transporter permit/registration renewal calendar; a per-job biohazard waste manifest and chain-of-custody log with the state's required retention period built in; and a PPE-inventory and exposure-control-plan review-date tracker.

**Skill fit:** State regulated medical waste program registries and DOT hazmat shipping-paper requirements are public, structured regulatory data, and per-job manifest logging with retention-period enforcement is a straightforward records-management data problem well suited to an engineer.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the training-certificate tracker, the transporter-permit renewal calendar, and the manifest/chain-of-custody log. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent trauma/biohazard cleanup companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Bio-Recovery Association (ABRA) maintains a member directory of independent crime scene and biohazard cleanup companies nationally, reaching exactly the small operators managing this training and permitting burden without dedicated compliance staff.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $179-299/mo per company depending on technician headcount and multi-state operations. 55-70 companies × $229/mo avg = $12,595-16,030/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Annual BBP training renewals recur for every employee, the state transporter permit renews yearly, and every job generates a fresh manifest requiring multi-year retention — a continuous, headcount-and-job-volume-driven compliance stream tied to the company's legal authority to keep hauling biohazard waste.

**Biggest risk:** Companies often treat a paper log as sufficient until a state audit or an OSHA complaint investigation exposes gaps. Mitigate by positioning the product explicitly as "audit-ready in one click" and leading with the transporter-registration-suspension scenario, which owners recognize as an existential threat to the business.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 companies ($3.4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 35 companies ($8K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 55-70 companies plus a multi-state transporter-permit tracking tier for companies expanding their service radius.

### 3. Fire Alarm & Sprinkler System Inspection/Testing Contractor NFPA 25/72 Interval & AHJ Filing Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Fire alarm and sprinkler system contractors that inspect, test, and maintain systems at commercial buildings operate under NFPA 25 (water-based fire protection systems) and NFPA 72 (fire alarm systems), which set concrete inspection-testing-maintenance intervals per system component — monthly visual checks, quarterly tests of certain devices, annual full-system tests, and a five-year internal pipe inspection for sprinkler systems — layered with state fire-alarm/sprinkler contractor licensing (administered in Texas by the State Fire Marshal's Office) and individual technician NICET certification levels that must stay current. After each inspection, the local Authority Having Jurisdiction (the municipal fire marshal) typically requires the report filed within a set window, often through a third-party portal, and any deficiency tagged during inspection needs documented follow-up remediation before the building is considered compliant. A small contracting firm servicing 100-300 buildings across a metro area tracks each building's next-due monthly/quarterly/annual/five-year interval per system component, each technician's NICET certification and state license renewal, AHJ filing deadlines, and unresolved deficiency tags — usually across a scheduling spreadsheet and a stack of paper inspection reports, and a building whose annual test lapses past its interval, or a deficiency tag that never gets a documented follow-up, is exactly the gap a fire marshal's compliance review or a post-incident investigation is built to catch.

**What to build:** An interval scheduler computing each building's next-due date per system component from the NFPA 25/72 tables and generating a rolling inspection calendar; a technician NICET-certification and state-license renewal tracker; an AHJ report-filing deadline tracker per building; and a deficiency-tag follow-up tracker flagging any unresolved code violation past its remediation window.

**Skill fit:** NFPA's published interval tables and state fire-alarm/sprinkler contractor licensing rules are structured, public reference data, and scheduling recurring inspection events across hundreds of buildings with multiple component types is a data-modeling problem an engineer is well positioned to solve.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the interval scheduler, the technician-credential tracker, and the AHJ filing-deadline tracker. First 5-8 contractor clients through direct outreach, positioned as an internal license/deadline operations tool rather than a client-facing inspection-report platform.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Fire Sprinkler Association (NFSA) and the Automatic Fire Alarm Association (AFAA) both run regional chapters and member directories reaching small independent inspection/testing contractors directly.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-349/mo per contractor firm depending on buildings-under-contract and technician count. 45-60 firms × $269/mo avg = $12,105-16,140/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every serviced building runs its own recurring monthly/quarterly/annual/five-year inspection clock indefinitely, and every technician's NICET certification and state license renews independently — a permanent, building-portfolio-driven compliance stream that grows as the contractor wins new buildings.

**Biggest risk:** Contractors may already use client-facing inspection-report software like BuildingReports for the reports themselves, so the product must be clearly positioned as internal operations management (license/deadline tracking, deficiency follow-up) rather than competing head-on for the inspection-report workflow. Mitigate by explicitly scoping the product to the contractor's own credential and filing-deadline management, not report generation.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 firms ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 35 firms ($9.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 firms plus a combined alarm-and-sprinkler multi-license tier for firms holding both contractor licenses.

### 4. Non-Emergency Medical Transportation (NEMT) Broker/Provider Monthly Exclusion-Screening & Fleet Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Non-emergency medical transportation companies that transport Medicaid beneficiaries to appointments — either as a state Medicaid managed-care organization's contracted broker or as a direct provider — must screen every driver and employee monthly against the OIG's List of Excluded Individuals/Entities (LEIE) and each state's own Medicaid exclusion list under 42 CFR 455.436, since transporting a beneficiary or billing Medicaid while employing an excluded individual exposes the company to recoupment and False Claims Act exposure. Drivers separately need current state DOT-required background checks, CPR/first aid certification renewal, and vehicles need annual state inspection plus wheelchair-lift certification where applicable, with insurance minimums set by the state Medicaid contract. A small NEMT company running a fleet of 10-40 vehicles and drivers tracks each driver's monthly exclusion-screening status, background-check and CPR-cert renewal dates, and each vehicle's inspection and lift-certification renewal — usually on a spreadsheet the dispatcher updates between rides, and a driver who slips past a monthly exclusion re-check (a step easy to forget amid daily dispatch pressure) is exactly the gap a Medicaid program-integrity audit is built to catch, with recoupment demands that can reach back over months of billed trips.

**What to build:** A driver roster with license, CPR/first-aid, and background-check expiration tracking; an automated monthly cross-check log against the publicly downloadable OIG LEIE dataset and the relevant state Medicaid exclusion list, flagging any driver who newly appears on either list; a vehicle inspection and wheelchair-lift certification renewal tracker; and an insurance-certificate vault matched to the state Medicaid contract's minimum coverage.

**Skill fit:** The OIG LEIE is a public, downloadable dataset updated monthly, and building an automated match against a company's driver roster is squarely a data-engineering problem — turning what's currently a manual monthly lookup into a one-click reconciliation is the single most differentiating feature in this idea.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the driver roster tracker and the automated monthly LEIE/state-exclusion-list matching log, plus the vehicle inspection tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to state-contracted NEMT brokers and providers.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state Medicaid managed-care organizations publish lists of their contracted NEMT broker networks, giving a direct cold-outreach list, supplemented by regional non-emergency transportation trade associations that many state NEMT provider groups participate in.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-399/mo per NEMT company (priced per fleet, covering all drivers and vehicles). 40-50 companies × $319/mo avg = $12,760-15,950/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** The monthly LEIE/exclusion-list screening obligation recurs every single month for the life of the company's Medicaid contract, and vehicle/driver credentials renew on their own independent cycles — a continuous, fleet-and-driver-driven compliance stream with direct financial stakes tied to Medicaid billing integrity.

**Biggest risk:** Companies often underestimate exclusion-screening risk until a recoupment letter arrives, since the monthly check feels like a low-priority administrative task amid daily dispatch operations. Mitigate by leading sales conversations with the automated LEIE-matching feature as the flagship time-saver that also closes the company's single biggest audit-exposure gap.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 NEMT companies ($3.8K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 28 companies ($8.9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 40-50 companies plus a multi-state exclusion-list expansion tier for brokers operating across state lines.

### 5. Underground Storage Tank (UST) / Petroleum Equipment Contractor Testing-Interval & Operator-Training Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Contractors who install, remove, and service underground storage tanks at gas stations and fleet-fueling facilities operate under EPA's UST technical standards (40 CFR Part 280) as implemented through state UST programs, which require state-certified installer/remover/inspector credentials (often via ICC or Petroleum Equipment Institute-affiliated certification), and set concrete recurring testing intervals for the tank owner's equipment: annual testing of release-detection equipment, testing of cathodic protection systems within six months of installation and every three years thereafter, and testing of spill and overfill prevention equipment every three years. Tank owners must also ensure their facility's Class A, B, and C UST operators complete state-mandated training, typically with an annual refresher, and file compliance inspection reports with the state environmental agency on a set schedule. A small UST service contractor managing testing schedules across 50-150 client tank systems tracks each tank's release-detection, cathodic-protection, and spill/overfill testing due dates on their own independent 1-year/3-year cycles, each technician's installer/inspector certification renewal, and each client facility's operator-training currency — usually across a spreadsheet the office manager maintains per client, and a tank whose cathodic-protection test lapses past its three-year window, discovered during a state UST program inspection, is exactly the finding that can trigger state fines against the tank owner and reputational damage to the contractor who missed it.

**What to build:** A per-tank testing-interval tracker computing each tank's next-due release-detection (annual), cathodic-protection (3-year), and spill/overfill-prevention (3-year) test dates and flagging any approaching or past due; a certified installer/inspector credential renewal tracker for the contractor's own technicians; a Class A/B/C operator-training currency tracker per client facility; and a state filing-deadline tracker for compliance inspection reports.

**Skill fit:** EPA's UST technical standards and state UST program testing-interval rules are published, structured federal and state regulatory data with concrete numeric thresholds, and computing three independent testing clocks (annual, 3-year, 3-year) across a portfolio of client tank systems is the same interval-scheduling pattern that recurs across this series, applied to a petroleum-storage category distinct from any prior fuel or oil-handling idea.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-tank testing-interval tracker, the technician credential tracker, and the operator-training currency tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to state-certified UST service contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Petroleum Equipment Institute (PEI) runs a member directory and regional chapters reaching small independent UST service contractors directly, supplemented by state-published lists of UST-certified installers and inspectors.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-349/mo per contractor, priced per tank-system portfolio managed on behalf of client tank owners. 45-60 contractors × $269/mo avg = $12,105-16,140/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every managed tank runs three independent recurring testing clocks (annual, 3-year, 3-year) for the life of the tank system, and every technician's certification renews on its own cycle — a permanent, tank-portfolio-driven compliance stream that scales directly with how many client facilities the contractor services.

**Biggest risk:** Contractors already track this manually per client and may not immediately see the value until a missed test triggers a client's state fine. Mitigate by positioning the product on preventing exactly that scenario — a fined tank owner blaming their service contractor — which is a direct threat to the contractor's own client relationships and reputation.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 contractors ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 32 contractors ($8.6K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 contractors plus a multi-state expansion tier as contractors take on client facilities across state UST program boundaries.

## This Session's Pick: #4 (NEMT Broker/Provider) for the clearest single-feature wedge with direct financial stakes; #2 (Crime Scene & Biohazard Cleanup) for the most differentiated, least-crowded niche

The NEMT idea stands out because the automated monthly OIG LEIE/state-exclusion-list matching feature solves a specific, recurring, easy-to-forget task with a hard dollar consequence attached (Medicaid recoupment) — it's the kind of single-feature wedge that sells itself in a five-minute demo, and state Medicaid managed-care organizations publish their contracted broker networks, making the initial prospect list easy to build without relying on a single association's member directory.

Crime scene and biohazard cleanup is the most differentiated pick because almost no compliance-SaaS competitor targets this narrow niche, ABRA's member directory delivers a small but highly organized and reachable buyer community, and the training-certificate-plus-manifest combination gives the product two independent, non-optional recurring events (annual BBP training, per-job manifests) that keep usage constant regardless of how the company's job volume fluctuates seasonally.
