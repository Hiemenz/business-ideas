  - **Class V UIC well owners/operators** register non-oil-and-gas injection wells (car wash and dry-cleaner drywells, stormwater drainage wells, aquifer storage and recovery wells, large-capacity septic systems serving non-residential facilities) with EPA or a state primacy agency under 40 CFR Parts 144-148. This is a completely different regulatory track from the **Oil & Gas Well Operator** idea already in this repo (Session 137), whose compliance core is a state oil and gas commission's Permit-to-Drill, statewide plugging bond, and monthly production/disposition reporting for Class II hydrocarbon wells — a Class II well's whole purpose is producing or disposing of oilfield fluid under a production-tax-driven filing cadence, while a Class V well's owner is typically a car wash, dry cleaner, or municipal stormwater program with no production reporting at all, just mechanical-integrity and closure documentation under an entirely different EPA program office.
  - **Lead service line inventory & replacement contractors** perform drinking-water infrastructure work under EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (LCRI), finalized in 2024 with a 2027 inventory-and-initial-replacement-plan deadline. This has no overlap with the **Asbestos & Lead-Based Paint Abatement Contractor** idea already in this repo (Session 45), whose compliance core is EPA's RRP Rule (40 CFR Part 745, Subpart E) governing lead-paint dust hazards during renovation of pre-1978 housing — an entirely different EPA office (Office of Water vs. Office of Chemical Safety), an entirely different hazard pathway (drinking-water contamination from lead pipe versus lead-paint dust exposure), and an entirely different customer (a utility-side pipe-replacement contractor versus a renovation/painting contractor).
  - **340B covered entities and contract pharmacies** are HRSA-administered drug-discount-program participants — the compliance core is HRSA Office of Pharmacy Affairs Information System (OPAIS) registration and recertification, GPO-prohibition and duplicate-discount audit defense, and contract-pharmacy arrangement documentation. No pharmacy-adjacent idea already in this repo (compounding pharmacy USP <795>/<797>/<800>, long-term-care pharmacy, or pharmacy benefit manager ideas) touches the 340B discount program at all — 340B is a distinct HRSA grant-compliance and drug-pricing-integrity regime layered on top of a covered entity's ordinary pharmacy operations, not a state board of pharmacy licensing or sterile-compounding framework.
  - **Earned wage access (EWA) providers** are a fintech category regulated under a fast-growing patchwork of state-specific statutes — Nevada's NRS Chapter 604F, Missouri's EWA Services Act, and Wisconsin's DFI registration requirement among the first movers — plus the CFPB's 2024 interpretive rule treating certain fee-based EWA products as credit subject to Truth in Lending Act/Regulation Z disclosure. This is a brand-new financial-services category for this repo, distinct from every other fintech/lending idea already covered (money services businesses, merchant cash advance brokers, payment facilitators/ISOs) because none of those involve a per-paycheck wage-advance product or the EWA-specific state licensing regime this niche is accumulating state by state.
  - **Towing vessel (tugboat) operators** are governed by the U.S. Coast Guard's Subchapter M (46 CFR Subchapter M), which requires every towing vessel company to operate under a Towing Safety Management System (TSMS) and hold a Certificate of Inspection issued either directly by an OCMI or through an approved Third-Party Organization survey, with vessel masters and mates credentialed separately under 46 CFR Part 15. This is a distinct maritime subchapter with no overlap to the **Marina Operator** idea already in this repo (Session 137), whose compliance core is an Army Corps Section 10 permit, a submerged-lands lease, and Clean Vessel Act pumpout-station compliance for a waterfront facility that never operates a vessel at all — a marina is real property regulated by a leasehold and permitting framework, while a towing vessel company is a fleet of working vessels regulated by USCG vessel-inspection and crew-credentialing rules a marina never touches.

### 1. Class V UIC Well Owner/Operator EPA/State Primacy Registration, Mechanical Integrity & Closure Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Facilities that inject fluid underground through a well without producing oil or gas — car washes and dry cleaners using drywells for wastewater disposal, municipalities and private developers using stormwater drainage wells, and facilities running aquifer storage and recovery (ASR) wells or large-capacity cesspools/septic systems serving non-residential sites — must register those wells as Class V injection wells under EPA's Underground Injection Control (UIC) Program (40 CFR Parts 144-148), either directly with EPA Region in non-primacy states or with a state primacy agency (most states hold UIC primacy for Class V wells). Registration requires submitting well-inventory information (well type code, injectate characterization, construction details) via EPA Form 7520-6 or the state equivalent, and many states require periodic mechanical integrity testing (MIT) confirming the well isn't leaking into a protected aquifer, plus a formal closure/plugging report when a well is taken out of service. A facility that never registers a drywell, or that lets an MIT lapse, is operating what the EPA calls an "unauthorized" Class V well — a finding that in several states has triggered five-figure penalties even where no actual contamination occurred, simply because the paperwork gate itself is the violation. A regional car wash chain or dry-cleaning franchise operator with wells at 10-40 sites currently tracks this, if at all, on a facilities-manager spreadsheet with no systematic link between a specific well's registration status, its MIT due date, and the site's own operating permit.

**What to build:** A per-well registration and inventory tracker (well type code, injectate characterization, EPA Form 7520-6/state-form status) across a multi-site portfolio; an MIT scheduling and results log with alerts ahead of the state's required interval; a closure/plugging documentation workflow for decommissioned wells; and a portfolio dashboard rolling every site's well status up for a facilities or environmental compliance manager overseeing dozens of locations at once.

**Skill fit:** Matching each facility's well inventory against its state's public UIC well database (most primacy states publish a searchable UIC well registry, which doubles as a lead-generation data source) and generating the state-specific registration/MIT-due calendar is a data-engineering and public-records problem well within reach of a solo developer with no environmental-consulting credential required.

**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: single-state (start with a large primacy state like Texas or California) well registration tracker, MIT scheduling, and Form 7520-6 status log. First 5-8 clients from the International Car Wash Association (ICA) member directory — car wash drywells are one of the most common Class V well types nationally — cross-referenced against the state's public UIC well registry to confirm which chains actually have registered wells.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — direct outreach to multi-site car wash and dry-cleaning operators identified in the ICA directory and the state UIC well registry, offering a free portfolio audit of their current well registration status as the hook.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $600-1,200/mo per multi-site operator managing a portfolio of wells, since the value scales with site count rather than a single-well flat fee. At 15 multi-site chain clients × $800/mo average = $12,000/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** MIT testing intervals and registration renewal are recurring, non-optional obligations tied to a state primacy agency's own audit cycle, and a growing chain adds new wells (and new registration/MIT obligations) with every new site it opens — usage grows with the client's own expansion rather than staying flat.

**Biggest risk:** Many facility operators genuinely don't know their drywell is a regulated Class V well at all, so the sales motion has to start with education, not a feature pitch; mitigate by leading with the free registry cross-check that shows a prospect exactly which of their sites has (or is missing) a registered well.

**Growth path:** Side project auditing 8-10 car wash/dry-cleaning chains → ICA conference presence → 15+ multi-site clients replaces-full-time-salary.

### 2. Lead Service Line Inventory & Replacement Contractor EPA LCRI/State Drinking-Water Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (LCRI), finalized in October 2024, requires nearly every community water system to complete a full lead service line inventory and, for systems with lead or unknown-material lines, a replacement plan and initial replacement work by October 2027 (with a narrow subset of systems eligible for extension to 2037). Small and mid-size municipal utilities overwhelmingly can't staff this in-house and hire specialized service-line inventory and replacement contractors, who must classify each service line's material (lead, galvanized-requiring-replacement, non-lead, or unknown) from a patchwork of historical tap cards, meter records, and predictive statistical models; submit the utility's inventory to the state primacy drinking-water agency by the deadline; issue the LCRI-mandated notification to an affected customer within 24 hours whenever a lead or galvanized-requiring-replacement line is found or disturbed; and confirm every replacement part used is certified lead-free under NSF/ANSI Standards 61 and 372 as required by the Safe Drinking Water Act §1417. A contractor juggling inventory and replacement work for 10-20 small municipal utility clients simultaneously currently tracks material classification, state submission deadlines, and 24-hour notification compliance on spreadsheets per client, with a missed notification window or a late inventory submission landing squarely on the contractor's own liability, not just the utility's.

**What to build:** A per-utility-client inventory tracker classifying every service line by material with a confidence source (tap card, meter record, predictive model, or field-verified) and a rollup against the utility's state-submission deadline; a 24-hour notification workflow triggered the moment a lead or galvanized-requiring-replacement line is found or disturbed, generating the required customer notice automatically; and an NSF/ANSI 61/372 lead-free material certification log tied to every replacement part installed, so a state audit reconciles instantly against actual materials used.

**Skill fit:** Reconciling scattered historical utility records into a defensible material classification, and computing state-specific submission deadlines from each utility's own inventory-completion status, is a data-engineering and public-records problem — a strong fit for a developer comfortable normalizing messy tabular data, with no plumbing or engineering credential required to build the tracker itself.

**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: inventory classification tracker, 24-hour notification generator, and NSF/ANSI material certification log. First 5-8 clients from the American Water Works Association (AWWA) contractor member directory and state Rural Water Association chapters, since rural and small-system water utilities — the segment hit hardest by the 2027 deadline with the least in-house capacity — increasingly outsource this work to small specialized contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — direct outreach to AWWA-listed inventory/replacement contractors and state Rural Water Association member contractors ahead of the 2027 LCRI deadline.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $350-700/mo per utility client managed, since a contractor typically juggles several municipal contracts at once. At 25 utility-client accounts across 6-8 contractor firms × $500/mo average = $12,500/mo achievable from a modest handful of contractor clients each managing multiple utilities.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** The 2027 LCRI deadline creates a hard, dated compliance event every contractor in this space is racing against right now, and the per-line-found 24-hour notification requirement recurs on every single service line touched for the life of the replacement program — this isn't a one-time inventory snapshot, it's a running obligation through the entire multi-year replacement effort.

**Biggest risk:** Large national engineering firms (Black & Veatch, HDR) already serve big-city utilities with enterprise GIS-based tools; differentiate by targeting the long tail of small/rural utilities and their small specialized contractors who can't afford enterprise engineering-firm pricing.

**Growth path:** Side project serving 3-4 small contractors → AWWA/Rural Water Association channel presence → 6-8 contractor clients each managing multiple utilities replaces-full-time-salary.

### 3. 340B Covered Entity & Contract Pharmacy HRSA Program-Integrity Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Hospitals, Federally Qualified Health Centers, Ryan White grantees, and other 340B-eligible covered entities — along with the independent contract pharmacies many of them partner with to dispense 340B-priced drugs — must register and annually recertify with HRSA's Office of Pharmacy Affairs Information System (OPAIS), maintain documentation proving no duplicate discount was claimed on the same prescription under both 340B pricing and a Medicaid rebate, and prove the entity never purchased a 340B drug through a Group Purchasing Organization in violation of the GPO prohibition that applies to most hospital covered-entity types. Every contract pharmacy arrangement must be separately registered in OPAIS and supported by a written agreement documenting the entity's oversight of the arrangement, and HRSA's own program-integrity audits — along with increasingly aggressive manufacturer restrictions on contract-pharmacy shipments — target exactly these gaps: an expired OPAIS recertification, an undocumented contract-pharmacy relationship, or a duplicate-discount claim that wasn't caught before a manufacturer or HRSA auditor found it. A small rural hospital, critical access hospital, or FQHC with one or two contract-pharmacy relationships and no dedicated 340B compliance officer currently manages OPAIS recertification dates, contract-pharmacy documentation, and self-audit scheduling on a pharmacy director's spreadsheet, with real financial exposure — repayment of improperly claimed discounts plus potential program termination — if a gap surfaces during a HRSA audit rather than before one.

**What to build:** An OPAIS registration and annual-recertification deadline tracker per covered-entity site; a contract-pharmacy arrangement registry linking each pharmacy relationship to its required written agreement and oversight documentation; a duplicate-discount self-audit workflow that ingests 340B claims data and Medicaid billing data to flag any prescription claimed under both, ahead of a HRSA audit finding it first; and a GPO-prohibition compliance log for entity types subject to it.

**Skill fit:** Cross-referencing 340B claims data against Medicaid billing data to catch duplicate-discount patterns is a data-reconciliation problem directly suited to an analytics background, layered on top of a standard OPAIS deadline-tracking calendar.

**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: OPAIS recertification tracker, contract-pharmacy registry, and a CSV-based duplicate-discount reconciliation report. First 5-8 clients through the National Association of Community Health Centers (NACHC) member directory and state Rural Health Association chapters, targeting small FQHCs and critical access hospitals with one or two contract-pharmacy relationships and no in-house 340B compliance staff.

**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — direct outreach to NACHC-member FQHC pharmacy directors, offering a free duplicate-discount self-audit of a recent claims sample as the opening hook.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $400-700/mo per covered-entity site. At 25 small covered-entity clients × $500/mo average = $12,500/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** OPAIS recertification is an annual, non-optional filing tied directly to continued 340B program eligibility, and every new prescription claimed under the program is a fresh duplicate-discount risk — this is continuous transactional exposure, not a one-time compliance checklist, for as long as the entity keeps participating in the program.

**Biggest risk:** Established enterprise vendors (Sentry Data Systems, Verity Solutions, Kalderos) already dominate 340B compliance software for large hospital systems; differentiate explicitly by targeting the small FQHC/critical-access-hospital segment those enterprise platforms price out, where a pharmacy director is still doing this by hand.

**Growth path:** Side project auditing 5 small FQHCs → NACHC/state Rural Health Association channel presence → 20-25 covered-entity clients replaces-full-time-salary.

### 4. Earned Wage Access (EWA) Provider State Licensing & CFPB Disclosure Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Small and regional earned wage access providers — fintechs offering employees early access to already-earned wages, often built into a payroll platform or targeted at a specific industry vertical like healthcare staffing or restaurants — face a rapidly accumulating patchwork of state-specific EWA statutes: Nevada's NRS Chapter 604F, Missouri's Earned Wage Access Services Act, and Wisconsin's Department of Financial Institutions registration requirement are among the first-mover states, with several more actively considering similar legislation, each carrying its own registration or licensing renewal, surety bond, and fee-disclosure requirement. Layered on top, the CFPB's 2024 interpretive rule treats certain fee-based EWA products as "credit" subject to Truth in Lending Act/Regulation Z disclosure obligations, meaning a provider's fee structure and disclosure language have to be reviewed against a federal standard that didn't clearly apply to this product category until recently. A small EWA provider expanding state by state currently tracks registration status, bond currency, and disclosure-language compliance across a growing patchwork of state requirements on outside counsel's advice and an internal spreadsheet, with no systematic gate stopping the product from going live in a new state before that state's registration is actually confirmed.

**What to build:** A state-by-state EWA registration/licensing tracker (Nevada, Missouri, Wisconsin, and each newly enacted state as it comes online) with renewal deadlines and surety bond currency; a fee-disclosure template library mapped to each state's specific disclosure-language requirement plus the CFPB's TILA/Reg Z-aligned disclosure standard; and a state-launch readiness gate that blocks marketing an EWA product in a new state until registration is confirmed complete.

**Skill fit:** Tracking a fast-moving, state-by-state regulatory patchwork and generating jurisdiction-specific disclosure templates is a structured-data and document-generation problem — well suited to a developer comfortable maintaining a rules engine against a regulatory landscape that changes every legislative session.

**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: state registration tracker for the handful of states with EWA-specific statutes today, a disclosure-template library, and a state-launch gate. First 5-8 clients from the American Fintech Council and the Innovative Payments Association's EWA working group member list, plus direct outreach to smaller EWA vendors identified in the public state licensing databases the first-mover states already publish.

**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — this is a small, fast-growing niche market (a few dozen genuinely independent small/regional EWA providers, as opposed to the handful of national giants), so expect a relationship-driven sales cycle through fintech association channels.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $600-1,200/mo per provider given the direct regulatory and lending-law stakes involved. At 15 small/regional EWA provider clients × $800/mo average = $12,000/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new state that enacts EWA-specific legislation adds a fresh registration obligation for every provider operating there, and the CFPB's disclosure standard applies to every single fee charged — this is a compliance surface that expands automatically as both the regulatory landscape and the provider's own state footprint grow.

**Biggest risk:** The regulatory landscape here is genuinely unsettled — states may converge on model legislation, or the CFPB's interpretive rule could be revised — so the product has to be built as a rules engine that's easy to update per state rather than hardcoded logic; mitigate by treating the state-rule library as the core asset to maintain, not the UI.

**Growth path:** Side project serving 3-5 small EWA providers → fintech association channel presence → 15+ clients replaces-full-time-salary.

### 5. Towing Vessel (Tugboat) Operator USCG Subchapter M TSMS & Credentialing Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Companies operating towing vessels — harbor tugs, river towboats, and ship-assist tugs — must comply with the U.S. Coast Guard's Subchapter M (46 CFR Subchapter M), which requires every towing vessel company to implement a Towing Safety Management System (TSMS) and obtain a Certificate of Inspection (COI) for each vessel, renewed on a roughly five-year cycle, either through direct USCG Officer in Charge, Marine Inspection (OCMI) survey or through an approved Third-Party Organization (TPO) if the company elects the TSMS option, which also requires an annual internal audit plus a periodic external audit by the TPO. Every vessel master and mate must hold credentials specific to towing vessels under 46 CFR Part 15 — a Merchant Mariner Credential with the towing vessel endorsement, current medical certificate, and enrollment in a USCG-accepted drug-testing consortium with random testing performed on schedule. A small towing company running 3-8 vessels currently tracks each vessel's COI expiration, TSMS internal/external audit cycle, and every credentialed mariner's MMC/medical/drug-testing status on a dispatcher's spreadsheet, with a lapsed COI or an uncredentialed mate assigned to a vessel exposing the company to a USCG detention order that ties up a vessel — and its revenue — until the paperwork is cured.

**What to build:** A per-vessel COI expiration and TPO/OCMI survey scheduling tracker; a TSMS internal-audit and external-audit cycle calendar; a per-mariner credentialing tracker (MMC towing endorsement, medical certificate expiration, drug-testing consortium enrollment and random-test scheduling); and a vessel/crew assignment eligibility gate that flags before a vessel is dispatched with an expired COI or an uncredentialed mariner aboard.

**Skill fit:** This is a fleet-and-personnel scheduling problem structurally similar to license-renewal tracking elsewhere in this series, built against publicly available USCG Subchapter M regulatory text and TPO audit-cycle requirements — no maritime credential of your own is needed to build it.

**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: COI/TSMS audit-cycle tracker and per-mariner credentialing tracker with a dispatch eligibility gate. First 5-8 clients from the American Waterways Operators (AWO) member directory, the towing and barge industry's primary trade association, and regional river/harbor towing association chapters.

**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — direct outreach to AWO-member small towing companies and regional harbor-tug operators, offering a free audit of their current fleet's COI/credentialing status as the hook.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $500-900/mo per towing company depending on fleet size (3-8 vessels). At 22 towing company clients × $650/mo average = $14,300/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** The five-year COI renewal cycle, the annual TSMS internal audit, and the ongoing per-mariner drug-testing and credential-renewal cadence are all independently recurring obligations enforced by the Coast Guard with real vessel-detention consequences for a lapse — continuous fleet-and-crew usage, not a one-time setup.

**Biggest risk:** The towing industry is close-knit and word-of-mouth driven, with real skepticism toward outside software vendors; mitigate by attending an AWO regional meeting in person before pitching remotely, and by leading with the vessel-detention cost of a single lapsed COI rather than the software itself.

**Growth path:** Side project serving 3-5 small towing companies → AWO regional chapter presence → 20+ clients replaces-full-time-salary.
