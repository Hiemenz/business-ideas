### 1. Fireworks Display Operator & Retailer ATF Explosives License, NFPA 1123/1126 & Fire Marshal Permit Compliance
**Problem:** Fireworks display companies that shoot professional 1.3G aerial shells for municipal, sporting-event, and private displays must hold current ATF federal explosives licensing — a Type 54 Manufacturer of High Explosives license or, more commonly for a display-only operator, ATF User Permit status allowing lawful receipt and storage of 1.3G display fireworks from a licensed manufacturer — renewed on a set federal cycle, with any lapse making possession of the company's own inventory a federal explosives violation. Every display must be planned and executed under NFPA 1123 (Outdoor Display) or, for shows fired closer to spectators, NFPA 1126 (Proximate Audience Display), both of which specify minimum shell-to-audience separation distances scaled to shell size, a required site diagram and pre-show safety briefing, and a licensed pyrotechnician-in-charge on scene — a company firing a display without a current pyrotechnician license on file, or with a shell-separation distance that doesn't meet the shell size actually fired, is exactly the finding a post-incident ATF or state fire marshal investigation is built to catch, and several states now require the site diagram and permit to be filed and approved in advance of the show date, not merely available on request. Separately, consumer/display fireworks retailers selling 1.4G product to the public operate under NFPA 1124 (retail sale, storage, and manufacture of fireworks), a state fire marshal or state explosives-license seasonal retail permit that in most states must be renewed and re-inspected every season, and DOT hazmat Class 1.4G packaging and shipping-paper requirements for any interstate transport of retail inventory. A small display/retail company running several shows a season and one or more seasonal retail stands manages ATF permit currency, per-show NFPA 1123/1126 site-diagram and pyrotechnician-license documentation, and per-location NFPA 1124 seasonal retail permit renewal with no dedicated compliance function — and a show fired without a current pyrotechnician license, or a shell separation distance that doesn't match NFPA's table for the shell size used, is exactly the gap a fire marshal's pre-show permit review or a post-incident ATF investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-show compliance packet generator computing the required NFPA 1123/1126 shell-separation distance from the largest shell diameter planned, producing the site diagram and pyrotechnician-license attachment a fire marshal's advance-permit review requires; an ATF permit and per-pyrotechnician license currency tracker with a "cleared to fire" gate blocking a show from being scheduled once either lapses; and a per-location NFPA 1124 seasonal retail permit renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** NFPA 1123/1126's separation-distance tables and NFPA 1124's retail permit cycle are public, structured code requirements with a well-defined per-show and per-location record structure, and the shell-size-to-separation-distance calculator plus the fire-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "compute the requirement from the input, block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a seasonal, high-consequence trade most small display companies currently plan on a paper site sketch with no systematic cross-check against NFPA's own distance tables.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the shell-separation calculator and site-diagram generator, the ATF/pyrotechnician license tracker with the fire-eligibility gate, and the seasonal retail permit tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through American Pyrotechnics Association (APA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Pyrotechnics Association (APA) member directory and Pyrotechnics Guild International (PGI) regional chapter meetings, where small display company owners already navigate per-show fire marshal permit filings and seasonal ATF/retail permit renewal by hand.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $20/mo per licensed pyrotechnician tracked. Average client (5 pyrotechnicians) = $249 + $100 = $349/mo. 27-33 companies × $349/mo = **$9,423-11,517/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every scheduled show opens its own NFPA 1123/1126 separation-distance and pyrotechnician-eligibility check, every pyrotechnician license renews on its own independent cycle, and every retail location's NFPA 1124 seasonal permit comes up for its own annual reinspection — a per-show, per-location compliance stream that peaks hard around July 4th and New Year's but recurs across the full event calendar.
**Biggest risk:** A show fired without a currently licensed pyrotechnician on scene, or with a shell-separation distance that doesn't match NFPA's table for the shells actually used, is exactly the finding a fire marshal's advance-permit review or a post-incident ATF investigation is built to catch — the fire-eligibility gate and the separation-distance calculator are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → shell-separation calculator, ATF/pyrotechnician license tracker, and retail permit tracker deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through APA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-18 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 27-33 companies, pushing past $9.42-11.52K/mo.

### 2. RV Dealer State License, RVIA Seal & Manufacturer Warranty-Reimbursement Compliance
**Problem:** Recreational vehicle dealers hold a state RV dealer license — a distinct dealer classification in most states, separate from the general motor vehicle dealer license, commonly requiring its own bond amount and a licensed sales lot meeting state display-inventory rules — renewed annually with continuing education in several states. Every new unit on the lot must carry current RVIA (RV Industry Association) Standards Seal compliance, the industry's voluntary-but-market-required certification confirming the unit meets NFPA 1192 RV fire and life-safety standards, and a dealer caught selling a unit whose Seal documentation lapsed or was never transferred from the manufacturer risks both a state licensing complaint and a manufacturer relationship review. State RV-specific "lemon law" statutes exist separately from general automotive lemon laws in most states and commonly run on a different repair-attempt threshold and cure-period timeline — a dealer who miscounts repair attempts under the wrong statute (applying the general auto lemon law's threshold to an RV, or vice versa) exposes itself to a warranty dispute that plays out under the wrong legal framework entirely. Manufacturer warranty-reimbursement claims — labor and parts a dealer performs under factory warranty and bills back to the manufacturer — are audited against the manufacturer's own flat-rate labor schedule, and a dealer whose claim documentation doesn't match the flat-rate time allowed for a given repair code risks the manufacturer clawing back the reimbursement during a periodic warranty audit, sometimes months after the claim was paid. A small RV dealership managing a rotating lot inventory and multiple manufacturer warranty relationships tracks state license renewal, per-unit RVIA Seal documentation, RV-specific lemon-law repair-attempt counting, and warranty-reimbursement claim-to-flat-rate matching with no dedicated compliance function — and a lemon-law repair count run under the wrong statute, or a warranty claim that doesn't match the manufacturer's flat-rate schedule, is exactly the gap a state licensing audit or a manufacturer warranty-audit letter is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-unit RVIA Seal documentation tracker confirming Seal transfer from manufacturer to dealer before a unit is marked sale-ready; an RV-specific lemon-law repair-attempt counter applying each state's correct RV statute (distinct from its general auto lemon law) to a unit's service history, flagging when a unit crosses the state's cure-period threshold; and a warranty-reimbursement claim tracker matching each submitted claim's labor time against the manufacturer's flat-rate schedule before submission, flagging any mismatch before it becomes a post-payment clawback.
**Skill fit:** State RV dealer licensing statutes, RV-specific lemon-law thresholds, and manufacturer flat-rate labor schedules are structured, lookup-table data well suited to a developer who can model state-by-state regulatory variance cleanly, and the repair-attempt counter plus the flat-rate-matching gate are the same deterministic "compute the constraint from the input, flag the mismatch before submission" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a dealer trade most small RV lots currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic per-state lemon-law or flat-rate cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the RVIA Seal tracker, the RV-specific lemon-law repair-attempt counter for the 15 highest-RV-registration states, and the warranty-reimbursement flat-rate matcher; land the first 5-8 dealers through RV Dealers Association (RVDA) outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — RV Dealers Association (RVDA) member directory and regional RV dealer association meetings, where independent lot owners already navigate RVIA Seal documentation and manufacturer warranty-audit letters by hand.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $12/mo per unit in active warranty-tracking inventory. Average client (12 units under active warranty tracking) = $299 + $144 = $443/mo. 22-26 dealers × $443/mo = **$9,746-11,518/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new unit added to inventory opens its own RVIA Seal-verification event, every warranty repair opens its own flat-rate-matching check, and RV-specific lemon-law counting recurs independently on every unit with a service history — a per-unit compliance stream tied directly to the dealer's inventory turnover and warranty-service volume.
**Biggest risk:** A warranty claim that doesn't match the manufacturer's flat-rate schedule, discovered during a periodic warranty audit months after payment, forces a clawback the dealer didn't budget for — the flat-rate-matching gate catching the mismatch before submission is the core protection, alongside the lemon-law counter preventing a dealer from applying the wrong statute's repair-attempt threshold to an open unit.
**Growth path:** Side project → RVIA Seal tracker, lemon-law counter, and flat-rate matcher deployed, first 5-8 dealer retainers through RVDA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-15 dealers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 22-26 dealers, pushing past $9.75-11.52K/mo.

### 3. OSHA-Authorized Safety Training Provider OTI Trainer Authorization & Outreach Reporting Compliance
**Problem:** Companies delivering OSHA 10-hour and 30-hour Outreach Training, HAZWOPER 40-hour/8-hour refresher courses, and other regulated safety curricula must have each individual instructor authorized through an OSHA Training Institute (OTI) Education Center — a multi-day trainer course specific to the construction, general industry, or maritime curriculum the trainer will teach — and since OSHA's 2020 policy change, that authorization now expires on a fixed four-year cycle requiring an update course to renew, rather than the indefinite authorization trainers held previously; a trainer teaching a class past their authorization's expiration date has delivered instruction that OSHA does not recognize, invalidating the completion cards issued to every student in that class. Every completed Outreach class also carries a per-class student-roster reporting obligation to OSHA's outreach training tracking system, submitted within the program's required window, and a completion card issued without the underlying class properly reported is a documentation gap an OTI Education Center audit or an employer's own OSHA inspection can trace back to the training provider. A small training company running several trainers across construction and general-industry curricula, teaching dozens of classes a year for contractor and manufacturing clients, manages staggered four-year trainer-authorization renewal per curriculum, per-class roster reporting deadlines, and card-issuance documentation with no dedicated compliance function — and a class taught by a trainer whose authorization lapsed, or a roster never submitted within the reporting window, is exactly the gap an OTI Education Center's periodic trainer audit is built to catch, risking the provider's authorization to keep issuing OSHA cards at all.
**What to build:** A per-trainer authorization tracker (curriculum type, OTI Education Center of record, authorization date, and four-year renewal deadline) with a "cleared to teach" gate blocking a class from being scheduled with a trainer whose authorization has lapsed for that curriculum; a per-class roster-submission tracker computing each class's OSHA reporting deadline from its completion date, with a submitted/pending status flag; and a completion-card issuance log matching every card issued to a class confirmed reported.
**Skill fit:** OTI's four-year trainer-authorization cycle and OSHA's outreach-program roster-reporting requirement are public, structured program rules with a well-defined per-trainer and per-class record requirement, and the teach-eligibility gate plus the roster-deadline tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a training-provider niche most small safety-training companies currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a trainer's authorization status and the class they're scheduled to teach.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the trainer-authorization tracker with the teach-eligibility gate, the roster-submission deadline tracker, and the card-issuance log; land the first 5-8 training companies through National Safety Council (NSC) chapter outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Safety Council (NSC) local chapter meetings and OTI Education Center regional networks, where small safety-training company owners already navigate staggered four-year trainer renewal across multiple curricula and instructors.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $18/mo per authorized trainer tracked. Average client (6 trainers) = $199 + $108 = $307/mo. 30-36 companies × $307/mo = **$9,210-11,052/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every trainer's authorization renews on its own independent four-year clock per curriculum, every class taught opens its own roster-reporting deadline, and a growing instructor roster adds new authorization-tracking events continuously — a per-trainer, per-class compliance stream tied directly to the provider's class volume and instructor headcount.
**Biggest risk:** A class taught by a trainer whose OTI authorization lapsed invalidates every completion card issued in that class, a discovery that can force the provider to re-teach the course for affected students at its own cost — the teach-eligibility gate is the core protection, alongside the roster-deadline tracker preventing a missed reporting window from compounding the audit exposure.
**Growth path:** Side project → trainer-authorization tracker, roster-deadline tracker, and card-issuance log deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through NSC outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 30-36 companies, pushing past $9.21-11.05K/mo.

### 4. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Center UHMS Accreditation, CMS Indication-Match & NFPA 99 Fire-Safety Compliance
**Problem:** Freestanding hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) centers operate under Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) facility accreditation, a periodic re-accreditation cycle covering chamber maintenance, staff credentialing, and emergency-procedure documentation that most third-party payers now treat as a de facto prerequisite for reimbursement. CMS covers hyperbaric oxygen therapy for a narrow, specifically enumerated list of roughly 15 diagnoses — diabetic foot ulcers meeting defined Wagner-grade criteria, carbon monoxide poisoning, and a handful of others — and a treatment billed for any indication outside that list is denied outright and, if paid in error and later caught in a post-payment audit, is recouped along with potential False Claims Act exposure if the pattern looks systematic; a center's core billing-compliance task is confirming every treatment plan matches an approved indication and its supporting documentation before the claim goes out, not after. Separately, every hyperbaric chamber operates under NFPA 99 Chapter 14's fire-safety code, which imposes strict controls on materials, electrical equipment, and permitted oxygen-concentration levels inside and around a pressurized chamber given the severe fire risk of an oxygen-enriched environment — a documented, dated fire-safety inspection and staff fire-drill record is exactly what a state health department or accreditation surveyor requests first, since a chamber fire is one of the most catastrophic failure modes in outpatient medicine. A small center running one or more chambers across a growing patient census manages UHMS re-accreditation, per-treatment CMS indication-matching before billing, and NFPA 99 Chapter 14 fire-safety inspection currency with no dedicated compliance function — and a treatment billed against an indication CMS doesn't cover, or a chamber operated past its NFPA 99 fire-safety inspection interval, is exactly the gap a payer audit or an accreditation survey is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-treatment CMS indication-match gate blocking a claim from being marked ready-to-bill until the documented diagnosis matches one of CMS's approved HBOT indications; a UHMS accreditation-cycle tracker with a document checklist tied to the current accreditation standard; and a per-chamber NFPA 99 Chapter 14 fire-safety inspection and staff fire-drill tracker computing each chamber's next-due inspection.
**Skill fit:** CMS's enumerated HBOT coverage list and NFPA 99 Chapter 14's inspection requirements are public, structured regulatory sources with a well-defined per-treatment and per-chamber record requirement, and the indication-match billing gate plus the fire-safety inspection tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a high-consequence outpatient modality most small centers currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic pre-billing indication cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the CMS indication-match gate, the UHMS accreditation-cycle tracker, and the per-chamber NFPA 99 fire-safety inspection tracker; land the first 5-8 centers through Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) member directory and Baromedical Nurses Association (BNA) chapter meetings, where independent center administrators already navigate CMS's narrow indication list and UHMS re-accreditation documentation with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $25/mo per chamber tracked. Average client (3 chambers) = $299 + $75 = $374/mo. 25-30 centers × $374/mo = **$9,350-11,220/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every treatment billed opens its own CMS indication-match check, every chamber carries its own independent NFPA 99 fire-safety inspection clock, and UHMS re-accreditation recurs on its own cycle regardless of treatment volume — a per-treatment, per-chamber compliance stream tied directly to the center's patient census and chamber count.
**Biggest risk:** A treatment billed against an indication CMS doesn't cover, discovered during a post-payment audit, risks recoupment and — if the pattern looks systematic — False Claims Act exposure; a chamber operated past its NFPA 99 fire-safety inspection interval carries a catastrophic fire risk in an oxygen-enriched environment — the indication-match gate and the fire-safety inspection tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → indication-match gate, UHMS accreditation tracker, and fire-safety inspection tracker deployed, first 5-8 center retainers through UHMS outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-15 centers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 25-30 centers, pushing past $9.35-11.22K/mo.

### 5. Medical Weight Loss Clinic State Medical Board, DEA Registration & Compounded-GLP-1 Shortage-Status Compliance
**Problem:** Medical weight loss clinics prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) and adjunct Schedule III/IV appetite suppressants (phentermine, phendimetrazine) operate under state medical board rules governing physician-directed weight-management protocols — commonly requiring a documented treatment plan, periodic in-person or telehealth follow-up, and a supervising physician's sign-off on any nurse practitioner or physician assistant prescribing under a collaborative agreement — and the prescribing clinician must separately hold current DEA registration whenever a Schedule III or IV appetite suppressant is used alongside GLP-1 therapy, since prescribing a controlled substance on a lapsed DEA registration is a federal violation independent of any state licensing issue. The compounded-GLP-1 era created a compliance obligation unique to this trade: clinics sourcing compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide from an outside 503A pharmacy (rather than dispensing manufacturer-branded product) must confirm the specific drug remains on the FDA's active shortage list, because compounding a drug the FDA has declared no longer in shortage becomes an unauthorized-compounding violation exposing both the sourcing pharmacy and the prescribing clinic the moment the shortage determination changes — a status that has shifted multiple times as manufacturers scaled production, catching clinics that kept prescribing from an outdated shortage list. Every advertising claim a clinic makes about expected weight-loss results also falls under FTC scrutiny per the agency's Health Products Compliance Guidance, which requires claims to be substantiated by competent scientific evidence specific to the product and dosing protocol advertised — a claim implying results the clinic can't substantiate is exactly the pattern FTC enforcement actions against weight-loss marketers target. A small clinic running several prescribing providers across a growing patient panel manages DEA registration currency per provider, per-prescription compounded-product shortage-status verification, and marketing-claim substantiation with no dedicated compliance function — and a compounded semaglutide prescription filled after the FDA removed that drug from its shortage list, or a provider prescribing on a lapsed DEA registration, is exactly the gap a state medical board complaint or a DEA inspection is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-provider DEA registration and state medical board license currency tracker with a "cleared to prescribe" gate; a compounded-product shortage-status tracker cross-referencing the FDA's current drug shortage list before a compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide prescription is marked fillable; and a marketing-claim substantiation log tied to each advertised result claim's supporting clinical citation.
**Skill fit:** DEA registration renewal cycles, state medical board collaborative-practice rules, and the FDA's published drug shortage list are public, structured regulatory sources with a well-defined per-provider and per-prescription record requirement, and the prescribe-eligibility gate plus the shortage-status cross-reference are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fast-growing clinical niche most small clinics currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic pre-fill shortage-status check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the DEA/license currency tracker with the prescribe-eligibility gate, the compounded-product shortage-status tracker, and the marketing-claim substantiation log; land the first 5-8 clinics through Obesity Medicine Association (OMA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Obesity Medicine Association (OMA) member directory, where board-certified obesity medicine physicians and their clinics already navigate DEA registration currency and the shifting compounded-GLP-1 shortage list with no dedicated regulatory-affairs staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per prescribing provider tracked. Average client (4 providers) = $249 + $60 = $309/mo. 30-36 clinics × $309/mo = **$9,270-11,124/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every prescribing provider's DEA registration and state license renew on their own independent cycles, every compounded-product prescription requires a fresh shortage-status check as the FDA's list shifts, and a growing patient panel generates a continuous stream of prescribe-eligibility checks — a compliance stream tied directly to the clinic's provider count and prescription volume.
**Biggest risk:** A compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide prescription filled after the FDA removed that specific drug from its shortage list is an unauthorized-compounding violation exposing the clinic alongside the sourcing pharmacy — the shortage-status tracker cross-referencing the FDA's current list before every compounded fill is the core protection, alongside the prescribe-eligibility gate catching a lapsed DEA registration before it becomes a federal violation.
**Growth path:** Side project → DEA/license tracker, shortage-status tracker, and claim-substantiation log deployed, first 5-8 clinic retainers through OMA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 clinics. Replaces-full-time-salary → 30-36 clinics, pushing past $9.27-11.12K/mo.
