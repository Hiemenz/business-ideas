### 1. Mold Assessment & Remediation Contractor State Licensure & Assessor/Remediator Separation Compliance
**Problem:** Mold assessment and remediation contractors in states with dedicated mold statutes — most notably Florida's Mold-Related Services license (Florida Statutes Chapter 468, Part XVI, administered by DBPR) and the Texas Mold Assessment and Remediation Licensing Program (Texas Occupations Code Chapter 1958, administered by TDLR) — operate under a hard statutory firewall requiring the mold assessor who inspects and writes the remediation protocol and the mold remediator who performs the actual removal work on the same job to be different, independently licensed companies with no ownership overlap; a firm that performs both roles on a single job, or that dispatches a technician without a current individual license on file, has violated the statute's core consumer-protection mechanism, exactly the finding a state licensing-board complaint investigation targets first, and a violation can void the job's entire clearance documentation in a subsequent mold-related property dispute or lawsuit. Every remediation job must be documented against the IICRC S520 Standard for Professional Mold Remediation, including a post-remediation verification (clearance) test performed by an independent, separately licensed assessor confirming mold levels have returned to a normal fungal ecology before the containment barriers come down — a job closed out without documented clearance-test results is exactly the gap a homeowner's insurance claim dispute or a real-estate transaction's mold-disclosure challenge is built to expose. Individual technician licenses in these states renew on a fixed cycle with continuing-education requirements, and a technician working a job on a lapsed license exposes the firm to the same licensing-board penalty as operating without any license at all. A small remediation company running several crews across dozens of jobs a month manages per-technician license currency, per-job assessor/remediator separation documentation, and clearance-test result tracking with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-technician state license currency tracker with a "cleared to remediate" gate blocking a job assignment once a license lapses; a per-job assessor/remediator separation checker flagging any job where the same firm or a related entity is assigned both roles; and a clearance-test documentation tracker confirming an independent post-remediation verification result is on file before a job is marked closed.
**Skill fit:** Florida's and Texas's mold-licensure statutes and IICRC S520's clearance-testing protocol are public, structured regulatory and standards data with a well-defined per-technician and per-job record requirement, and the license-currency gate plus the separation checker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / flag the conflict before it becomes a violation" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an indoor-environmental trade most small remediation companies currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a job's assigned assessor and its assigned remediator.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license-currency tracker with the remediate-eligibility gate, the assessor/remediator separation checker, and the clearance-test documentation tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through Indoor Air Quality Association (IAQA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Indoor Air Quality Association (IAQA) member directory and state mold-licensure program public license-holder lists, where small remediation companies already navigate the assessor/remediator separation rule and per-technician license renewal with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $15/mo per licensed technician tracked. Average client (5 technicians) = $199 + $75 = $274/mo. 33-42 companies × $274/mo = **$9,042-11,508/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every technician's state license renews on its own independent cycle, every job opens its own assessor/remediator separation check, and every remediation completion generates its own clearance-test documentation event — a continuous, per-technician and per-job compliance stream tied directly to the company's job volume.
**Biggest risk:** A firm performing both the assessment and remediation role on the same job, or a job closed without a documented independent clearance test, is exactly the finding a state licensing-board investigation or a mold-related property lawsuit is built to catch — the separation checker and the clearance-test tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → license-currency tracker, separation checker, and clearance-test tracker deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through IAQA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 33-42 companies, pushing past $9.04-11.51K/mo.

### 2. Locksmith & Physical Security Installer State Licensing & Alarm Contractor Compliance
**Problem:** Locksmith companies operate under state private-security-board licensing regimes — such as the Texas Private Security Board locksmith license under Texas Occupations Code Chapter 1702, California's Locksmith license issued by the Bureau of Security and Investigative Services, and comparable regimes in New Jersey, Illinois, and Louisiana — that require every individual technician to hold a personal license tied to a fingerprint-based criminal-background check, renewed on a fixed multi-year cycle; a technician dispatched to a job on a lapsed license has performed unlicensed security work, an exposure a state licensing-board complaint investigation targets directly, and in several states operating unlicensed is itself a criminal offense rather than a simple fine. Locksmiths who expand into electronic access-control and alarm-system installation must separately hold a state alarm/security-system contractor license — a distinct credential from the locksmith license itself, with its own renewal cycle and, in many states, its own bonding requirement — and a technician installing an alarm system under only a locksmith license, without the separate alarm-contractor credential on file, is exactly the gap a state board audit or a client's insurance-carrier verification call is built to expose. Several states also require a locksmith company to maintain a transaction log for high-security or master-key work (recording the client's identification and the specific work performed) to guard against illegitimate lock-bypass services, and a company unable to produce that log during a licensing-board inquiry faces the same exposure as an unlicensed technician. A small locksmith and security-installer company running several technicians across a mix of residential, commercial, and alarm-installation jobs manages per-technician license currency, alarm-contractor credential tracking for technicians who cross into electronic work, and transaction-log completeness with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-technician state locksmith-license currency tracker with a "cleared to dispatch" gate blocking a job assignment once a license lapses; an alarm/security-contractor credential tracker flagging any electronic-work job assigned to a technician who holds only the locksmith license; and a high-security transaction-log tracker confirming required client-ID and work-performed records are on file.
**Skill fit:** State locksmith and alarm-contractor licensing cycles are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-technician and per-job-type record requirement, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the credential-mismatch flag are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / flag the mismatch before it becomes a violation" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a private-security-adjacent trade most small locksmith companies currently track on a paper roster with no systematic cross-check between a technician's specific credential and the job type they're dispatched to.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Associated Locksmiths of America (ALOA) member directory and state private-security-board public license-holder lists, where small locksmith companies already navigate per-technician license renewal and the separate alarm-contractor credential requirement with no dedicated compliance staff.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every technician's state license renews on its own independent cycle, every alarm-installation job opens its own credential-match check, and every high-security job generates its own transaction-log entry — a continuous, per-technician and per-job compliance stream tied directly to the company's dispatch volume.
**Biggest risk:** A technician dispatched on a lapsed license, or an alarm system installed by a technician holding only the locksmith credential, is exactly the finding a state private-security-board investigation is built to catch — the dispatch-eligibility gate and the alarm-contractor credential tracker are the core protections.

### 3. Backflow Prevention Device Tester/Certifier Water Utility Cross-Connection Compliance
**Problem:** Backflow prevention device testers — hired by commercial property owners, restaurants, irrigation contractors, and municipalities to test the backflow-prevention assemblies protecting a public water supply from contamination — must hold a current tester credential, most commonly the ASSE 5110 (or a state-equivalent such as the California-Nevada AWWA certification), renewed on a fixed multi-year cycle through continuing education, before a local water utility's cross-connection control program will accept a submitted test report; a test performed by a tester whose credential has lapsed produces a report the water utility is entitled to reject outright, forcing an expensive re-test and putting the tester's standing with that utility's approved-tester list at risk. Every tested assembly's pass/fail result must be filed with the specific water utility administering that property's water service within a strict local reporting window — commonly 10-30 days from the test date — and a tester managing devices across several different municipal water utilities, each with its own filing deadline and report format, can't reliably track which utility's window is closing without a dedicated system; a report filed past the deadline is exactly the finding a water utility's cross-connection program audit targets, and a client whose device lapses out of compliance risks having their water service shut off by the utility until a passing test is filed. Every backflow assembly itself also carries its own annual (in most jurisdictions) retest due date independent of the utility's filing-window mechanics, and a tester running a route of 100-300 devices across dozens of commercial properties and multiple water-utility jurisdictions manages per-device retest scheduling, per-utility report-filing-deadline tracking, and tester-credential currency with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-device annual retest due-date tracker computing each assembly's next-due date and generating an optimized route; a per-utility report-filing-deadline tracker computing each water utility's specific submission window from the test date and flagging any report at risk of missing it; and a tester-credential currency tracker with a "cleared to test" gate.
**Skill fit:** Local water utility cross-connection control program filing rules and ASSE tester-credential renewal cycles are public, structured municipal and certifying-body data with a well-defined per-device and per-utility record requirement, and the retest scheduler plus the filing-deadline tracker are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the cycle / flag the report at risk before it's late" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a water-safety-adjacent trade most small testing companies currently track on a paper route sheet with no systematic cross-check between a device's retest date and the specific water utility's own filing window.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-device retest scheduler, the per-utility filing-deadline tracker, and the tester-credential currency tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through American Backflow Prevention Association (ABPA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Backflow Prevention Association (ABPA) member directory and state AWWA section backflow-tester meetings, where small testing companies already juggle per-device retest scheduling across multiple water-utility filing deadlines with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $149/mo base + $2/mo per device tracked. Average client (150 devices) = $149 + $300 = $449/mo. 20-26 companies × $449/mo = **$8,980-11,674/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every tested device carries its own recurring annual retest clock, every water utility's report-filing window opens fresh with each test cycle, and the tester's own credential renews on its own independent multi-year cycle — a continuous, per-device and per-utility compliance stream tied directly to the company's device count.
**Biggest risk:** A report filed past a specific water utility's deadline, or a test performed by a tester with a lapsed credential, is exactly the finding a cross-connection program audit is built to catch — and either can end in the client's water service being shut off — the filing-deadline tracker and the tester-credential gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → retest scheduler, filing-deadline tracker, and credential gate deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through ABPA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-15 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-26 companies, pushing past $8.98-11.67K/mo.

### 4. Crane Operator & Rigging Company NCCCO Certification & OSHA Subpart CC Inspection Compliance
**Problem:** Crane operator and rigging companies must ensure every operator holds current NCCCO (National Commission for the Certification of Crane Operators) certification specific to the crane type and capacity class being operated, a requirement made mandatory by OSHA's Cranes and Derricks in Construction standard (29 CFR 1926 Subpart CC) and renewed on a 5-year cycle — an operator running a crane on a lapsed or mismatched certification (certified for a mobile crane but operating a tower crane, for instance) is exactly the finding an OSHA compliance inspection targets first on any jobsite visit, and a citation following any lift-related incident routinely centers on operator-certification status as the first document requested. Subpart CC also requires a documented pre-shift inspection before every day's use, a more thorough monthly inspection, and a comprehensive annual inspection performed by a qualified person, each logged separately — a crane dispatched to a job without a current-day pre-shift inspection on file, or one operating past its annual inspection due date, is exactly the gap an OSHA citation or a post-incident investigation is built to expose, and rigging failures are among the most closely scrutinized fact patterns in construction-site fatality investigations. Many municipalities layer their own crane-operator licensing on top of NCCCO certification — New York City's DOB crane operator license is the most prominent example — with its own separate renewal cycle a company operating across multiple jurisdictions must track independently of the federal certification. A small crane and rigging company running several operators and cranes across a rotating jobsite schedule manages per-operator NCCCO certification currency (matched to crane type), per-crane pre-shift/monthly/annual inspection documentation, and municipal license tracking with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-operator NCCCO certification tracker matched to crane type and capacity class with a "cleared to operate" gate blocking a dispatch assignment on any mismatch or lapse; a per-crane inspection tracker logging pre-shift, monthly, and annual inspections with a hard block preventing dispatch once an inspection lapses; and a municipal crane-operator license tracker for jurisdictions layering their own credential on top of NCCCO.
**Skill fit:** NCCCO's certification-type matrix and OSHA's Subpart CC inspection-interval requirements are public, structured federal regulatory and certifying-body data with a well-defined per-operator and per-crane record requirement, and the operate-eligibility gate plus the inspection tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / compute the next-due inspection from the cycle" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a high-consequence construction trade most small crane companies currently track on a paper logbook with no systematic cross-check between an operator's specific certification type and the crane they're assigned to.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the certification tracker with the operate-eligibility gate, the per-crane inspection tracker, and the municipal license tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through Specialized Carriers & Rigging Association (SC&RA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Specialized Carriers & Rigging Association (SC&RA) member directory and regional crane-rental/rigging contractor networks, where small companies already navigate per-operator NCCCO certification-type matching and Subpart CC's three-tier inspection schedule with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $20/mo per crane tracked. Average client (4 cranes) = $299 + $80 = $379/mo. 24-30 companies × $379/mo = **$9,096-11,370/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every operator's NCCCO certification renews on its own independent 5-year cycle per crane type, every crane carries its own continuous pre-shift/monthly/annual inspection clock regardless of job volume, and any municipal license renews on its own separate schedule — a continuous, per-operator and per-crane compliance stream tied directly to the company's fleet size and jobsite volume.
**Biggest risk:** An operator running a crane on a lapsed or mismatched NCCCO certification, or a crane dispatched past its inspection due date, is exactly the finding an OSHA investigation following any lift-related incident is built to catch — the operate-eligibility gate and the inspection tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → certification tracker, inspection tracker, and municipal license tracker deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through SC&RA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-18 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 24-30 companies, pushing past $9.10-11.37K/mo.

### 5. Boiler & Pressure Vessel Inspection/Service Company State Certificate-of-Operation & ASME/NBIC Compliance
**Problem:** Boiler and pressure vessel service companies operate in a regulatory environment where every commercial and industrial boiler must hold a current state (or, in some jurisdictions, National Board-administered) certificate of operation, issued after a state-commissioned boiler inspector's periodic inspection — commonly annual for high-pressure boilers — and a service company whose client's certificate has lapsed has effectively kept an unauthorized boiler running, an exposure that surfaces the moment a state boiler-safety inspector's routine visit or a post-incident investigation checks the certificate's status, with several states empowering inspectors to order an uncertified boiler shut down on the spot. Any repair or alteration performed on an in-service boiler must follow the National Board Inspection Code (NBIC) and, for more significant work, be performed by a shop holding the appropriate National Board "R" stamp (repair) or "VR" stamp (safety valve repair) authorization — a repair performed by an unstamped shop, or without the required NBIC-compliant documentation, is exactly the finding a subsequent state inspection or an insurance-carrier boiler-and-machinery audit targets, and can void the client's coverage on that specific vessel. New boiler construction and major alterations must separately meet ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section I (power boilers) or Section IV (heating boilers) requirements, with the completed work documented on the appropriate ASME data report before the vessel can be registered with the National Board. A small boiler service company running several commissioned inspectors and technicians across dozens of client boilers in multiple states manages per-client certificate-of-operation renewal tracking, per-repair R-stamp/NBIC documentation, and per-inspector state commission currency with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-boiler state certificate-of-operation renewal tracker computing each vessel's next-due inspection date with a hard alert before a client's boiler runs out of authorization; a per-repair R-stamp/NBIC documentation tracker confirming the required data report is on file for every repair or alteration; and a per-inspector state commission currency tracker.
**Skill fit:** State boiler-safety certificate-of-operation renewal cycles and the National Board's R-stamp/NBIC documentation requirements are public, structured regulatory and code-body data with a well-defined per-boiler and per-repair record requirement, and the certificate-renewal tracker plus the R-stamp documentation tracker are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the cycle / confirm the required record is on file" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a pressure-vessel-safety trade most small boiler service companies currently track on a paper inspection binder with no systematic cross-check between a client's certificate status and their next scheduled inspection.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the certificate-of-operation tracker, the R-stamp/NBIC documentation tracker, and the inspector-commission currency tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors-affiliated state chief-inspector office outreach and American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) boiler-code committee member networks.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors state-chapter contacts and regional boiler-service contractor associations, where small service companies already navigate per-client certificate-of-operation renewal across multiple states and R-stamp documentation for every repair with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per boiler tracked. Average client (10 boilers) = $249 + $150 = $399/mo. 23-29 companies × $399/mo = **$9,177-11,571/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every client boiler carries its own recurring certificate-of-operation renewal clock, every repair or alteration opens its own R-stamp/NBIC documentation event, and every commissioned inspector's own state credential renews on its own independent cycle — a continuous, per-boiler and per-repair compliance stream tied directly to the company's client fleet size.
**Biggest risk:** A client boiler operating past a lapsed certificate of operation, or a repair performed without the required R-stamp/NBIC documentation, is exactly the finding a state boiler inspection or an insurance-carrier boiler-and-machinery audit is built to catch — the certificate-renewal tracker and the R-stamp documentation tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → certificate-of-operation tracker, R-stamp documentation tracker, and inspector-commission tracker deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through National Board/ASME outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 13-16 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 23-29 companies, pushing past $9.18-11.57K/mo.

Idea #1 (mold assessment & remediation) has the cleanest single-point compliance failure of the five — the assessor/remediator separation rule is a bright-line statutory requirement with no gray area, making the separation checker an unusually easy, easy-to-explain pitch to a Florida or Texas contractor who has seen a competitor cited for violating it.
