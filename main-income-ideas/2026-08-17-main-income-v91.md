### 1. Payroll Service Bureau IRS Reporting Agent (Form 8655), 94x E-File & Multi-State Deposit Compliance
**Problem:** Independent payroll service bureaus that process payroll and file employment taxes for small-business clients operate under the IRS Reporting Agent framework — a completed Form 8655 (Reporting Agent Authorization) on file for every client before the bureau can file that client's Forms 940, 941, or 944 electronically through the IRS 94x e-file program, or deposit federal payroll taxes on the client's behalf — and a client whose Form 8655 lapses, was never filed, or doesn't match the exact EIN and tax form combination the bureau is filing under is exactly the finding an IRS e-file program compliance check is built to catch, since filing or depositing without current authorization is treated as an unauthorized third-party filing. Every client also carries its own federal deposit schedule, redetermined annually under the lookback-period rule (26 CFR § 31.6302-1) as either monthly (deposit due the 15th of the following month) or semi-weekly (due the Wednesday or Friday following each payday, depending on which day the payroll fell on) — a bureau running dozens of clients across a mixed monthly/semi-weekly book has no room for a missed deposit deadline, since a late federal tax deposit triggers an automatic, non-negotiable IRS penalty scaled to how many days late the deposit was (2% to 15% of the unpaid amount) charged directly against the employer client, not the bureau, but landing squarely on the bureau's reputation. State new-hire reporting under the federal PRWORA framework (states set their own reporting window, commonly 20 days from hire) and quarterly state unemployment insurance wage filings add a second, state-by-state layer of per-client deadlines that varies by which states each client has employees in. A small bureau running 30-80 client companies across a mix of monthly and semi-weekly deposit schedules and several states manages per-client Form 8655 authorization currency, per-client deposit-schedule deadline tracking, and per-client new-hire/SUTA filing deadlines with no dedicated compliance function beyond the payroll software's own generic reminder emails.
**What to build:** A per-client Form 8655 authorization tracker flagging any client without a current, matching Reporting Agent authorization on file; a per-client federal deposit-schedule calendar computing each upcoming deposit's exact due date from the client's monthly or semi-weekly lookback-period assignment; and a per-client new-hire-reporting and SUTA quarterly-filing deadline tracker scoped to each state the client has employees in.
**Skill fit:** IRS Reporting Agent authorization rules and the lookback-period deposit-schedule formula are public, structured federal regulatory data with a well-defined per-client, per-tax-form record requirement, and the authorization tracker plus the deposit-schedule calendar are the same deterministic "confirm the required record is on file before the action proceeds / compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a payroll-processing trade most small bureaus currently track through their payroll software's generic client list with no systematic cross-check between a specific client's Form 8655 status and today's deposit due date.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the Form 8655 tracker, the deposit-schedule calendar, and the new-hire/SUTA tracker; land the first 5-8 bureaus through American Payroll Association (APA, now PayrollOrg) local chapter outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — PayrollOrg's local chapter network and independent payroll-bureau owner Facebook and LinkedIn groups, where small bureaus already juggle mixed monthly/semi-weekly deposit schedules across dozens of clients with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $10/mo per active employer client tracked. Average client (a bureau serving 60 employer clients) = $299 + $600 = $899/mo. 12-15 bureaus × $899/mo = **$10,788-13,485/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every payroll run reopens the deposit-deadline countdown for every client, every new client onboarded opens its own Form 8655 authorization event, and each client's lookback-period deposit schedule is redetermined every year — a continuous, per-client and per-payroll-cycle compliance stream tied directly to the bureau's client roster.
**Biggest risk:** A federal tax deposit made late, or a filing submitted without a current Form 8655 authorization on file, is exactly the finding an IRS e-file compliance check or a penalty notice is built to catch — the authorization tracker and the deposit-schedule calendar are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → Form 8655 tracker, deposit calendar, and new-hire/SUTA tracker deployed, first 5-8 bureau retainers through PayrollOrg outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 7-9 bureaus. Replaces-full-time-salary → 12-15 bureaus, pushing past $10.79-13.49K/mo.

### 2. Reverse Mortgage (HECM) Loan Originator/Broker HUD Counseling-Certificate & FHA Case-Number Compliance
**Problem:** Reverse mortgage originators and brokerages processing HUD's Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) program operate under a compliance sequence unique to reverse lending: HUD Handbook 4000.1 requires every borrower to complete counseling from a HUD-approved HECM counseling agency and obtain a signed counseling certificate — valid for a fixed window, commonly 180 days from issuance — before the lender may even order the property appraisal or request an FHA case number, and a case number requested or an appraisal ordered ahead of a valid counseling certificate is exactly the finding a HUD/FHA quality-control review targets first, potentially voiding the loan's FHA insurance eligibility entirely. Every borrower must also clear HUD's mandatory financial assessment (Mortgagee Letters 2014-21 and 2015-11) — a documented review of income, credit history, and residual income — and, for borrowers flagged as at financial risk, the lender must establish a Life Expectancy Set-Aside (LESA) escrowing a calculated portion of loan proceeds to cover future property taxes and insurance, a determination that must be documented and consistently applied per case. Because HECM originators also hold standard NMLS mortgage loan originator licenses layered with SAFE Act continuing education on top of the HECM-specific requirements, a small brokerage running a rotating pipeline of a dozen or more active cases manages per-case counseling-certificate validity, financial-assessment/LESA documentation, and originator NMLS/CE renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a loan-officer's personal spreadsheet.
**What to build:** A per-case HUD counseling-certificate validity tracker with a "cleared to request case number" gate blocking a case-number request or appraisal order until a current certificate is on file; a per-case financial-assessment and LESA-determination documentation tracker; and a per-originator NMLS license and SAFE Act CE renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** HUD Handbook 4000.1's counseling-certificate sequencing and financial-assessment requirements are public, structured federal regulatory data with a well-defined per-case record requirement, and the case-number-eligibility gate plus the LESA documentation tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / require the record before the action proceeds" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a reverse-lending trade most small brokerages currently track on a loan officer's personal spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a specific case's counseling-certificate validity window and today's pipeline stage.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the counseling-certificate tracker with the case-number-eligibility gate, the financial-assessment/LESA tracker, and the originator NMLS/CE tracker; land the first 5-8 brokerages through National Reverse Mortgage Lenders Association (NRMLA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — NRMLA's member directory and regional reverse-mortgage broker networks, where small brokerages already juggle per-case counseling-certificate windows against a rotating pipeline with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $12/mo per active case in pipeline. Average client (15 active cases) = $199 + $180 = $379/mo. 24-30 brokerages × $379/mo = **$9,096-11,370/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new case opens its own counseling-certificate-validity clock and its own financial-assessment/LESA documentation event, and every originator's NMLS license and CE hours renew on their own independent cycle — a continuous, per-case compliance stream tied directly to the brokerage's active pipeline.
**Biggest risk:** A case number requested or an appraisal ordered without a valid counseling certificate on file is exactly the finding a HUD/FHA quality-control review is built to catch and can void the loan's FHA insurance eligibility — the case-number-eligibility gate is the core protection.
**Growth path:** Side project → counseling-certificate tracker, financial-assessment tracker, and NMLS/CE tracker deployed, first 5-8 brokerage retainers through NRMLA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-17 brokerages. Replaces-full-time-salary → 24-30 brokerages, pushing past $9.10-11.37K/mo.

### 3. Section 1031 Qualified Intermediary Safe-Harbor Deadline & State Exchange-Facilitator Bonding Compliance
**Problem:** Section 1031 qualified intermediaries (QIs) hold a taxpayer's relinquished-property sale proceeds in a qualified escrow or qualified trust under Treas. Reg. § 1.1031(k)-1's safe harbor while the taxpayer identifies replacement property within a hard, unwaivable 45-day window from the relinquished-property closing and completes the exchange within 180 days (or the taxpayer's tax-return due date, if earlier) — a deadline missed by even one day forfeits the client's entire capital-gains tax deferral, with no extension available regardless of the reason, making the QI's deadline-tracking the single most consequential compliance function in the transaction. A growing number of states — Nebraska, California, Idaho, Colorado, Washington, Virginia, and Maine among them — now require the QI itself to be licensed or registered as an "exchange facilitator" and to maintain a fidelity bond or errors-and-omissions insurance policy at a minimum coverage level, plus documented segregation of every client's exchange funds from the QI's own operating funds and from every other client's funds, since commingled or misappropriated exchange proceeds is exactly the failure mode behind the QI-insolvency scandals that prompted these state statutes in the first place. Every exchange also requires confirming the QI itself isn't a "disqualified person" under the regulation — an exchanger's attorney, accountant, or real estate agent within the two years preceding the exchange is barred from serving as that exchanger's QI — a conflict-check most small QI firms currently perform from memory rather than a documented record. A small QI firm running a rotating book of a dozen or more concurrent exchanges across different clients and closing dates manages per-exchange 45-day and 180-day deadline tracking, per-client fund-segregation documentation, and the firm's own state exchange-facilitator bond/registration renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a paper closing-date calendar.
**What to build:** A per-exchange deadline tracker computing each client's exact 45-day identification and 180-day exchange due dates from the relinquished-property closing date, with escalating alerts as either deadline approaches; a per-client segregated-fund ledger confirming each exchanger's proceeds are held separately and reconciled against the firm's qualified escrow/trust account; and a state exchange-facilitator bond and registration renewal tracker paired with a disqualified-person conflict-check log per exchange.
**Skill fit:** Treas. Reg. § 1.1031(k)-1's safe-harbor deadline formula and state exchange-facilitator bonding requirements are public, structured federal and state regulatory data with a well-defined per-exchange record requirement, and the deadline tracker plus the fund-segregation ledger are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / cross-check two records that must match" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a tax-deferral fiduciary trade most small QI firms currently track on a paper closing-date calendar with no systematic cross-check between a specific exchange's proceeds and the firm's own qualified-escrow account balance.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the 45/180-day deadline tracker, the segregated-fund ledger, and the state bond/registration and conflict-check tracker; land the first 5-8 QI firms through Federation of Exchange Accommodators (FEA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Federation of Exchange Accommodators' (FEA) member directory and Certified Exchange Specialist (CES) network, where small QI firms already juggle overlapping 45-day and 180-day deadlines across a rotating exchange book with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $25/mo per active exchange in the 45/180-day window. Average client (8 active exchanges) = $299 + $200 = $499/mo. 19-24 QI firms × $499/mo = **$9,481-11,976/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new exchange opens its own 45-day and 180-day deadline clock and its own fund-segregation record, and the firm's own state bond and registration renew on their own independent cycle — a continuous, per-exchange compliance stream tied directly to the firm's transaction volume.
**Biggest risk:** A missed 45-day identification or 180-day exchange deadline forfeits the client's entire tax deferral with no extension available, and commingled exchange funds is exactly the failure mode state exchange-facilitator statutes exist to prevent — the deadline tracker and the fund-segregation ledger are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → deadline tracker, fund-segregation ledger, and bond/conflict-check tracker deployed, first 5-8 QI firm retainers through FEA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 11-14 firms. Replaces-full-time-salary → 19-24 firms, pushing past $9.48-11.98K/mo.

### 4. Franchisor FTC Franchise Rule FDD Disclosure & Multi-State Registration Compliance
**Problem:** Franchisors offering and selling franchises operate under the FTC Franchise Rule (16 CFR Part 436), which requires a Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) to be delivered to every prospective franchisee at least 14 calendar days before that franchisee signs any binding agreement or pays any money to the franchisor — a franchise sold on a shortened disclosure window is exactly the finding an FTC or state franchise-regulator investigation targets first, and can expose the franchisor to franchisee rescission claims and regulatory penalties years after the sale closed. The FDD itself must be refreshed annually within 120 days of the franchisor's fiscal year-end and amended promptly whenever a material change occurs — a new litigation disclosure, a changed initial-investment range, a new officer — and separately registered, with its own renewal cycle, in each of the 14 state "registration states" (California, Hawaii, Illinois, Indiana, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, New York, North Dakota, Rhode Island, South Dakota, Virginia, Washington, and Wisconsin) where the franchisor is actively offering franchises, several of which also require any franchise salesperson or broker working on the franchisor's behalf to hold their own state registration. A franchisor selling in a registration state on a lapsed FDD registration, or one that hasn't filed the required renewal or material-change amendment on time, is exactly the gap a state franchise examiner's routine review is built to catch, and an out-of-compliance registration in even one state can freeze that state's entire franchise-sales pipeline until it's cured. A small but growing franchise system selling into eight or more states manages FDD annual-update currency, per-state registration renewal deadlines that don't share a common calendar, and franchise-seller registration tracking with no dedicated compliance function beyond outside counsel invoiced by the hour.
**What to build:** An FDD annual-update deadline tracker computing the 120-day-after-fiscal-year-end due date and flagging any pending material change requiring an amendment filing; a per-state registration renewal calendar tracking each of the 14 registration states' independent renewal cycles with a "cleared to sell" gate per state; and a franchise-seller/broker state registration tracker.
**Skill fit:** The FTC Franchise Rule's disclosure-timing requirements and the 14 registration states' independent renewal cycles are public, structured federal and state regulatory data with a well-defined per-state record requirement, and the FDD-update tracker plus the sell-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / block the action until the credential clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a franchise-sales trade most small franchise systems currently track through outside counsel's own calendar with no systematic, always-on cross-check between a specific state's registration status and today's sales activity in that state.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the FDD annual-update tracker, the 14-state registration renewal calendar with the sell-eligibility gate, and the franchise-seller registration tracker; land the first 5-8 franchisors through International Franchise Association (IFA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the International Franchise Association's (IFA) emerging-franchisor member network and franchise-development consultant referral relationships, where small, growing franchise systems already juggle staggered registration-state renewal dates with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base + $20/mo per registration state the franchisor is active in. Average client (10 of 14 registration states) = $349 + $200 = $549/mo. 17-21 franchisors × $549/mo = **$9,333-11,529/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every fiscal year-end reopens the FDD annual-update cycle, every registration state renews on its own independent schedule, and every new state the franchisor expands into adds a fresh registration-tracking obligation — a continuous, per-state compliance stream tied directly to the franchise system's growth.
**Biggest risk:** A franchise sold on a shortened 14-day disclosure window, or one sold in a state with a lapsed FDD registration, is exactly the finding an FTC or state franchise-regulator review is built to catch and can expose the franchisor to rescission claims — the FDD-update tracker and the sell-eligibility gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → FDD tracker, 14-state registration calendar, and seller-registration tracker deployed, first 5-8 franchisor retainers through IFA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-12 franchisors. Replaces-full-time-salary → 17-21 franchisors, pushing past $9.33-11.53K/mo.

### 5. Home Warranty & Service Contract Provider State DOI Registration & Reserve/Insurance-Backing Compliance
**Problem:** Home warranty and home service contract providers — companies selling consumers a contract covering breakdown repair or replacement of home systems and appliances — operate under state service-contract-provider statutes, most modeled on the NAIC Service Contracts Model Act, that require registration with the state department of insurance (or, in some states, the department of financial services) before a single contract can be sold in that state, and a provider selling contracts in a state where its registration has lapsed is exactly the finding a state insurance-department market-conduct exam is built to catch, with penalties that can include an order to stop selling in that state entirely. Every registered provider must also maintain financial backing for its outstanding contract obligations — either a contractual liability insurance policy (CLIP) from an authorized insurer covering the provider's claim obligations, or, as a state-permitted alternative, a funded reserve account holding a specified percentage of gross contract revenue plus a minimum net-worth threshold — and a provider whose CLIP has lapsed or whose reserve account has fallen under the required funding level is exactly the insolvency-risk gap these statutes exist to prevent, since a home warranty company's entire business model is a promise to pay future claims. Annual financial-statement filings to each state department of insurance, and state-specific contract-form filing and approval requirements, add a further per-state layer, and several states also mandate a "free-look" cancellation period and a pro-rata refund formula that must be applied correctly on every cancelled contract. A small or regional home warranty provider selling into a dozen or more states manages per-state registration renewal, CLIP/reserve-account funding-level currency, and per-state financial-statement filing deadlines with no dedicated compliance function beyond a spreadsheet shared with outside counsel.
**What to build:** A per-state DOI registration renewal tracker with a "cleared to sell" gate per state; a CLIP policy and reserve-account funding-level tracker flagging any state where coverage or reserve funding has fallen below the required threshold; and a per-state annual financial-statement filing deadline tracker paired with a cancellation/pro-rata-refund rule reference by state.
**Skill fit:** State service-contract-provider registration cycles and CLIP/reserve-funding thresholds are public, structured state insurance-regulatory data with a well-defined per-state record requirement, and the sell-eligibility gate plus the funding-level tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the credential clears / compute the metric against the required threshold" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a consumer-service-contract trade most small providers currently track through outside counsel's own state-by-state notes with no systematic, always-on cross-check between a specific state's registration status and the provider's active sales in that state.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the DOI registration tracker with the sell-eligibility gate, the CLIP/reserve funding-level tracker, and the financial-statement filing tracker; land the first 5-8 providers through National Home Service Contract Association (NHSCA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Home Service Contract Association's (NHSCA) member directory and regional home warranty industry conference networks, where small and regional providers already juggle staggered per-state registration renewals with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $15/mo per state licensed in. Average client (12 states) = $299 + $180 = $479/mo. 19-24 providers × $479/mo = **$9,101-11,496/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every state registration renews on its own independent cycle, every annual financial statement reopens its own filing event, and the CLIP/reserve funding level is a continuously monitored metric that changes with every contract sold — a continuous, per-state compliance stream tied directly to the provider's multi-state footprint.
**Biggest risk:** A contract sold in a state with a lapsed registration, or a CLIP/reserve account that falls below the required funding level, is exactly the insolvency-risk gap a state market-conduct exam is built to catch — the sell-eligibility gate and the funding-level tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → DOI registration tracker, funding-level tracker, and financial-statement tracker deployed, first 5-8 provider retainers through NHSCA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 11-14 providers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 19-24 providers, pushing past $9.10-11.50K/mo.
