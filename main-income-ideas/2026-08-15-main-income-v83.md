### 1. Occupational Health Clinic & DOT Medical Examiner NRCME Registry & Exam-Reporting Compliance
**Problem:** Every commercial driver subject to FMCSA regulation must pass a physical exam performed by a healthcare provider — an MD, DO, PA, NP, or in some states a DC — who has completed FMCSA-approved training, passed a certification exam, and is actively listed on FMCSA's National Registry of Certified Medical Examiners (NRCME), established under the 2012 MAP-21 Act and codified at 49 CFR Part 391 Subpart E. That listing isn't permanent: an examiner's 10-year certification requires a mandatory refresher-training-and-exam cycle roughly every 5 years, and any exam performed while an examiner's registry status has lapsed produces a Medical Examiner's Certificate that FMCSA and state DMVs can treat as invalid — exposing both the examiner and the clinic that scheduled the exam, and putting the driver's certification (and the trucking company that relies on it) at risk. On top of the credentialing clock, every completed exam carries its own deadline: the examiner must submit exam results to FMCSA's National Registry within 3 business days, a filing window occupational health clinics running dozens of DOT physicals a week routinely miss when tracking it by hand across multiple examiners and locations. A multi-location occupational health chain or urgent care group offering DOT physicals has no single view of which examiners are current, which are approaching their refresher deadline, and which exams from this week still need to be submitted.
**What to build:** An examiner roster tracking each provider's NRCME registry status and 5-year refresher-training deadline; a per-exam 3-business-day FMCSA submission-deadline tracker with a red/yellow/green dashboard; and a booking eligibility gate that blocks scheduling a DOT physical with any examiner whose registry status is expired or approaching lapse, so front-desk staff can't accidentally book a driver with a non-current examiner.
**Skill fit:** FMCSA's National Registry has a public examiner-lookup interface, and syncing examiner status against it, layered with deterministic date-math for the 5-year refresher cycle and the 3-business-day submission window, is the same structured public-registry-plus-deadline-tracker pattern this series builds repeatedly — applied to a credentialing trade most occupational health clinics currently track in a shared spreadsheet with no automated cross-check against the live registry.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: examiner roster with registry-status sync, refresher-deadline tracker, and the booking eligibility gate. First 5-8 clients from multi-location occupational health and urgent care groups already running DOT physical programs.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the American College of Occupational and Environmental Medicine (ACOEM) member directory and the Urgent Care Association's occupational-medicine service-line forum, where clinic operations managers already coordinate DOT physical program compliance across locations.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $200-350/mo per clinic location (roster size and exam volume scale the price within that band). 40 clinic locations × $275/mo average = $11,000/mo. A regional occupational health chain with 15-20 locations under one contract can alone clear $4-7K/mo, with the rest of the ceiling built from independent urgent care groups.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The 5-year refresher cycle and the 3-business-day per-exam submission window are non-optional, recurring, and individually consequential — a single missed submission or a lapsed examiner performing exams creates an audit finding and a liability exposure, not a one-time filing a clinic can "get right once" and stop paying for.
**Biggest risk:** A clinic assumes its EHR or scheduling system already tracks this (most don't have registry-status sync or a booking gate) — the product's pitch has to lead with a live registry-status check the clinic can verify against a specific examiner in the demo, not a generic reminder claim.
**Growth path:** Side project → first regional occupational health chain contract (5+ locations) → replaces-part-time-income → national urgent care group with multi-state locations → replaces-full-time-salary.

### 2. Construction Stormwater SWPPP & NPDES Construction General Permit Compliance for General Contractors
**Problem:** Any construction project disturbing one acre or more (or a smaller site that's part of a larger common plan of development) triggers coverage under the Clean Water Act's NPDES Construction General Permit (CGP) — issued directly by EPA in the handful of states without their own delegated program, and by the state environmental agency everywhere else — which requires filing a Notice of Intent (NOI) before breaking ground, maintaining a site-specific Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) documenting Best Management Practices (BMPs), conducting inspections on a fixed weekly cadence plus within 24 hours of any qualifying rain event (commonly a 0.25"+ storm), retaining every inspection report, and filing a Notice of Termination (NOT) once the site reaches final stabilization. EPA's 2024 inflation-adjusted maximum civil penalty for Clean Water Act violations runs into the tens of thousands of dollars per day per violation, and a missed post-storm inspection is exactly the kind of gap a state inspector or a downstream complaint uncovers. A small general contractor or site developer running three to five concurrent active sites has no dedicated environmental compliance staff and typically tracks SWPPP inspections on whatever superintendent remembers to walk the site after it rains.
**What to build:** A per-site SWPPP document repository with NOI/NOT filing-deadline tracking; a weekly inspection scheduler layered with a NOAA weather-API-triggered 24-hour post-storm-event inspection window that fires automatically when a qualifying rainfall hits a site's coordinates; a BMP maintenance checklist and photo-documentation log per inspection; and a final-stabilization gate that blocks NOT filing until the last required inspection is on file.
**Skill fit:** Pulling live rainfall data from NOAA's public API to auto-trigger the 24-hour inspection window per site's GPS coordinates is a genuinely differentiated engineering feature most calendar-reminder competitors don't build, and the state-by-state CGP inspection-frequency variance is the same structured regulatory-data problem this series solves repeatedly.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: SWPPP repository, NOAA-triggered inspection scheduler, and NOI/NOT deadline tracker for one state's CGP rules first. First 5-8 clients from local Associated General Contractors (AGC) chapter members running multiple concurrent sites.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — direct outreach through the AGC local chapter's environmental/regulatory committee and the local Home Builders Association (HBA), where site superintendents already discuss stormwater inspection burden.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $500-900/mo per GC company, covering unlimited active sites up to a reasonable cap (additional fee beyond that). 20 GC clients × $750/mo average = $15,000/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The weekly-plus-post-storm inspection cadence never stops as long as a site is active, and a GC running multiple projects year-round has a permanent, growing site count rather than a single filing that ends the relationship.
**Biggest risk:** A GC believes its civil engineer's SWPPP document (drafted once at permitting) already satisfies ongoing compliance — the pitch has to show the automated 24-hour post-storm trigger catching an inspection window the GC's own team would have missed, using real recent rainfall data for one of their actual sites.
**Growth path:** Side project → first AGC chapter referral → 8-10 GC clients → replaces-part-time-income → regional expansion via a second AGC chapter → replaces-full-time-salary.

### 3. Structured Settlement Factoring Company State SSPA & IRC §5891 Qualified-Order Compliance
**Problem:** Companies that purchase a consumer's future structured-settlement annuity payments for a discounted lump sum operate under every state's Structured Settlement Protection Act (SSPA), adopted following the NCOIL model act, which requires court (or in some states administrative) approval before a transfer is effective, a mandatory disclosure statement delivered to the payee days in advance of signing — spelling out the payment amounts and due dates being transferred, the discount rate, the gross advance amount, itemized broker and other fees, the net advance amount, and the effective annual interest rate — and in several states, documented independent professional advice or a written waiver of it. The federal stakes are even higher than the state penalty: IRC §5891 imposes a 40% excise tax on the factoring company against the discounted present value of every transferred payment stream, unless the transfer was approved by a "qualified order" from a court or responsible administrative authority under that state's SSPA — meaning a defective or missing court-approval record isn't just a state compliance gap, it's a federal tax liability on the transaction itself. A factoring company buying settlements across a dozen states manages state-specific disclosure content, cooling-off timing, and court-filing venue rules with no standardized workflow tying each transaction's disclosure and court-approval documentation together.
**What to build:** A per-transaction disclosure-statement generator populating the state-required fields (discount rate, gross/net advance amount, effective annual interest rate, itemized fees) with a timestamped proof-of-delivery log satisfying each state's cooling-off period before signature; a court-filing/petition deadline and venue tracker by state; and a §5891 qualified-order documentation checklist that gates fund disbursement until proof of court approval is on file for that specific transaction.
**Skill fit:** Every state's SSPA disclosure-content requirements and cooling-off periods are published statutory text — turning that into a per-state template engine plus a disbursement gate keyed to a specific compliance document being present is the same deterministic "don't release the money until the record exists" pattern this series builds for licensing and filing gates elsewhere.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: disclosure-statement generator for the 10 highest-transaction-volume states, cooling-off timer, and the disbursement gate. First 5-8 clients from mid-sized factoring companies doing transactions across multiple states.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the National Association of Settlement Purchasers (NASP) member directory, where compliance officers at member companies already coordinate on state-by-state disclosure practice.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $600-1,000/mo per company (fewer clients, but each is a company doing meaningful transaction volume that can't risk a §5891 excise-tax exposure). 15 clients × $700/mo average = $10,500/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every single transaction — not a periodic filing — carries its own disclosure-timing, court-approval, and disbursement-gate requirement, so usage scales directly with a client's transaction volume rather than settling into an annual-renewal-only rhythm.
**Biggest risk:** A factoring company already has in-house legal counsel drafting disclosures manually — the pitch needs to lead with the disbursement gate closing the specific gap between "counsel drafted a disclosure" and "someone verified the court order is actually on file before wiring funds," a control most shops don't have even when they have a lawyer.
**Growth path:** Side project → first NASP-referral client → 8-10 clients → replaces-part-time-income → the majority of NASP's mid-sized member roster → replaces-full-time-salary.

### 4. Data Broker Multi-State Registration & Consumer Disclosure Compliance
**Problem:** Companies that sell or license personal information about consumers they have no direct relationship with are "data brokers" under a growing list of state statutes — California's Delete Act (Civil Code § 1798.99.80 et seq.), now administered by the California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) rather than the Attorney General, Vermont's data broker law (9 V.S.A. § 2446), and Texas's data broker registration requirement (Business & Commerce Code Chapter 509) — each requiring annual registration, typically due by January 31, a filing fee, and public disclosures covering the categories of data collected and the consumer opt-out mechanism offered. California's original data broker statute imposes a penalty of $200 for each day a company fails to register once required, and starting in 2026 California's centralized DROP (Delete Request and Opt-out Platform) will let any consumer submit a single deletion request that every registered data broker must process across its own databases within a set verification window — a brand-new operational obligation layered on top of the existing registration requirement. A small ad-tech, marketing-data, or people-search company selling into multiple states manages separate January 31 filing deadlines, separate disclosure content per state, and — for California — an entirely new deletion-request-processing workflow, with no dedicated compliance function tracking any of it.
**What to build:** A multi-state data broker registration tracker with a January 31 filing-deadline dashboard across CA/CPPA, VT, and TX; a required-disclosure content generator (data categories collected, opt-out mechanism, breach history where required); and, ahead of California's 2026 rollout, a DROP-compliant deletion-request intake and processing-deadline tracker.
**Skill fit:** State data-broker registries are public structured filings with a fixed annual deadline, and the DROP deletion-request workflow is a genuinely new, well-defined data-processing pipeline problem — exactly the kind of "structure the regulatory data, gate the deadline" build this series repeats, applied to a still-small compliance software market with essentially no incumbent competitor built specifically for data brokers.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: CA/VT/TX registration tracker with disclosure-content generator, deletion-request intake queue stubbed in ahead of the 2026 DROP deadline. First 5-8 clients from small ad-tech and marketing-data companies who file today with a spreadsheet.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through International Association of Privacy Professionals (IAPP) local chapter meetups and the Network Advertising Initiative (NAI) member company list, where privacy/compliance leads at smaller data companies already discuss multi-state registration burden.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $200-350/mo per client. 45 clients × $275/mo average = $12,375/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The list of states requiring data broker registration is actively growing, California's DROP deletion mechanism adds an ongoing per-request processing obligation on top of the annual filing, and a client's exposure only increases as more states adopt similar laws — this isn't a one-time filing that plateaus.
**Biggest risk:** A prospect assumes "we're not selling data, we're just using it for advertising" and doesn't realize they meet the statutory data-broker definition — the pitch needs a short eligibility quiz mapped to each state's actual statutory language, since the gap between "we thought we were exempt" and "we owed $200/day" is the entire sales hook.
**Growth path:** Side project → first IAPP-chapter referral client → 10-12 clients → replaces-part-time-income → national ad-tech/data-industry client base as more states pass data broker laws → replaces-full-time-salary.

### 5. Telemarketing & Lead-Generation Company TCPA/TSR Consent, DNC-Scrub & State Registration Compliance
**Problem:** Companies making outbound sales calls or generating leads for resale operate under a stack of federal and state rules with real teeth: the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule (16 CFR Part 310) requires scrubbing call lists against the National Do Not Call Registry no less than every 31 days, honoring a company-specific internal do-not-call list, and respecting calling-time restrictions; the TCPA (47 U.S.C. § 227) requires prior express written consent for autodialed or prerecorded marketing calls and texts to cell phones and creates a private right of action with statutory damages of $500 per negligent violation and up to $1,500 per willful violation — the engine behind the wave of TCPA class actions that have bankrupted small telemarketing and lead-gen operators; and the FCC's newly finalized one-to-one consent rule requires that consent be tied to a single, specifically named seller rather than a batch of buyers on a shared "aggregated" lead form, upending how most lead-gen companies have documented consent for years. On top of the federal rules, states including Florida (Telemarketing Act, Fla. Stat. § 501.059), Texas, North Carolina, and Louisiana require commercial telemarketers to register — and in some cases post a bond — before making calls into or from that state. A small outbound sales or lead-generation shop has no systematic way to prove, per lead, that consent was properly captured for the specific seller now calling on it, or that its call list was scrubbed within the last 31 days.
**What to build:** An automated 31-day National DNC Registry re-scrub scheduler with a proof-of-scrub audit log; a per-lead consent-record tracker capturing the source, timestamp, and the single named seller the consent was tied to, structured to satisfy the FCC's one-to-one consent rule; an internal company-specific DNC list manager; and a multi-state commercial-telemarketer registration/bond renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** The National DNC Registry has a defined data-exchange interface for subscribers, and building the 31-day scrub-and-log cycle plus a structured per-lead consent record with a queryable audit trail is exactly the "prove compliance happened, on schedule, per record" pattern this series builds — applied to a trade currently exposed to statutory class-action damages that make a missed scrub or an undocumented consent extraordinarily expensive relative to most other compliance failures in this repo.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: DNC re-scrub scheduler with audit log, per-lead consent tracker built around the one-to-one rule's data fields, and state registration tracker for the top handful of telemarketer-registration states. First 5-8 clients from small lead-gen shops already worried about TCPA exposure.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through LeadsCouncil (the lead-generation industry trade association) and the Professional Association for Customer Engagement (PACE, the former American Teleservices Association), where compliance is a constant topic given the industry's class-action exposure.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $350-550/mo per client. 30 clients × $450/mo average = $13,500/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The 31-day DNC re-scrub cycle and per-lead consent documentation never stop as long as a client keeps calling or generating leads, and the FCC's one-to-one consent rule is forcing every lead-gen company in the industry to rebuild their consent-capture process at the same time — a wave of forced adoption this product is timed directly against.
**Biggest risk:** A client assumes their CRM or dialer platform already handles TCPA compliance — the pitch needs to show a specific gap (an aggregated-consent lead form that fails the new one-to-one rule, or a DNC scrub timestamp older than 31 days) pulled from the prospect's own current process during the demo.
**Growth path:** Side project → first LeadsCouncil-referral client → 10-12 clients → replaces-part-time-income → broader PACE member base as the one-to-one consent rule forces industry-wide re-compliance → replaces-full-time-salary.
