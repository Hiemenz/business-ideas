### 1. Consumer Reporting Agency (Background Check Company) FCRA & State CRA Registration Compliance
**Problem:** Companies that run employment, tenant, and volunteer background checks operate as "consumer reporting agencies" under the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.), which requires maximum possible accuracy in every report furnished (§ 1681e(b)), a 30-day reinvestigation deadline on every consumer dispute (§ 1681i), and specific disclosure and summary-of-rights documents accompanying every report — a dispute reinvestigated on day 32 instead of day 30, or a report furnished without the required FCRA summary-of-rights notice, is exactly the kind of procedural miss that turns into an FTC or CFPB enforcement action, and FCRA litigation over furnishing and disclosure defects is one of the most active areas of consumer-class-action filing in federal court. On top of the federal baseline, several states layer their own consumer reporting agency registration and bonding requirements before a CRA can lawfully issue a single report on a state resident — Massachusetts (M.G.L. c. 93 § 54A) requires registration with the Attorney General and a $5,000 surety bond, and Vermont (9 V.S.A. § 2480e) imposes its own AG registration requirement — and a background check company issuing reports on residents of a state where it hasn't registered is exactly the exposure a state AG consumer-protection sweep is built to find. A small background screening company running thousands of reports a month across dozens of states manages per-dispute reinvestigation-deadline tracking, per-state CRA registration and bond-renewal currency, and FCRA disclosure-document version control with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-dispute reinvestigation countdown timer flagging any dispute approaching the 30-day FCRA deadline; a per-state CRA registration and bond-renewal tracker with a "cleared to report" gate blocking report issuance in any state where registration has lapsed; and an FCRA disclosure-document version tracker confirming the current summary-of-rights notice is attached to every report batch.
**Skill fit:** FCRA's statutory reinvestigation deadline and the handful of state CRA registration/bond regimes are public, structured federal and state regulatory data with a well-defined per-dispute and per-state record requirement, and the dispute-deadline timer plus the state-registration gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a consumer-data-regulated trade most small background screening companies currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a dispute's filing date and its 30-day statutory clock.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the dispute-deadline tracker, the per-state CRA registration/bond tracker, and the disclosure-document version tracker; land the first 5-8 companies through Professional Background Screening Association (PBSA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Professional Background Screening Association (PBSA) member directory and its Background Screening Agency Accreditation Program (BSAAP) applicant network, where small screening companies already juggle per-dispute FCRA deadlines and multi-state CRA registration with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $20/mo per state registered. Average client (8 states) = $249 + $160 = $409/mo. 22-28 companies × $409/mo = **$9,000-11,452/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every consumer dispute opens its own 30-day statutory reinvestigation clock, every state's CRA registration and bond renews on its own independent cycle, and every report batch depends on the current disclosure-document version — a continuous, per-dispute and per-state compliance stream tied directly to the company's report volume.
**Biggest risk:** A dispute reinvestigated past the 30-day FCRA deadline, or a report issued in a state where CRA registration has lapsed, is exactly the finding an FTC/CFPB inquiry or a state AG sweep is built to catch — the dispute-deadline timer and the state-registration gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → dispute-deadline tracker, state-registration gate, and disclosure-version tracker deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through PBSA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 13-16 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 22-28 companies, pushing past $9.00-11.45K/mo.

### 2. Virtual Currency Kiosk (Crypto ATM) Operator State Licensing & Kiosk-Specific Compliance
**Problem:** Companies operating cryptocurrency ATM kiosks must first clear the same baseline any money transmitter faces — FinCEN Money Services Business registration under 31 CFR 1022.380, renewed biennially, plus state money transmitter licensure (via NMLS) in every state a kiosk sits — but a wave of kiosk-specific statutes passed between 2023 and 2025 layers an entirely separate compliance surface on top: Minnesota's crypto kiosk law caps new-customer transactions at $2,000 per day and mandates an on-screen fraud-warning disclosure before every transaction, and California's Digital Financial Assets Law (effective July 2025) requires separate DFPI licensure specifically for digital-asset kiosk businesses, with several other states (Connecticut, Vermont, Colorado) following similar patterns. An operator running dozens of kiosks across multiple states manages per-kiosk transaction-limit software configuration proving compliance with each state's specific dollar cap, per-kiosk on-screen warning-display audits confirming the required disclosure actually renders before a transaction completes, and per-state kiosk-operator registry filings — a kiosk still configured with the old $500 limit after a state raises or lowers its statutory cap, or a machine whose fraud-warning screen silently fails to render after a software update, is exactly the gap a state financial-regulator field audit is built to expose, and consumer-fraud complaints tied to crypto kiosks are a top enforcement priority for several state AG offices. A small kiosk operator running a regional network manages FinCEN renewal, per-state MTL currency, per-kiosk transaction-limit configuration, and warning-display audit trails with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-kiosk transaction-limit configuration tracker cross-checked against each state's current statutory cap with an alert on any mismatch; a per-kiosk warning-display audit log confirming the fraud-disclosure screen renders on a scheduled test cycle; and a FinCEN/state-MTL renewal tracker with a "cleared to operate" gate per kiosk location.
**Skill fit:** State kiosk-specific transaction-limit statutes and FinCEN/NMLS renewal cycles are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-kiosk and per-state record requirement, and the configuration cross-check plus the operate-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "flag the mismatch before it becomes a violation / block the action until the license clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a newly regulated fintech-hardware trade most small kiosk operators currently track on a spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a kiosk's software configuration and the specific state statute governing that location.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the transaction-limit configuration tracker, the warning-display audit log, and the FinCEN/MTL renewal tracker; land the first 5-8 operators through Money Services Business Association (MSBA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Money Services Business Association (MSBA) member directory and state money-transmitter-licensing conference networks, where small kiosk operators already navigate the fast-moving 2023-2025 wave of kiosk-specific state statutes with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $15/mo per kiosk tracked. Average client (15 kiosks) = $299 + $225 = $524/mo. 18-22 operators × $524/mo = **$9,432-11,528/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every kiosk's transaction-limit configuration must be re-verified against its state's statute on its own ongoing cycle, every warning-display audit runs on a recurring schedule, and every FinCEN/MTL credential renews independently — a continuous, per-kiosk compliance stream tied directly to the operator's network size.
**Biggest risk:** A kiosk still configured with an outdated transaction limit after a state statute changes, or a fraud-warning screen that silently fails to render, is exactly the finding a state financial-regulator field audit or a consumer-fraud complaint investigation is built to catch — the configuration cross-check and the warning-display audit log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → configuration tracker, warning-display audit log, and MTL renewal gate deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through MSBA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 operators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 18-22 operators, pushing past $9.43-11.53K/mo.

### 3. Private Adoption & Child-Placing Agency State Licensure & Hague/COA Accreditation Compliance
**Problem:** Private adoption agencies operate under state child-welfare licensure — such as Texas's child-placing agency license under Human Resources Code Chapter 42, administered by HHSC, and California's adoption agency license under the Community Care Licensing Division — that requires every placement's home study to be completed within a state-specific timeline linked to Adoption and Safe Families Act (ASFA) permanency principles, and a home study that lapses past its validity window (commonly one year) before a placement finalizes forces a costly re-study and can delay or jeopardize the placement itself, exactly the finding a state licensing-board complaint investigation targets first. Agencies handling intercountry adoptions must separately hold Council on Accreditation (COA) accreditation — COA is the IAAME-designated accrediting entity under 22 CFR Part 96 implementing the Hague Convention — renewed on a four-year cycle, and a placement processed by an agency whose Hague accreditation has lapsed is not just a licensing violation but can void the adoption's legal recognition in the country of origin. Domestic placements involving a child who may be an "Indian child" under the Indian Child Welfare Act trigger a mandatory notice-and-inquiry requirement to the relevant tribe before placement, and a placement finalized without documented ICWA inquiry is exactly the procedural gap that gets an otherwise-completed adoption overturned on appeal. A small agency running a caseload of concurrent domestic and intercountry placements manages per-case home-study validity tracking, agency-wide COA/Hague accreditation renewal, and per-case ICWA inquiry documentation with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-case home-study validity tracker computing each study's expiration from its completion date with an alert before it lapses ahead of finalization; a COA/Hague accreditation renewal tracker on the four-year cycle with a "cleared to place internationally" gate; and a per-case ICWA inquiry-and-notice documentation tracker confirming the required tribal notice is on file before a domestic placement finalizes.
**Skill fit:** State home-study validity periods, COA's four-year Hague re-accreditation cycle, and ICWA's notice-and-inquiry requirement are public, structured regulatory and accrediting-body data with a well-defined per-case record requirement, and the validity tracker plus the ICWA documentation gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the completion date / confirm the required record is on file before the action proceeds" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a social-services-regulated trade most small adoption agencies currently track on a paper case file with no systematic cross-check between a home study's completion date and its finalization-window expiration.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the home-study validity tracker, the COA/Hague accreditation renewal tracker, and the ICWA inquiry documentation tracker; land the first 5-8 agencies through National Council For Adoption (NCFA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Council For Adoption (NCFA) member directory and state child-placing agency public license-holder lists, where small agencies already juggle per-case home-study windows and Hague re-accreditation with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $329/mo base + $25/mo per active case tracked. Average client (12 active cases) = $329 + $300 = $629/mo. 14-18 agencies × $629/mo = **$8,806-11,322/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every case's home study carries its own finalization-window clock, every intercountry placement depends on the agency's own four-year Hague re-accreditation cycle, and every domestic case opens its own ICWA inquiry-documentation event — a continuous, per-case compliance stream tied directly to the agency's caseload.
**Biggest risk:** A placement finalized on a lapsed home study, or a domestic case closed without documented ICWA inquiry, is exactly the finding a state licensing-board investigation or an appellate challenge to the adoption itself is built to catch — the validity tracker and the ICWA documentation gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → home-study tracker, COA/Hague renewal tracker, and ICWA documentation tracker deployed, first 5-8 agency retainers through NCFA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 8-10 agencies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 14-18 agencies, pushing past $8.81-11.32K/mo.

### 4. Professional Fundraiser & Paid Solicitor State Registration & Contract-Filing Compliance
**Problem:** For-profit companies hired by nonprofits to run donation campaigns — telemarketing solicitation firms, direct-mail fundraising vendors, and event-based paid solicitors — are regulated as a distinct entity type under most state charitable solicitation acts, separate from the charities themselves: California Government Code § 12599 requires a "commercial fundraiser for charitable purposes" to register with the Attorney General's Registry of Charitable Trusts, file each specific campaign contract with the AG before that campaign's solicitation begins, and submit a post-campaign financial accounting (Form CT-503/CT-504) disclosing gross receipts and the percentage retained, while New York Executive Law § 174-b imposes its own Charities Bureau registration and a 10-day pre-campaign contract-filing deadline. A campaign contract filed after solicitation has already started, or a post-campaign accounting submitted past its deadline, is exactly the finding a state AG charities-bureau audit targets first, and several states can suspend a paid solicitor's registration outright for a filing miss, cutting off every nonprofit client relationship at once. Most states also require the paid solicitor itself to carry a surety bond (commonly $25,000) renewed on a fixed cycle, separate from any bonding the nonprofit client carries. A small professional fundraising firm running concurrent campaigns for a rotating roster of nonprofit clients across multiple states manages per-campaign pre-solicitation contract-filing deadlines, per-campaign post-campaign accounting deadlines, and multi-state registration/bond currency with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-campaign contract-filing countdown flagging any upcoming solicitation start date without a filed state contract on record; a post-campaign financial-accounting deadline tracker computing each state's specific due date from the campaign's end date; and a multi-state registration and bond-renewal tracker with a "cleared to solicit" gate per state.
**Skill fit:** State charitable solicitation acts' professional-fundraiser contract-filing and post-campaign accounting deadlines are public, structured state regulatory data with a well-defined per-campaign and per-state record requirement, and the contract-filing countdown plus the accounting-deadline tracker are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / block the action until the filing is on record" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a nonprofit-adjacent regulated trade most small fundraising firms currently track on a shared calendar with no systematic cross-check between a campaign's actual start date and the specific state's pre-solicitation filing window.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the contract-filing tracker, the post-campaign accounting-deadline tracker, and the multi-state registration/bond tracker; land the first 5-8 firms through Association of Fundraising Professionals (AFP) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Association of Fundraising Professionals (AFP) chapter directory and state AG charitable-trust registry public professional-fundraiser lists, where small firms already navigate per-campaign, multi-state contract-filing deadlines with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $30/mo per active campaign tracked. Average client (6 concurrent campaigns) = $249 + $180 = $429/mo. 21-27 firms × $429/mo = **$9,009-11,583/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new campaign opens its own pre-solicitation contract-filing clock and its own post-campaign accounting deadline, and every state registration and bond renews on its own independent cycle — a continuous, per-campaign and per-state compliance stream tied directly to the firm's client roster.
**Biggest risk:** A campaign contract filed after solicitation has already begun, or a post-campaign accounting submitted past its deadline, is exactly the finding a state AG charities-bureau audit is built to catch — and either can trigger a registration suspension that cuts off every client relationship at once — the contract-filing countdown and the accounting-deadline tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → contract-filing tracker, accounting-deadline tracker, and registration/bond gate deployed, first 5-8 firm retainers through AFP outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-15 firms. Replaces-full-time-salary → 21-27 firms, pushing past $9.01-11.58K/mo.

### 5. EMT & Paramedic Training Academy State EMS Approval & CoAEMSP/NREMT Compliance
**Problem:** EMT and paramedic training academies must maintain state EMS Office program approval — such as California EMSA's Education Program Approval process and Texas DSHS EMS certification — renewed on a fixed cycle, and a cohort trained under a lapsed program approval produces graduates whose certification eligibility is immediately in question, exactly the finding a state EMS Office program audit targets first. Paramedic-level programs specifically must separately hold Committee on Accreditation of Educational Programs for the EMS Professions (CoAEMSP) accreditation, without which graduates are ineligible to sit the National Registry of Emergency Medical Technicians (NREMT) certification exam at all — an academy that lets a student progress through a full paramedic cohort only to discover a lapsed CoAEMSP accreditation blocks every graduate's NREMT eligibility has destroyed that cohort's entire value proposition. Every student must also complete a minimum threshold of clinical and field-internship patient contacts and specific skill counts (live intubations, IV starts, medication administrations) set by the National EMS Education Standards before they're eligible to test, and a program unable to produce documented per-student skill-count records during a CoAEMSP site visit or an NREMT eligibility audit faces the same exposure as a program with no accreditation at all. A small academy running overlapping EMT-Basic and paramedic cohorts manages per-cohort state program-approval and CoAEMSP renewal currency, per-student clinical/field patient-contact and skill-count tracking against required minimums, and per-instructor state EMS instructor-credential currency with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A state EMS program-approval and CoAEMSP accreditation renewal tracker with a "cleared to enroll" gate blocking a new cohort start once either lapses; a per-student clinical/field skill-count tracker computing progress against National EMS Education Standards minimums with an NREMT-eligibility gate; and an instructor state-credential currency tracker.
**Skill fit:** State EMS program-approval cycles, CoAEMSP's accreditation renewal schedule, and the National EMS Education Standards' per-skill minimum-count requirements are public, structured regulatory and accrediting-body data with a well-defined per-cohort and per-student record requirement, and the enrollment-eligibility gate plus the skill-count tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / compute progress against the required minimum" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an emergency-medical training trade most small academies currently track on a paper skills-log binder with no systematic cross-check between a student's documented patient contacts and the specific minimum NREMT eligibility requires.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the program-approval/CoAEMSP renewal tracker, the per-student skill-count tracker, and the instructor-credential tracker; land the first 5-8 academies through National Association of EMS Educators (NAEMSE) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Association of EMS Educators (NAEMSE) member directory and state EMS Office public program-approval lists, where small academies already juggle per-student skill-count minimums against CoAEMSP's NREMT-eligibility gate with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $279/mo base + $10/mo per active student tracked. Average client (40 active students) = $279 + $400 = $679/mo. 14-17 academies × $679/mo = **$9,506-11,543/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every cohort's program approval and CoAEMSP accreditation renews on its own recurring cycle, every enrolled student generates a continuous stream of clinical/field skill-count entries against a fixed minimum, and every instructor's own state credential renews independently — a continuous, per-cohort and per-student compliance stream tied directly to the academy's enrollment volume.
**Biggest risk:** A cohort trained under a lapsed CoAEMSP accreditation, or a graduate whose documented skill counts fall short of the NREMT eligibility minimum, is exactly the finding a CoAEMSP site visit or an NREMT eligibility audit is built to catch — the accreditation gate and the skill-count tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → program-approval/CoAEMSP tracker, skill-count tracker, and instructor-credential tracker deployed, first 5-8 academy retainers through NAEMSE outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 9-11 academies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 14-17 academies, pushing past $9.51-11.54K/mo.

## This Session's Pick: #2 for Fastest-Moving Regulatory Target; #4 for Highest-Consequence Single-Point Failure

**Fastest-moving regulatory target:** Idea #2 (crypto ATM/kiosk operators) sits on the freshest regulatory surface of the five — the wave of state kiosk-specific statutes only began landing in 2023-2025, meaning most operators are still tracking a fast-changing patchwork of transaction limits and disclosure requirements on spreadsheets with no purpose-built tool on the market yet. MSBA's concentrated member network gives a well-targeted first 5-8 clients already exposed to this compliance gap.

**Highest-consequence single-point failure:** Idea #4 (professional fundraisers) has the sharpest all-or-nothing enforcement mechanic of the five — a single missed contract-filing deadline in one state can trigger a registration suspension that instantly cuts off every nonprofit client relationship a firm has in that state, not just the one late campaign. AFP's large, well-organized chapter network gives a reachable first 5-8 clients despite the high-stakes compliance surface.

Idea #5 (EMT/paramedic academies) has the most emotionally loaded failure mode of the five — a full cohort of students discovering after graduation that a lapsed CoAEMSP accreditation blocks their NREMT eligibility is a reputational catastrophe no small academy recovers from quickly, making the accreditation-renewal gate an unusually easy, high-urgency pitch.
