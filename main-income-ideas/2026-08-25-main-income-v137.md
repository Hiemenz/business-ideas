### 1. Licensed Card Room & Poker Room Operator State Gambling Control Act, Dealer Work-Permit & Title 31 BSA Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** State-licensed card rooms — non-tribal card clubs operating under a state gambling control act (California's Gambling Control Act, Business & Professions Code §19800 et seq., administered by the Bureau of Gambling Control and the California Gambling Control Commission; Washington's card rooms under the Washington State Gambling Commission) — run on a "no house banking" model where the house collects a seat-rental or rake and is legally barred from wagering against its own players. Every dealer, floor supervisor, and key employee who touches a game must hold an individually renewed state and, in many jurisdictions, local police-department work permit issued only after a background check — a dealer working a table on a lapsed permit is exactly the finding a Bureau of Gambling Control compliance audit is built to catch, and a pattern of lapsed permits can jeopardize the cardroom's own state gambling license. Because a cardroom itself cannot bank most house-banked-style games, it contracts with separately licensed third-party proposition player companies (TPPPs) to supply the bankroll and player-dealer rotation, and a TPPP whose own state registration has gone stale or whose contract with the cardroom has lapsed voids the legal basis for every hand dealt against it that shift. Layered on top, federal law classifies a card club above a revenue threshold as a "casino" for Bank Secrecy Act purposes (31 CFR §1021.100), requiring FinCEN registration, a Currency Transaction Report by Casinos (FinCEN Form 103) for any patron's aggregated cash transactions exceeding $10,000 in a gaming day tracked through a Multiple Transaction Log, and Suspicious Activity Report filings for patterns that look like structuring — a Title 31 examination cycle a state gaming audit checks entirely independently of the cardroom's own state license. A mid-size independent card room running dozens of dealers and floor staff across several concurrent TPPP contracts manages per-employee work-permit renewal, per-TPPP contract/registration currency, and per-shift cash-aggregation logging on a floor manager's binder and a bookkeeper's spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific dealer's permit and a specific TPPP's registration are both actually current before that shift's tables open.

**What to build:** A per-employee work-permit renewal tracker with a "cleared to deal" gate blocking any dealer or floor supervisor from being scheduled once their state or local permit lapses; a TPPP contract and state-registration currency tracker flagging any table running against a TPPP whose registration or contract has gone stale; and a per-shift Title 31 cash-aggregation log computing each patron's running total against the $10,000 CTRC threshold with an automated SAR-review flag for structuring-pattern activity.

**Skill fit:** State gaming-license renewal cycles, TPPP registration rules, and Title 31's cash-aggregation threshold are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-employee and per-transaction triggers, and the "cleared to deal" gate plus the cash-aggregation log are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a non-tribal card-room niche this repo has never addressed and that shares no regulator, license type, or banking mechanism with the tribal gaming, VGT route, or charitable-gaming ideas already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the work-permit tracker with the dealer-eligibility gate, the TPPP contract/registration tracker, and the Title 31 cash-aggregation log with the SAR-flag. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent card rooms.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the California Gaming Association's member directory and the Washington State Gambling Commission's licensed-cardroom roster reach exactly the small independent card rooms currently reconciling per-employee permit renewal and Title 31 cash logs on a floor manager's binder with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349-649/mo per card room depending on table count and TPPP-contract complexity. 18-24 card rooms × $479/mo avg = $8,622-11,496/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every dealer and floor employee's work permit renews on its own independent cycle for as long as they're staffed, every gaming day generates a fresh Title 31 cash-aggregation cycle regardless of how routine the shift, and every additional TPPP relationship the cardroom adds multiplies the number of simultaneously tracked contract-and-registration records — a continuous, staffing-and-shift-driven compliance stream tied directly to the cardroom's table count.

**Biggest risk:** The total number of licensed card rooms nationally is small and geographically concentrated (a few dozen in California, a comparable cluster in Washington, and smaller pockets elsewhere), capping the addressable market. Mitigate by pricing at the higher end given the Title 31 examination stakes and by expanding the customer base to TPPP companies themselves, who face the same registration-currency problem across every cardroom they contract with.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 card rooms ($4.8K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 16 card rooms ($7.7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 18-24 card rooms plus a regulator-facing compliance export card rooms can hand a Bureau of Gambling Control auditor during a routine examination.

### 2. Oil & Gas Well Operator State Commission Permit-to-Drill, Plugging-Bond & Production-Reporting Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Small independent oil and gas well operators running dozens to a few hundred wells hold operator registration with their state's oil and gas regulatory agency — the Texas Railroad Commission's Form P-5 Organization Report, the Oklahoma Corporation Commission, the North Dakota Industrial Commission, and Colorado's Energy and Carbon Management Commission among them — renewed annually and tied to a statewide blanket plugging bond sized to the operator's total well count and depth category; a bond that isn't recalculated and increased as the operator acquires additional wells is exactly the finding a state commission's routine audit is built to catch, since operating even one well beyond the bond's covered count is a licensing violation. Every well requires its own Permit to Drill (Texas's Form W-1) filed before spud, and once a well stops producing it enters a strict inactive-well clock — Texas Statewide Rule 14 requires a well idle 12 or more months to be either returned to service, granted an extension with proof of financial security, or plugged — and a well that quietly ages past its inactive-status deadline with no filed extension or plugging report is exactly the gap that can see the state declare it an "orphan well" and draw directly against the operator's bond. Every producing well must also file a monthly production and disposition report (Texas's Form PR) reconciling volumes produced against volumes sold or transported, since state severance-tax assessments derive directly from this reported volume, and a report filed late or a well omitted from the monthly filing is a routine but consequential enforcement target. A small operator running 40-150 wells across several counties manages per-well permit status, the inactive-well compliance clock, bond-adequacy recalculation against a growing well count, and monthly per-well production-report filing on a pumper's paper route sheet and a bookkeeper's spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific inactive well's extension or plugging report is actually on file before the deadline arrives.

**What to build:** A per-well Permit-to-Drill and inactive-status clock tracker with a plugging/extension deadline gate flagging any well approaching its 12-month inactive threshold; a bond-adequacy calculator recomputing the operator's required statewide bond against current well count and depth category; and a monthly production-report filing tracker reconciling reported volumes against a well-by-well checklist across the operator's full portfolio.

**Skill fit:** State oil and gas commission filing forms, inactive-well deadlines, and bond-sizing formulas are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-well triggers, and the inactive-well clock plus the bond-adequacy calculator are the same deterministic "compute the deadline/threshold from reported activity" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a well-operator compliance niche entirely distinct from the mineral-rights royalty-data aggregation and mineral-rights brokerage tools already in this repo, neither of which files a single document with a state oil and gas commission on an operator's own behalf.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the inactive-status clock tracker with the plugging/extension gate, the bond-adequacy calculator, and the monthly production-report tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small independent operators.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Texas Alliance of Energy Producers and the Panhandle Producers and Royalty Owners Association member directories reach exactly the small independent operators currently tracking inactive-well deadlines and monthly production filings across a growing well count on a pumper's paper route sheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per operator depending on well count and inactive-well volume. 22-28 operators × $429/mo avg = $9,438-12,012/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every well generates its own monthly production-report obligation for as long as it produces, every well that goes idle opens its own independent inactive-well clock, and every new well the operator acquires or drills both expands the bond-adequacy calculation and adds another permit and reporting record to track — a continuous, well-count-driven compliance stream tied directly to the operator's portfolio growth.

**Biggest risk:** A missed plugging deadline that lets a well become an orphan well drawn against the state's bond is a severe financial and reputational event for a small operator. Mitigate by making the inactive-well clock a hard, unmissable alert well before the 12-month threshold and by qualifying leads toward operators running enough wells that the staggered deadline calendar genuinely outgrows a paper route sheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 operators ($5.1K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 20 operators ($8.6K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 22-28 operators plus a state-commission-facing compliance export operators can produce during a routine well-status audit.

### 3. Marina Operator Army Corps Section 10 Permit, Submerged-Lands Lease & Clean Vessel Act Pumpout Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Marina operators maintaining docks, piers, and mooring fields in navigable waters hold a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Section 10 permit under the Rivers and Harbors Act (33 U.S.C. §403) for every structure extending into navigable water, and separately a state submerged/aquatic-lands lease from the state's land or natural-resources department for the water-bottom acreage the marina's slips actually occupy — a lease that runs on its own periodic rent-reappraisal and renewal cycle independent of the Corps permit, and a marina whose physical footprint has quietly expanded beyond its leased boundary (a common finding when new slips or a fuel dock are added) is exactly the gap a state lands-department site inspection is built to catch, with consequences ranging from a back-rent assessment to a forced removal of unpermitted structures. Marinas offering fuel sales must separately register their fuel dock's underground or aboveground storage tanks under EPA and state UST/AST rules, maintaining release-detection monitoring and tank/line tightness testing on a fixed interval, since a lapsed tank test is both an environmental-enforcement exposure and a fuel-dock insurance-coverage risk. Under the Clean Vessel Act (33 U.S.C. §1322) and its state grant programs, marinas above a state-set slip-count threshold must provide and maintain a functioning sewage pumpout station and report its usage and functional status to the state boating agency on a fixed reporting cycle — a marina that lets its pumpout station sit broken for months is both a discharge-prevention gap and grounds for losing the state grant funding that built the station in the first place. A marina running several hundred wet and dry slips manages Section 10 permit compliance across every structure, submerged-lands lease renewal and footprint reconciliation, fuel-dock UST/AST testing intervals, and pumpout-station functionality reporting on a dockmaster's binder, with no systematic cross-check confirming the marina's actual physical footprint still matches its leased and permitted boundary before the next lease-renewal or Corps inspection.

**What to build:** A Section 10 permit and structure-inventory tracker mapping every permitted dock and pier against the marina's current physical layout; a submerged-lands lease renewal and footprint-reconciliation tracker flagging any structure built beyond the leased boundary; a fuel-dock UST/AST testing-interval tracker; and a Clean Vessel Act pumpout-station functionality and usage-reporting tracker tied to the state's fixed reporting cycle.

**Skill fit:** Corps permit records, state submerged-lands lease terms, and UST testing intervals are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-structure and per-tank triggers, and the footprint-reconciliation and testing-interval trackers are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a marina-operator niche this repo has never addressed and that shares no regulator, permit type, or leasehold mechanism with the vessel-documentation service or used-oil-collection-route ideas already in this repo, both of which reference marinas only incidentally.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the Section 10 permit/structure tracker, the submerged-lands lease and footprint-reconciliation tracker, and the fuel-dock UST/AST and pumpout-reporting trackers. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent marinas.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Association of Marina Industries (AMI) member directory and its annual conference reach exactly the small independent marinas currently reconciling lease boundaries and pumpout-reporting deadlines on a dockmaster's binder with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-499/mo per marina depending on slip count and fuel-dock complexity. 26-34 marinas × $359/mo avg = $9,334-12,206/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every submerged-lands lease renews on its own periodic cycle for as long as the marina operates, every fuel-dock tank recurs on its own testing interval, and every new slip or structure the marina adds both reopens the Section 10 permit question and expands the footprint the lease has to cover — a continuous, expansion-and-renewal-driven compliance stream tied directly to the marina's physical growth.

**Biggest risk:** A very small marina with no fuel dock and a stable, long-unchanged footprint has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward fuel-dock marinas with growing slip counts and recent expansion history, where the footprint-reconciliation-and-testing calendar genuinely outgrows a dockmaster's binder.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 marinas ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 20 marinas ($7.2K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 26-34 marinas plus a lands-department-facing footprint-reconciliation export marinas can produce ahead of a lease-renewal site inspection.

### 4. RCRA-Licensed Hazardous Waste Transporter EPA ID, DOT Hazmat Registration & Manifest Chain-of-Custody Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Companies that physically haul RCRA-regulated hazardous waste — industrial solvents, chemical byproducts, contaminated debris — between generator sites and permitted treatment/storage/disposal facilities (TSDFs) must hold their own EPA hazardous-waste transporter identification number under 40 CFR Part 263, a registration entirely distinct from a generator's own EPA ID or a TSDF's own permit, plus an annual DOT/PHMSA hazmat carrier registration under 49 CFR Part 107 Subpart G required of any carrier moving hazardous materials above defined quantity thresholds. Every driver must hold a CDL with a hazmat endorsement backed by a TSA security threat assessment (49 CFR §1572) and complete DOT's function-specific and safety hazmat training cycle under 49 CFR Part 172 Subpart H, renewed at least every three years — a driver dispatched on a load with a lapsed hazmat endorsement or an expired training cycle is exactly the finding a roadside DOT inspection or a post-incident investigation is built to catch. The transporter's core legal role is signing and retaining its copy of the EPA Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest, filed electronically through EPA's e-Manifest system, for every load — confirming the waste received from the generator matches what's delivered to the TSDF — and a transporter who can't produce a signed manifest accounting for a specific load's full chain of custody is exactly the gap an EPA RCRA inspection targets, while a manifest discrepancy the transporter fails to report to the generator and destination facility within the regulatory window is an independently citable violation. A small hazardous-waste hauling company running several trucks and drivers across a multi-state generator-to-TSDF network manages EPA transporter ID currency, per-driver hazmat endorsement/training-cycle tracking, and per-load manifest signing-and-reconciliation on a dispatcher's binder and a filing cabinet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific load's manifest actually reconciles before the driver is dispatched on the next run.

**What to build:** An EPA transporter ID and DOT hazmat carrier registration renewal tracker; a per-driver hazmat endorsement/TSA threat-assessment and Subpart H training-cycle tracker with a dispatch-eligibility gate blocking any driver from being assigned a hazmat load once a credential lapses; and a per-load manifest signing-and-reconciliation tracker flagging any load lacking a matched generator-to-TSDF signature set within the expected turnaround window.

**Skill fit:** 40 CFR Part 263's transporter requirements and DOT's hazmat carrier-registration and training cycles are public, structured federal regulatory data with concrete per-driver and per-load triggers, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the manifest-reconciliation tracker are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a RCRA industrial-waste transporter niche entirely distinct from the small-business hazardous-waste-generator compliance coordination service and the regulated medical-waste transporter idea already in this repo, neither of which holds an EPA transporter ID or signs a RCRA manifest as the carrier of record.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the EPA/DOT registration tracker, the driver dispatch-eligibility gate, and the manifest reconciliation tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent hazardous-waste hauling companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Hazardous Materials Advisory Council and National Tank Truck Carriers member directories reach exactly the small independent transporters currently reconciling per-load manifests and driver hazmat-endorsement renewal on a dispatcher's binder with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per transporter depending on truck/driver count and load volume. 22-28 companies × $429/mo avg = $9,438-12,012/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every load hauled generates its own manifest-reconciliation event regardless of how routine the run, every driver's hazmat endorsement and training cycle renews on its own independent clock, and every new truck or driver the company adds multiplies the number of simultaneously tracked credential-and-manifest records — a continuous, load-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the transporter's fleet growth.

**Biggest risk:** A very small transporter running a single truck on a fixed local route has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for transporters running multiple trucks across a multi-state generator-to-TSDF network, where the staggered manifest-and-credential calendar genuinely outgrows a dispatcher's binder.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 transporters ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 16 transporters ($6.9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 22-28 companies plus a TSDF-facing manifest-reconciliation export transporters can hand a generator client during its own RCRA audit.

### 5. Construction & Demolition (C&D) Debris Recycling Facility Operator State Processing-Permit & Asbestos Pre-Acceptance Screening Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Facilities that receive, sort, and process construction and demolition debris — concrete, wood, drywall, metal, and mixed loads — for material recovery hold a state solid-waste "C&D debris processing" or "recycling facility" permit, a distinct and typically lighter-weight tier than a landfill's Subtitle D disposal-site permit since the facility never buries waste on-site, but the permit still carries its own operating-plan approval, a fire-prevention plan for the combustible wood-and-mixed-debris piles state fire marshals inspect specifically, and a stormwater pollution-prevention plan tied to the facility's footprint. Because demolition debris frequently originates from structures that may contain asbestos, most state C&D facility permits require the operator to obtain and retain a generator's asbestos survey or NESHAP Subpart M pre-demolition notification confirmation before accepting a load from a covered demolition project — a facility that accepts and processes debris without that documentation on file is exposed the moment a state inspector traces a load back to an undocumented demolition site, since the facility itself becomes a party to the asbestos-handling violation rather than merely an innocent downstream recipient. A growing number of state and municipal C&D ordinances also require registered facilities to report tonnage received, diverted (recycled or reused), and residual (landfilled) on a periodic basis — sometimes tied to a minimum diversion rate the facility must maintain to keep its registration — since jurisdictions increasingly rely on this reported diversion data for their own state recycling-goal compliance, and commercial clients depend on the same data for green-building (LEED) certification documentation. A small C&D recycling facility processing dozens of incoming loads a day manages permit/operating-plan currency, per-load asbestos-documentation verification against the source demolition project, and periodic tonnage/diversion-rate reporting on a scale-house paper ticket, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific incoming load's asbestos documentation is actually on file before it's tipped onto the sort line.

**What to build:** A state processing-facility permit and operating-plan renewal tracker with fire-prevention-plan and stormwater-plan documentation; a per-load asbestos pre-acceptance documentation tracker with a "cleared to accept" gate tied to the source project's NESHAP notification or survey; and a tonnage/diversion-rate reporting tracker computing the facility's running diversion percentage against its permit or ordinance minimum ahead of each periodic filing.

**Skill fit:** State C&D facility permit requirements and NESHAP's pre-demolition notification framework are public, structured regulatory data with a concrete per-load documentation gate, and the "cleared to accept" gate plus the diversion-rate calculator are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a materials-recovery-facility niche explicitly distinct from the Municipal Solid Waste Landfill & Transfer Station Operator idea and the Scrap Metal & Secondhand Dealer idea already in this repo, neither of which processes bulk mixed C&D debris under a per-load asbestos-screening gate.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the permit/operating-plan tracker, the per-load asbestos "cleared to accept" gate, and the tonnage/diversion-rate reporting tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent C&D recycling facilities.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Construction & Demolition Recycling Association (CDRA) member directory and its annual conference reach exactly the small independent facilities currently reconciling per-load asbestos documentation and diversion-rate reporting on a scale-house paper ticket with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per facility depending on daily load volume and reporting-jurisdiction count. 20-26 facilities × $429/mo avg = $8,580-11,154/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every incoming load generates its own asbestos-documentation-verification event regardless of how routine the source project, every periodic diversion-rate report recurs on its own filing cycle for as long as the facility operates, and every new municipal C&D ordinance the facility becomes subject to adds another reporting record to the same compliance surface — a continuous, load-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the facility's throughput.

**Biggest risk:** A facility that accepts an undocumented load and is later traced back to an asbestos-contaminated demolition site faces an enforcement exposure well beyond a typical missed filing. Mitigate by making the per-load asbestos "cleared to accept" gate a hard block rather than an advisory flag, and by qualifying leads toward facilities running enough daily volume that the documentation-and-reporting calendar genuinely outgrows a scale-house paper ticket.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 facilities ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 16 facilities ($6.9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 20-26 facilities plus a municipality-facing diversion-rate export facilities can produce ahead of their periodic ordinance filing.

RCRA-licensed hazardous waste transporter compliance stands out because the transporter's signed manifest is the literal legal proof that a load of hazardous waste went where it was supposed to go — a transporter who can produce a clean, gate-enforced chain-of-custody record for every load isn't offering a convenience, it's offering the exact document an EPA inspector or a generator's own RCRA audit asks for first. HMAC's and NTTC's concentrated carrier membership gives direct, credible access to exactly the small hauling companies who feel this documentation burden on every single run.

Oil & gas well operator compliance has the deepest natural filing cadence in this session: every producing well generates its own monthly production report for as long as it produces, and every well that goes idle opens an independent 12-month countdown toward plugging or extension — a compliance calendar that grows automatically with the operator's well count rather than waiting for the builder to manufacture recurring touchpoints, and the concentrated, well-organized state producers' associations make the first cohort of clients unusually easy to reach directly.
