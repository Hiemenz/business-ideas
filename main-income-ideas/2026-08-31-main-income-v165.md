## Ideas

### 1. State-Licensed Sports Wagering & Daily Fantasy Sports (DFS) Operator License & Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Since *Murphy v. NCAA* struck down PASPA in 2018, over 30 states now license sports wagering, each through its own gaming commission (Michigan Gaming Control Board, Colorado Division of Gaming, Pennsylvania Gaming Control Board, and similar bodies elsewhere) with its own master operator license, "key employee" and "principal" individual licenses, and vendor/supplier licenses for any platform or data provider the operator uses — and daily fantasy sports operates under a *separate* state statute in many states (New York's Interactive Fantasy Sports law is the model several others copied) requiring its own registration entirely apart from sports-wagering licensure. Every wager or contest entry must be geofenced to confirm the bettor is physically inside a licensed state at the moment of the bet — a geolocation failure that lets an out-of-state bet through is a licensing violation regulators actively audit for — and every payout above regulatory thresholds must be checked against the state's self-exclusion registry before it's processed, since paying out a self-excluded gambler is one of the most common findings behind a state enforcement action and fine. Layered on top: Bank Secrecy Act-style suspicious activity and currency transaction reporting on large or unusual payouts, integrity-monitoring reports to sports governing bodies on suspicious betting patterns, and state-specific gross gaming revenue (GGR) tax remittance calculated differently in nearly every licensed state. A small or regional operator — a niche single-sport DFS platform, a licensed sportsbook supplier, or a tribal-casino sports-wagering partner licensed in a handful of states — manages license renewal dates, self-exclusion-list currency, and per-state tax filing deadlines across a spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific key employee's license hasn't lapsed before they're allowed to keep working.

**What to build:** A per-state license portfolio tracker (master license, key-employee/principal licenses, vendor/supplier licenses) with renewal alerts; a self-exclusion-list refresh reminder tied to each state's registry update cadence; a GGR tax-filing calendar computing each state's filing deadline and rate; and a "cleared to operate in this state" gate that flags any state where a required license has lapsed.

**Skill fit:** State gaming-commission license rosters, self-exclusion registry update schedules, and GGR tax rate tables are public, structured regulatory data spread across 30+ separate state gaming-commission websites — exactly the kind of scattered multi-jurisdiction dataset a developer can assemble into one dashboard faster than a compliance officer can track it by hand.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends: a multi-state license renewal tracker, a self-exclusion-registry-refresh reminder system, and a GGR tax-filing deadline calculator. First 5-8 clients from direct outreach to small/regional DFS platforms and licensed sportsbook suppliers.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the iGaming trade press and organizations like iDEA Growth and the National Council of Legislators from Gaming States (NCLGS) hold conferences where small and mid-size licensed operators and suppliers actively discuss the multi-state licensing burden.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $400/mo average retainer (scaling with number of states licensed in) × 25 operators/suppliers = $10,000/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new state a DFS or sportsbook operator enters reopens the entire license-application-and-renewal cycle from scratch, self-exclusion registries update on their own independent cadence per state, and GGR tax filings recur monthly or quarterly regardless of betting volume — a continuous, expansion-driven compliance stream.

**Biggest risk:** The largest operators (DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM) have in-house compliance teams and will never be a customer — this only works selling to the long tail of smaller licensed operators, suppliers, and tribal-casino sports-wagering partners. Mitigate by explicitly positioning against that smaller segment from the first sales conversation.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 clients ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 25 clients ($10K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary by adding integrity-monitoring report generation as a premium tier.

### 2. Shared Micromobility (E-Scooter/E-Bike Fleet) Operator City-Permit & MDS Data-Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Cities that permit shared e-scooter and e-bike fleets do it through a competitive, often annually re-bid permit process administered by the city transportation department, typically capping each operator to a fixed device count and requiring the operator to report every trip's start/end location and route in real time to the city under the Mobility Data Specification (MDS) standard — a data feed cities actively audit, since a gap in MDS reporting is grounds for permit suspension. Permits also carry minimum-deployment or "equity" requirements — a fixed percentage of the fleet must be deployed in specified underserved neighborhoods by a set time each morning — general liability insurance minimums the operator must keep current with the city on file, geofenced parking-corral rules that trigger fines when a device is left outside a designated corral, and incident/injury reporting to the city within a strict window after any reported collision. A regional operator running fleets across several mid-size cities (the markets Lime, Bird, and Spin's corporate teams don't bother contesting) juggles a different permit renewal date, device cap, equity-zone map, and MDS reporting cadence per city with no single view confirming every city's permit is actually current and every equity-zone requirement is being met before the morning deployment window closes.

**What to build:** A per-city permit tracker (device cap, renewal date, insurance-certificate-on-file status) with renewal alerts; an equity-zone deployment checker cross-referencing the morning fleet-rebalancing plan against each city's required underserved-neighborhood percentage; and an MDS data-feed health monitor flagging any gap in the city's real-time trip data before it becomes an audit finding.

**Skill fit:** The Mobility Data Specification is an open, published data standard, and city permit terms (device caps, equity-zone maps, insurance minimums) are public procurement documents — a developer can build the MDS feed integration and the permit-tracking dashboard as the same kind of structured-data problem this series solves repeatedly.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends: an MDS feed health-check dashboard, a per-city permit renewal tracker, and an equity-zone deployment checker. First 5-8 clients from direct outreach to regional micromobility operators bidding on secondary-market city contracts.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the North American Bikeshare and Scootershare Association (NABSA) is the direct trade association for this industry and holds an annual conference plus active regional-operator communication channels where smaller fleet operators openly discuss the permit-compliance burden of operating in multiple cities.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo average per operator account (scaling with number of cities operated in) × 30 operators = $10,470/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every city permit renews (or gets re-bid) on its own independent annual cycle, MDS reporting runs continuously as long as the fleet operates, and equity-zone deployment is checked every single morning — a daily-and-annual compliance rhythm that scales with every new city the operator wins.

**Biggest risk:** A city could revoke micromobility permits entirely (several have) over safety or sidewalk-clutter complaints, eliminating clients in that market overnight. Mitigate by pricing per-city rather than a flat platform fee, so revenue naturally follows wherever operators still hold active permits.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 clients ($4.2K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 30 clients ($10.5K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary by adding an incident-reporting module sold as a premium add-on.

### 3. Telehealth/Tele-Triage Veterinary Practice Multi-State VCPR & Licensure Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Most state veterinary practice acts require an established veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) — historically only creatable through an in-person exam — before a vet can legally diagnose a condition or prescribe treatment, and only a growing minority of states, following AVMA model-act updates, now permit a VCPR to be established through telehealth alone; a telehealth vet treating clients nationally must know, state by state, whether the state where the *animal* physically sits allows a telehealth-only VCPR or requires a prior in-person exam by any vet before telehealth follow-up is legal. Because the governing rule follows the animal's location rather than the vet's, a telehealth practice must hold an individual veterinary license in every state it takes appointments from — the American Association of Veterinary State Boards' PAVE program eases reciprocity but doesn't eliminate the need to hold and renew a license per state — and any prescribing of a federally controlled substance is additionally restricted by the Ryan Haight Act's in-person-exam requirement, layered on top of the state-by-state VCPR rules. A telehealth vet practice running appointments across a dozen or more states manages license renewal dates, a state-by-state "telehealth VCPR allowed: yes/no/conditional" reference table, and controlled-substance prescribing eligibility per state on a spreadsheet that's rarely updated the moment a state's rule changes.

**What to build:** A per-state veterinary license renewal tracker; a "telehealth VCPR eligibility" reference table (allowed / requires prior in-person exam / not allowed) checked automatically against the client's state before an appointment is booked; and a controlled-substance prescribing eligibility flag cross-referencing Ryan Haight Act exceptions by state.

**Skill fit:** State veterinary board telehealth rules and PAVE reciprocity data are public but scattered across 50+ separate board websites and updated piecemeal as states pass new telehealth statutes — a maintained, always-current reference table is exactly the kind of "50-state matrix nobody keeps clean" problem this series is built to solve.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends: the license renewal tracker, the telehealth-VCPR eligibility table with an appointment-booking gate, and the controlled-substance flag. First 5-8 clients from telehealth veterinary practices already operating across multiple states.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Veterinary Virtual Care Association (VVCA) is the dedicated trade association for telehealth veterinary practices and runs an active member directory and annual summit reaching exactly this audience.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $350/mo average per practice (scaling with number of states licensed in) × 30 practices = $10,500/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new state a telehealth practice expands into reopens the license-application cycle, and state telehealth-VCPR rules keep changing as legislatures catch up to the practice model — a moving-target reference table that needs continuous maintenance, not a set-and-forget compliance calendar.

**Biggest risk:** A telehealth vet practice operating in only 2-3 states has a low-stakes tracking need barely worth a subscription. Mitigate by targeting practices actively expanding their state footprint, where the multi-state matrix genuinely outgrows a spreadsheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 practices ($4.2K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 30 practices ($10.5K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary by adding a client-facing "is telehealth available in your state" eligibility widget practices can embed on their own booking page.

### 4. Third-Party Reprocessor of Single-Use Medical Devices FDA 510(k) & Quality System Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** A company that reprocesses another manufacturer's single-use medical device (pulse oximeter sensors, certain catheters, compression sleeves) for resale isn't just running a cleaning operation — under FDA's 2000 reprocessing guidance, the reprocessor becomes the legal "manufacturer" of record for that device and must obtain its own 510(k) premarket clearance for every distinct device model, demonstrating through validation testing that the reprocessing cycle achieves adequate cleaning and sterilization without degrading the device's original function or safety profile — a device reprocessed and sold without its own cleared 510(k) is exactly the finding behind FDA warning letters this industry has drawn. On top of the per-model clearance, the reprocessor must maintain FDA facility registration and device listing, operate under the full Quality System Regulation (21 CFR Part 820) as if manufacturing the device from scratch, file Medical Device Reports (MDRs) on any adverse event tied to a reprocessed unit, and label every reprocessed device with the reprocessor's own name and a clear "reprocessed" designation distinguishing it from the original manufacturer's product. A small reprocessor running several device-model lines manages per-model 510(k) clearance status, QSR audit-readiness documentation, and MDR filing deadlines on a mix of a regulatory consultant's periodic memo and a production log, with no systematic gate confirming a specific device model is actually cleared to ship before a batch goes out the door.

**What to build:** A per-device-model 510(k) clearance status tracker gating any model from being marked "cleared to ship" until FDA clearance is confirmed; a Quality System Regulation audit-readiness checklist mapped to 21 CFR Part 820's design-control, validation, and CAPA requirements; and an MDR filing-deadline tracker triggered the moment an adverse event is logged against a specific reprocessed device model.

**Skill fit:** FDA's 510(k) database and QSR structure are public, well-documented regulatory frameworks, and the per-model "cleared to ship" gate is the same deterministic release-hold pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a reprocessing-specific compliance surface with real patient-safety stakes.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends: the 510(k) clearance tracker with a ship gate, the QSR audit-readiness checklist, and the MDR deadline tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small independent SUD reprocessors.

**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — the Association of Medical Device Reprocessors (AMDR) is the dedicated trade association for this small, tight-knit industry and its member directory reaches nearly every company operating in this space in the US.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $700/mo average per facility (scaling with number of device models under active clearance) × 18 facilities = $12,600/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new device model a reprocessor adds reopens the 510(k) clearance cycle from scratch, QSR audit-readiness never expires since FDA can inspect at any time, and any adverse event anywhere in the field can trigger a fresh MDR filing deadline — a continuous, product-line-growth-driven compliance stream.

**Biggest risk:** This is a small industry — likely fewer than 40-50 companies in the US hold active third-party reprocessor registrations. Mitigate by pricing high per client from the start (this industry's FDA-clearance stakes justify $500-900+/mo) rather than counting on high client volume.

**Growth path:** Side project → 8 facilities ($5.6K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 18 facilities ($12.6K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary by adding a validation-testing-documentation module for new 510(k) submissions.

### 5. Sewer Lateral Inspection & Point-of-Sale Compliance Certification Contractor Tracker

**Problem:** A growing number of cities and sewer districts — Oakland's Sewer Lateral Ordinance and the East Bay Municipal Utility District's private-lateral program being the best-known models — require a certified CCTV video inspection of the private sewer lateral (the pipe connecting a property to the public sewer main) before a property sale can close, to catch cracked or root-infiltrated laterals contributing inflow and infiltration to the public system. A contractor performing these inspections must often hold a specific district-issued lateral-inspector certification on top of a standard plumbing contractor license, perform the CCTV inspection to the district's exact technical standard, and file a certificate of compliance with the sewer authority within a statutory window of the closing date — or, if the lateral fails, coordinate a compliant repair plan with a follow-up re-inspection deadline before the district will issue final clearance, sometimes against an escrow holdback that penalizes a missed deadline. A contractor running inspections across several jurisdictions each with a different filing deadline, technical standard, and certification requirement manages closing-date deadlines, certificate filing status, and failed-lateral repair-and-reinspection timelines on a paper job folder per property, with no systematic alert before a looming closing date turns into a rushed or missed certificate filing.

**What to build:** A per-jurisdiction technical-standard and filing-deadline reference table; a per-property job tracker counting down to the closing date with an automatic alert before the filing window closes; and a failed-lateral repair-and-reinspection pipeline tracking each property from failed inspection through repair completion to final clearance certificate.

**Skill fit:** Municipal sewer-lateral ordinances and their filing deadlines are public documents scattered across dozens of individual city and sewer-district websites — assembling them into one clean, always-current reference table is a straightforward data-aggregation problem for a developer, and directly prevents the missed-deadline failure mode that costs a contractor a closing-day emergency call.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends: the jurisdiction reference table, the closing-date countdown tracker, and the failed-lateral repair pipeline. First 5-8 clients from plumbing contractors already performing point-of-sale lateral inspections in ordinance-covered cities.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Association of Sewer Service Companies (NASSCO) is the direct trade association for sewer inspection and rehabilitation contractors and maintains an active certified-contractor directory and regional chapter meetings.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $175/mo average per contractor × 70 contractors = $12,250/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every property sale in an ordinance-covered jurisdiction generates a fresh closing-date deadline, and a growing number of cities are adopting point-of-sale lateral ordinances as aging sewer infrastructure becomes a bigger municipal priority — a continuous, transaction-volume-driven compliance stream with an expanding jurisdiction footprint.

**Biggest risk:** Point-of-sale lateral ordinances currently exist in a limited number of cities and districts, mostly in older infrastructure regions (the Bay Area, parts of the Midwest and Northeast). Mitigate by starting in the handful of metro areas with mature ordinances and expanding as more districts adopt similar rules.

**Growth path:** Side project → 25 contractors ($4.4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 70 contractors ($12.3K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary by adding a homeowner-facing pre-listing inspection scheduler contractors can offer real estate agents directly.
