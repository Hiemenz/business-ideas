### 1. Professional Fiduciary Guardian & Conservator State Probate Licensure & Court Accounting Compliance
**Problem:** Professional fiduciaries who serve as court-appointed guardians of the person and conservators/guardians of the estate operate under state licensing regimes — California's Professional Fiduciaries Act (Business & Professions Code Division 3, Chapter 6, administered by the Professional Fiduciaries Bureau), Washington's Certified Professional Guardian program, and the Center for Guardianship Certification's National Certified Guardian (NCG) credential among them — that require a criminal background check, a surety bond sized to the assets under management, and periodic continuing-education hours (commonly 30 CEUs every two years) to keep the license current. The core, non-negotiable obligation is the probate accounting: for every single ward or conservatee, the fiduciary must file a full accounting with the appointing court — typically annual, sometimes biennial — itemizing every receipt and disbursement against the ward's entire estate, not just a single benefit stream, and an accounting filed late, or one where the ledger doesn't reconcile to the ward's actual bank and investment statements, is exactly the finding a probate court's accounting examiner or a family member's objection is built to catch, with consequences ranging from a court citation to removal as fiduciary and personal surcharge liability for any unexplained shortfall. Every ward also has a separate court-review date for a status report on their personal well-being, distinct from the financial accounting, and a fiduciary managing a caseload of a dozen or more wards across different counties and different court review calendars has no single view of which accounting or status report is coming due next. A small professional fiduciary practice running several dozen wards manages per-ward accounting deadlines, receipt/disbursement ledger reconciliation, bond-coverage currency against total assets under management, and the fiduciary's own license/CEU renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a spreadsheet and a filing cabinet of bank statements.
**What to build:** A per-ward probate accounting deadline tracker computing each court's specific due date from the appointment or last-accounting date; a receipts-and-disbursements ledger reconciliation tool flagging any ward's book balance that doesn't match their actual bank/investment statement before an accounting is filed; a bond-coverage tracker cross-checked against total assets currently under management; and a license/CEU renewal tracker for the fiduciary's own credential.
**Skill fit:** State probate court accounting-cycle rules and certifying-body CEU requirements are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-ward record requirement, and the accounting-deadline tracker plus the ledger-reconciliation tool are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the appointment date / cross-check two records that must match before the filing goes out" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a court-supervised fiduciary trade most small practices currently track on a spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a specific ward's ledger and their actual bank balance before the accounting is due.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: the accounting deadline tracker, the ledger reconciliation tool, and the bond/CEU renewal tracker. First 5-8 clients from professional fiduciaries already managing multiple wards across county probate courts.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the National Guardianship Association (NGA) member directory and state professional fiduciary associations such as California's Professional Fiduciaries Association of California (PFAC), where practitioners already juggle staggered per-ward accounting deadlines across multiple courts with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per ward tracked. Average client (20 wards) = $249 + $300 = $549/mo. 18-22 fiduciary practices × $549/mo = **$9,882-12,078/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every ward's probate accounting recurs on its own independent court-review cycle for as long as the fiduciary holds the appointment, and a growing caseload adds accounting deadlines linearly — a continuous, per-ward compliance stream tied directly to the fiduciary's caseload rather than a one-time filing.
**Biggest risk:** An accounting filed late, or one whose ledger doesn't reconcile to the ward's actual bank statements, is exactly the finding a probate court examiner or an objecting family member is built to catch — and can trigger removal and personal surcharge liability — the accounting-deadline tracker and the ledger-reconciliation tool are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → accounting tracker, ledger reconciliation, and bond/CEU tracker deployed, first 5-8 fiduciary retainers through NGA/PFAC outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 practices. Replaces-full-time-salary → 18-22 practices, pushing past $9.88-12.08K/mo.

### 2. Opioid Treatment Program (OTP) SAMHSA 42 CFR Part 8 Certification & DEA Take-Home Dosing Compliance
**Problem:** Opioid treatment programs dispensing methadone or buprenorphine for medication-assisted treatment operate under SAMHSA certification granted under 42 CFR Part 8, which must be renewed on a fixed cycle alongside accreditation from a SAMHSA-approved accrediting body (CARF, the Joint Commission, or COA), and a program-specific DEA registration authorizing the OTP to dispense narcotic treatment medication — a lapsed certification, accreditation, or DEA registration doesn't just create a paperwork gap, it strips the program's legal authority to dispense to every enrolled patient simultaneously. The clinical core of daily operations is take-home dosing eligibility: 42 CFR §8.12(i)(2) sets out specific clinical criteria — absence of recent drug abuse, regularity of clinic attendance, absence of behavioral problems, stability of the patient's home environment, and others — that must all be met and documented before a patient can be authorized for an unsupervised take-home dose, and a patient given take-home doses without a documented eligibility determination is exactly the finding a SAMHSA or DEA diversion-control audit is built to catch. Every patient must also undergo a urine drug screen on a state- and program-defined minimum cadence, and a screen result that's overdue or shows a positive finding must be reflected in that patient's take-home eligibility before their next dose is dispensed — a cross-check most small OTPs currently perform by a nurse manually flipping through paper charts before each dosing window. A small independent OTP serving a rotating patient census manages per-patient take-home-eligibility determination, drug-screen-cadence currency, and program-level SAMHSA/accreditation/DEA renewal with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-patient take-home-dose eligibility calculator cross-referencing time-in-treatment and the 42 CFR §8.12 clinical criteria before a take-home allotment can be authorized; a urine-drug-screen cadence tracker flagging any patient overdue for their next required screen; and a program-level SAMHSA certification, accrediting-body, and DEA registration renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** 42 CFR Part 8's take-home eligibility criteria and SAMHSA/DEA renewal cycles are public, structured federal regulatory data with a well-defined per-patient record requirement, and the eligibility calculator plus the drug-screen tracker are the same deterministic "compute eligibility from the documented criteria / flag the gap before the action proceeds" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a narrowly regulated, high-stakes controlled-substance-dispensing trade most small OTPs currently track on paper charts with no systematic cross-check between a patient's drug-screen currency and their take-home authorization.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: the take-home eligibility calculator, the drug-screen cadence tracker, and the SAMHSA/DEA/accreditation renewal tracker. First 5-8 clinics from independent OTPs already juggling per-patient eligibility determinations by hand.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the American Association for the Treatment of Opioid Dependence (AATOD) member directory and state OTP provider association meetings, where clinic directors already manage per-patient take-home eligibility and drug-screen cadence with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $3/mo per enrolled patient tracked. Average client (200 patients) = $299 + $600 = $899/mo. 12-15 clinics × $899/mo = **$10,788-13,485/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every patient's take-home eligibility is re-evaluated on an ongoing basis tied to their drug-screen results and clinic attendance, and every new patient enrollment opens its own eligibility-tracking record — a continuous, per-patient compliance stream tied directly to the program's daily dosing census.
**Biggest risk:** A take-home dose authorized without a documented eligibility determination, or a patient dosed past their drug-screen due date, is exactly the finding a SAMHSA or DEA diversion-control audit is built to catch — and can jeopardize the program's entire dispensing authority — the eligibility calculator and the drug-screen tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → eligibility calculator, drug-screen tracker, and renewal tracker deployed, first 5-8 clinic retainers through AATOD outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 8-10 clinics. Replaces-full-time-salary → 12-15 clinics, pushing past $10.79-13.49K/mo.

### 3. Mobile IV Hydration Therapy Clinic State Nursing Board Delegation & Infusion Standing-Order Compliance
**Problem:** Mobile IV hydration businesses that send RNs to administer saline, vitamin, and NAD+ infusions at clients' homes, hotel rooms, and events operate under state nursing board scope-of-practice rules requiring a currently valid, physician- or APRN-issued standing order or protocol covering each specific infusion type offered before an RN can independently initiate IV therapy without a physician on-site — several state boards of nursing have issued specific advisory guidance on the mobile IV hydration business model requiring documented medical-director oversight and a physician-availability standard, and an infusion administered under a lapsed or missing standing order is unauthorized-practice-of-medicine exposure for both the RN and the supervising physician. Because the business model is mobile rather than a fixed clinic, every visit must also be logged against the specific service location it occurred at — a private home, hotel room, or event — since several states restrict where infusion therapy may legally be administered outside a licensed facility, and a visit logged to a non-compliant location type is a separate, independently citable finding. IV fluids and additives sourced from an outside 503A compounding pharmacy carry their own beyond-use-date (BUD), and administering a compounded IV bag past its BUD is a distinct patient-safety and licensing-board violation that a fixed medical spa's laser-and-injectable compliance model has no equivalent tracking requirement for. A small IV hydration business running several RNs across a rotating calendar of house calls and pop-up events manages standing-order currency, per-visit location logging, and IV-bag BUD tracking with no dedicated compliance function beyond a shared scheduling app.
**What to build:** A per-nurse standing-order/protocol currency tracker with a "cleared to infuse" gate blocking a booking once the covering standing order lapses; a per-visit mobile-service-location log flagging any visit booked to a location type the operating state restricts; and an IV-bag/compounded-product beyond-use-date tracker blocking a bag from being logged as administered past its BUD.
**Skill fit:** State nursing board delegation rules and compounding-pharmacy BUD conventions are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-nurse and per-bag record requirement, and the infuse-eligibility gate plus the BUD tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fast-growing mobile-wellness trade most small operators currently track on a booking app with no systematic cross-check between a nurse's standing-order status and the specific bag they're about to administer.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: the standing-order tracker with the infuse-eligibility gate, the mobile-location log, and the BUD tracker. First 5-8 businesses from mobile IV operators already running multiple RNs across a rotating booking calendar.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the American IV Association member directory and mobile IV hydration industry Facebook and LinkedIn groups, where small operators already juggle per-nurse standing-order renewal and per-visit location logging with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $20/mo per RN tracked. Average client (6 RNs) = $249 + $120 = $369/mo. 27-33 businesses × $369/mo = **$9,963-12,177/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every RN's standing order renews on its own independent cycle, every booking opens its own mobile-location compliance check, and every compounded IV bag received carries its own BUD tracking requirement — a continuous, per-nurse and per-bag compliance stream tied directly to the business's booking volume.
**Biggest risk:** An infusion administered under a lapsed standing order, or a compounded IV bag used past its beyond-use-date, is exactly the finding a state nursing board complaint investigation is built to catch — the infuse-eligibility gate and the BUD tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → standing-order tracker, location log, and BUD tracker deployed, first 5-8 business retainers through American IV Association outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 businesses. Replaces-full-time-salary → 27-33 businesses, pushing past $9.96-12.18K/mo.

### 4. Nonprofit Consumer Credit Counseling Agency State Debt-Management License & NFCC/FCAA Accreditation Compliance
**Problem:** Nonprofit consumer credit counseling agencies that enroll clients in Debt Management Plans (DMPs) — negotiating a reduced interest rate with each creditor rather than a reduced lump-sum payoff — operate under state budget-and-credit-counseling or debt-management-services licensure, commonly adopted from the Uniform Debt-Management Services Act (UDMSA), requiring a surety bond and periodic license renewal, and must separately maintain accreditation from the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) or the Financial Counseling Association of America (FCAA) to keep the "fair share" funding relationships creditors pay accredited agencies for administering DMPs — losing accreditation doesn't just cost a credential, it cuts off the agency's creditor-funded revenue stream entirely. Every enrolled client makes a single monthly payment to the agency, which the agency must then disburse pro-rata across every one of that client's enrolled creditors according to the negotiated plan terms, and a payment that's disbursed late or misallocated across creditors is exactly the finding a state examination or an NFCC/FCAA reaccreditation audit is built to catch. Nonprofit agencies must also satisfy IRS §501(q), added by the Pension Protection Act specifically to govern tax-exempt credit counseling organizations, which caps the share of revenue an agency can derive from DMP fees and requires a majority-independent board — a compliance surface with no equivalent in the for-profit debt settlement model. A small nonprofit agency running several hundred active DMP clients manages per-client pro-rata creditor disbursement reconciliation, state license/bond currency, and NFCC/FCAA reaccreditation documentation with no dedicated compliance function beyond a spreadsheet bolted onto the agency's payment processor.
**What to build:** A per-client DMP pro-rata creditor disbursement reconciliation engine confirming each client's single monthly payment is correctly allocated across every enrolled creditor per the agreed plan; a state license and bond renewal tracker; and an NFCC/FCAA reaccreditation documentation tracker paired with a §501(q) board-composition and DMP-revenue-mix compliance check.
**Skill fit:** State UDMSA licensing cycles and NFCC/FCAA accreditation renewal requirements are public, structured regulatory and accrediting-body data with a well-defined per-client, multi-creditor record requirement, and the disbursement-reconciliation engine is the same deterministic "confirm the record reconciles before the disbursement goes out" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a nonprofit financial-counseling trade whose pro-rata, multi-creditor disbursement model no other idea in this repo's compliance-tracker library touches.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: the DMP disbursement reconciliation engine, the state license/bond tracker, and the NFCC/FCAA reaccreditation and §501(q) compliance tracker. First 5-8 agencies from small nonprofit credit counseling shops already reconciling DMP disbursements by hand.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) member agency network and the Financial Counseling Association of America (FCAA) member directory, where agency compliance staff already juggle per-client pro-rata disbursement reconciliation and reaccreditation documentation with no dedicated software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $2/mo per active DMP client tracked. Average client (300 DMP enrollees) = $299 + $600 = $899/mo. 11-14 agencies × $899/mo = **$9,889-12,586/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every enrolled client's monthly payment opens its own pro-rata disbursement cycle for the life of their plan, and the agency's NFCC/FCAA reaccreditation and state license each renew on their own independent schedule — a continuous, per-client compliance stream tied directly to the agency's active DMP caseload.
**Biggest risk:** A DMP payment disbursed late or misallocated across a client's enrolled creditors is exactly the finding a state exam or an NFCC/FCAA reaccreditation audit is built to catch — and losing accreditation cuts off the agency's creditor-funded revenue entirely — the disbursement-reconciliation engine is the core protection.
**Growth path:** Side project → disbursement engine, license tracker, and reaccreditation tracker deployed, first 5-8 agency retainers through NFCC/FCAA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 7-9 agencies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 11-14 agencies, pushing past $9.89-12.59K/mo.

### 5. Combat Sports (Boxing/MMA) Gym & Event Promoter State Athletic Commission Compliance
**Problem:** Gyms that train amateur and professional fighters, and promoters who stage boxing and MMA events, operate under state athletic commission licensure — the California State Athletic Commission and the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation's Combative Sports Program among them — that requires every fighter, cutman, coach, and second to hold an individual state license, renewed annually or per event, and every fighter must clear the Association of Boxing Commissions & Combative Sports' (ABC) unified national medical suspension database before being cleared to compete or spar again after a bout in any state, since medical suspensions issued after a knockout or injury are recognized cross-state under the ABC framework — a fighter cleared to compete despite an active out-of-state suspension is exactly the finding an ABC cross-state audit is built to catch. Every fighter's pre-fight medical panel — commonly a physical exam plus an HIV/hepatitis blood panel valid for a fixed window such as 12 months — must be current before that fighter is licensed to compete, and a promoter staging an event must separately file event-specific documentation with the commission a set number of days out: ringside physician coverage, proof of event insurance, and confirmation that every fighter on the card has a current medical panel and no active suspension. A small gym producing several amateur or regional cards a year, or a multi-fighter professional gym sending athletes to bouts in other states, manages per-fighter license and medical-panel currency, ABC suspension-database cross-checks, and per-event permit and insurance documentation with no dedicated compliance function beyond a coach's notebook and a folder of blood-work PDFs.
**What to build:** A per-fighter state license and medical-panel expiration tracker with a "cleared to compete" gate cross-referenced against the ABC national suspension database before a bout can be booked; a per-event permit, ringside-physician, and insurance-filing deadline tracker; and a coach/cutman/second credential currency tracker.
**Skill fit:** State athletic commission licensing cycles and the ABC's unified national medical suspension database are public, structured regulatory and cross-state registry data with a well-defined per-fighter and per-event record requirement, and the compete-eligibility gate plus the event-filing tracker are the same deterministic "cross-check the national registry before clearing the action / require the record before the event proceeds" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a combat-sports trade most small gyms and promoters currently track on paper with no systematic cross-check between a specific fighter's out-of-state suspension status and today's fight card.
**MVP scope:** Two to three weekends: the fighter license/medical-panel tracker with the compete-eligibility gate against the ABC database, the event-filing tracker, and the coach/cutman credential tracker. First 5-8 gyms or promoters from regional combat-sports operations already sending fighters across state lines.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the Association of Boxing Commissions & Combative Sports (ABC) member-commission network and regional MMA/boxing promoter and gym-affiliate associations, where gym owners and promoters already juggle per-fighter medical-panel currency and cross-state suspension checks with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per active fighter tracked. Average client (15 fighters) = $249 + $225 = $474/mo. 20-25 gyms/promoters × $474/mo = **$9,480-11,850/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every fighter's license, medical panel, and suspension status is a continuously monitored record that changes with every bout they take anywhere in the country, and every event a gym or promoter stages opens its own permit-and-insurance filing cycle — a continuous, per-fighter and per-event compliance stream tied directly to the operation's fight-card volume.
**Biggest risk:** A fighter cleared to compete despite an active out-of-state medical suspension, or an event staged without a fighter's current medical panel on file, is exactly the finding an ABC cross-state audit or a commission field check is built to catch — the compete-eligibility gate and the event-filing tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → fighter tracker, event-filing tracker, and credential tracker deployed, first 5-8 gym/promoter retainers through ABC outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 13-16 gyms/promoters. Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-25 gyms/promoters, pushing past $9.48-11.85K/mo.
