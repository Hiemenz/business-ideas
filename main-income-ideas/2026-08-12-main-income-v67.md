### 1. Public Insurance Adjuster State Licensure, Fee-Cap & Contract Rescission Compliance
**Problem:** Public adjusters — professionals hired by a policyholder, not the insurer, to prepare, document, and negotiate an insurance claim on the policyholder's behalf — must hold a state public-adjuster license issued after a written exam, background check, and in most states an errors-and-omissions insurance policy and a surety bond, renewed on a 1-2 year cycle with continuing education, and critically, an adjuster working a claim in any state must independently hold that specific state's license — a fact that matters enormously after a hurricane or hailstorm pulls adjusters across state lines to chase catastrophe-claim volume, since working a claim without that state's license is unauthorized practice and grounds for license revocation in the adjuster's home state as well. Every state's public adjuster statute caps the contingency fee the adjuster may charge (commonly 10% of the claim during a declared state of emergency, higher outside one) and mandates specific written-contract disclosures (fee percentage, cancellation terms, scope of representation), plus a statutory right-of-rescission period — typically 3 business days — during which the policyholder can cancel the contract with no obligation, a period several state insurance departments actively audit adjuster contracts against following a wave of post-catastrophe consumer complaints. In states where the adjuster is authorized to receive claim proceeds directly from the insurer before disbursing the policyholder's share, those funds must be held in a separate trust or escrow account — commingling claim proceeds with the firm's operating account is one of the most severe findings a state insurance department license-revocation action is built around. A small public adjusting firm running 3-8 adjusters across dozens of active claims, often surging into an out-of-state disaster response, manages per-adjuster multi-state license currency, per-contract fee-cap and rescission-period compliance, and per-claim trust-fund reconciliation with no dedicated compliance function — and an adjuster dispatched to a claim in a state where their license isn't current, or a contract that failed to honor its 3-day rescission window, is exactly the finding a state insurance department audit or a policyholder complaint investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-adjuster, per-state license currency tracker with a "cleared to work this claim" gate matching the adjuster's licensed states against each new claim's location before assignment; a per-contract compliance checklist confirming the fee percentage falls within the applicable state's statutory cap and the required rescission-period disclosure is present, with a rescission-countdown flag before the contract becomes enforceable; and a claim-proceeds trust account reconciliation log confirming every dollar received from an insurer is disbursed to the policyholder's share without commingling.
**Skill fit:** State public-adjuster licensing rosters and fee-cap statutes are public regulatory data with a well-defined, checkable per-state structure, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the rescission-countdown tracker are the same deterministic "block the assignment / compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a claims-advocacy trade most small firms currently run on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic multi-state license cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the multi-state license tracker with the dispatch-eligibility gate, the contract compliance checklist, and the trust-fund reconciliation log; land the first 5-8 firms through National Association of Public Insurance Adjusters (NAPIA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Association of Public Insurance Adjusters (NAPIA) member directory and state public adjuster association meetings, where independent firm owners already navigate multi-state license currency and fee-cap compliance during every catastrophe-response surge.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $30/mo per licensed adjuster tracked. Average client (5 adjusters) = $199 + $150 = $349/mo. 28-34 firms × $349/mo = **$9,772-11,866/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new claim opens its own contract-compliance and trust-reconciliation event, every catastrophe response adds fresh multi-state license-currency checks, and per-adjuster license renewal recurs on staggered independent cycles per state — a continuous, claim-volume-driven compliance stream that spikes hardest exactly when the firm's revenue does.
**Biggest risk:** An adjuster dispatched to a claim in a state where their license has lapsed or was never held, or claim proceeds commingled with the firm's operating account instead of held in trust, is exactly the finding a state insurance department license-revocation action is built to catch — the dispatch-eligibility gate and the trust reconciliation log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → license tracker, contract checklist, and trust reconciliation deployed, first 5-8 firm retainers through NAPIA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-18 firms. Replaces-full-time-salary → 28-34 firms, pushing past $9.77-11.87K/mo.

### 2. Clinical & Consumer Genetic Testing Laboratory CLIA/CAP Accreditation, Personnel Qualification & Consent Compliance
**Problem:** Clinical and consumer genetic testing laboratories running inherited-disease-risk, carrier-screening, and pharmacogenomic panels operate as CLIA high-complexity testing facilities, layered in most cases with CAP (College of American Pathologists) molecular pathology or cytogenetics checklist accreditation — a voluntary but functionally required credential most insurers and referring physicians treat as a baseline before accepting a lab's results — re-surveyed roughly every 2 years against a detailed, assay-specific standards checklist. Every assay the lab offers requires an individually documented, qualified laboratory director meeting CLIA's high-complexity director credential requirements and technologists individually qualified on that specific test method, and a result reported under an assay the lab's CAP accreditation doesn't currently cover, or signed by a director or technologist not properly credentialed for that method, is exactly the finding a CAP accreditation inspection or a CMS CLIA validation survey targets. Twice-yearly proficiency testing per CLIA-regulated analyte category is mandatory, and a failed PT round suspends that specific test from the lab's reportable menu until a corrective action and passing retest clears — a fast-moving, method-by-method deadline most small labs juggling a dozen-plus genetic panels track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic per-assay PT calendar. A growing set of state genetic-privacy statutes also require a specific informed-consent form — distinct from a routine clinical lab consent — documenting the patient's understanding of what a genetic result can reveal before the sample is processed, and in several states a genetic counselor's involvement is required before certain high-impact results (cancer-risk variants, for example) can be released to the ordering provider. A small genetic testing lab running 4-10 technologists across 10-20 offered panels and processing hundreds of samples a month manages method-by-method PT scheduling, per-technologist assay qualification, and per-sample consent/counselor-release documentation with no dedicated compliance function — and a result reported under a suspended PT round, or released without the state-mandated consent form on file, is exactly the gap a CAP inspection or state health department audit is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-assay CLIA/CAP accreditation and proficiency-testing tracker computing each test's next PT deadline and flagging any assay carrying a failed or overdue round with a hard block preventing that assay from being marked "reportable"; a per-technologist/director assay-qualification record tied to a result-signoff eligibility gate; and a per-sample consent and, where required, genetic-counselor release-documentation tracker blocking a result from being released until the state-mandated paperwork is confirmed on file.
**Skill fit:** CLIA's personnel-qualification rules and CAP's assay-specific checklist structure are well-documented, checkable regulatory and accrediting-body frameworks, and the PT-deadline computation plus the result-release eligibility gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event, block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to the diagnostic-genetics side of laboratory medicine distinct from the cannabis testing lab and fertility clinic ideas already covered in this repo.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the PT-deadline tracker, the technologist/director assay-qualification gate, and the consent/counselor-release tracker; land the first 5-8 labs through Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) member directory and state clinical laboratory association meetings, where small lab directors already navigate staggered PT-round deadlines across a growing genetic-panel menu.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $22/mo per accredited assay tracked. Average client (a lab offering 12 assays) = $249 + $264 = $513/mo. 20-24 labs × $513/mo = **$10,260-12,312/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every sample submission opens its own consent-documentation event, PT rounds recur twice yearly per assay independently, and technologist/director qualification records accumulate per method as staff cross-train onto new panels — a continuous, testing-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to sample throughput.
**Biggest risk:** A result reported under an assay that failed its most recent PT round, or released without the state-mandated genetic consent form on file, is exactly the finding a CAP accreditation inspection or state health department audit is built to catch — the PT-deadline tracker and the consent-release gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → PT-deadline tracker, assay-qualification gate, and consent tracker deployed, first 5-8 lab retainers through AMP outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 labs. Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-24 labs, pushing past $10.26-12.31K/mo.

### 3. Blood & Plasma Donation Center FDA 21 CFR 606/630 cGMP, Donor Eligibility & Deferral-List Compliance
**Problem:** Independent blood and source-plasma donation centers operate as FDA-registered blood establishments under 21 CFR Part 606 (current Good Manufacturing Practice for blood and blood components) and Part 630 (donor eligibility determinations), requiring every donor to complete a documented health-history screening and pass required infectious-disease-marker testing (HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis, and others per the current testing panel) before each and every donation — not just at first registration — with the center biennially inspected by the FDA against its cGMP procedures for collection, processing, and record retention. The center must maintain a donor-deferral list flagging both temporary deferrals (recent travel to a malaria-endemic region, recent tattoo, low hemoglobin) and indefinite deferrals (a reactive infectious-disease test result, specific medical history), and a donor collected while on an active deferral — whether through a records-lookup failure or an incomplete cross-check against the center's own deferral history — is one of the most serious findings an FDA inspection can make, since it directly threatens the safety of the blood supply the center's entire licensure depends on. Every unit collected must be traceable from donor through processing to final disposition (a "quarantine release" step confirming all required test results came back negative before a unit can be released for distribution), and a unit released before its required testing was confirmed complete is an independently reportable cGMP deviation the center must document and, in serious cases, report to FDA. A small independent donation center running several phlebotomy staff and processing dozens of donations a day manages per-donor deferral-list cross-checking, per-unit test-result-to-release reconciliation, and biennial FDA cGMP inspection readiness with no dedicated compliance function — and a donor collected while on the deferral list, or a unit released before its full test panel cleared, is exactly the gap an FDA inspection or a post-transfusion adverse-event investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-donor deferral-list cross-check gate blocking a collection from being logged as accepted until the donor's current deferral status (temporary or indefinite) is confirmed clear; a per-unit quarantine-to-release tracker requiring every required infectious-disease-marker result to be logged and confirmed negative before a unit can be marked "released"; and an FDA cGMP inspection-readiness checklist covering the center's documented collection, processing, and record-retention procedures.
**Skill fit:** 21 CFR Parts 606 and 630's donor-eligibility and cGMP structure are public federal regulatory sources with a well-defined, checkable per-donation record requirement, and the deferral cross-check plus the quarantine-release gate are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a collection-establishment category distinct from every diagnostic-laboratory or tissue-bank idea already in this repo.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the deferral-list cross-check gate, the quarantine-to-release tracker, and the cGMP inspection-readiness checklist; land the first 5-8 centers through America's Blood Centers (ABC) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — America's Blood Centers (ABC) member directory and independent plasma-center operator networks, where small center managers already navigate per-donor deferral cross-checking and per-unit release documentation ahead of every FDA inspection cycle.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base + $25/mo per phlebotomy/processing staff member tracked. Average client (a center with 6 staff) = $349 + $150 = $499/mo. 20-24 centers × $499/mo = **$9,980-11,976/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every single donation opens its own deferral cross-check and quarantine-to-release event, and a center's daily collection volume drives a continuous, high-frequency compliance stream that never pauses between the biennial FDA inspection cycles.
**Biggest risk:** A donor collected while on the deferral list, or a unit released for distribution before its full infectious-disease-marker panel cleared, is exactly the fact pattern an FDA inspection or post-transfusion adverse-event investigation is built to catch — the deferral cross-check gate and the quarantine-release tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → deferral cross-check gate, quarantine-release tracker, and cGMP checklist deployed, first 5-8 center retainers through ABC outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 centers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-24 centers, pushing past $9.98-11.98K/mo.

### 4. Optical Laboratory FDA Impact-Resistance Testing & Device Manufacturer Compliance
**Problem:** Optical laboratories that grind, surface, and fabricate prescription eyeglass lenses from an optometrist's, ophthalmologist's, or optician's prescription operate as FDA-regulated device manufacturers, required to register their facility and list their device category annually, and every batch of finished spectacle lenses must pass the FDA's 21 CFR 801.410 impact-resistance ("drop-ball") test — dropping a steel ball onto the lens from a specified height — with the test result documented per batch, since prescription lenses are legally prohibited from being sold or dispensed without this specific safety testing having been performed and recorded, and an eyewear-related eye-injury complaint investigation routinely starts by requesting the lab's drop-ball test records for the exact batch involved. The lab must also operate under 21 CFR Part 820's Quality System Regulation covering process controls and record retention, and every order must be traceable to a complete, signed prescription from the dispensing provider specifying sphere, cylinder, axis, and any prism correction — a lens ground and shipped against an incomplete or ambiguous prescription is a documented cause of dispensing errors that a state optometry or ophthalmology board complaint traces directly back to the fabricating lab. Labs producing safety or occupational eyewear to ANSI Z87.1 standards carry an additional, distinct testing and marking requirement layered on top of the base FDA impact-resistance rule. A small optical lab running 5-15 lens technicians and processing hundreds of orders a week manages FDA registration/listing renewal, per-batch drop-ball test documentation, and per-order prescription-completeness verification with no dedicated compliance function — and a batch of lenses shipped without a documented drop-ball test result, or an order fabricated against an incomplete prescription, is exactly the gap an FDA inspection or a state board complaint investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** An FDA facility registration and device-listing renewal tracker with tiered alerts ahead of the annual deadline; a per-batch impact-resistance (drop-ball) test log linking each finished-lens batch to its documented test result, with a hard block preventing a batch from being marked "cleared to ship" until the test result is on file; and a per-order prescription-completeness gate blocking a job from entering production until the dispensing provider's complete, signed prescription is confirmed.
**Skill fit:** The FDA's 21 CFR 801.410 impact-resistance testing rule and Part 820 Quality System Regulation are public, structured federal requirements with a well-defined, checkable per-batch and per-order record structure, and the ship-eligibility gate plus the prescription-completeness gate are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fabrication trade distinct from the dental laboratory idea already covered in this repo.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the FDA registration/listing tracker, the per-batch drop-ball test log, and the prescription-completeness gate; land the first 5-8 labs through Optical Laboratories Association (OLA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Optical Laboratories Association (OLA) member directory and state optical laboratory trade meetings, where independent lab owners already navigate per-batch impact-resistance documentation and prescription-completeness checks with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $14/mo per technician tracked. Average client (8 technicians) = $199 + $112 = $311/mo. 32-38 labs × $311/mo = **$9,952-11,818/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every finished lens batch generates its own impact-resistance test-documentation event, every accepted order opens its own prescription-completeness check, and FDA registration/listing renews annually regardless of order volume — a continuous, per-batch compliance stream tied directly to the lab's production throughput.
**Biggest risk:** A batch of lenses shipped without a documented drop-ball impact-resistance test result on file, or an order fabricated against an incomplete prescription, is exactly the gap an FDA inspection or state optometry/ophthalmology board complaint investigation is built to catch — the ship-eligibility gate and the prescription-completeness gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → FDA registration tracker, drop-ball test log, and prescription-completeness gate deployed, first 5-8 lab retainers through OLA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 labs. Replaces-full-time-salary → 32-38 labs, pushing past $9.95-11.82K/mo.

### 5. USDA-Licensed Commercial Dog & Cat Breeder Animal Welfare Act & APHIS Inspection Compliance
**Problem:** Commercial dog and cat breeders selling wholesale to pet stores, brokers, or (above a federally defined threshold) directly to consumers sight-unseen must hold a USDA APHIS Class A or Class B license under the federal Animal Welfare Act (7 U.S.C. §2131 et seq.), subject to unannounced biannual facility inspections covering housing, temperature and ventilation standards, veterinary care protocols, and — the category APHIS inspectors cite most frequently against small breeders — recordkeeping: every breeder must maintain acquisition and disposition records for every animal (source, date acquired, date and manner of disposition, and for each litter, sire/dam identification and health certification) retained for a federally mandated period and produced on demand during an inspection, and an incomplete or inconsistent litter record is one of the most common findings behind an APHIS License Renewal denial or a formal warning that can escalate to license suspension. A growing number of states layer their own commercial-breeder ("puppy mill") licensing statute on top of the federal AWA license, with parallel but not identical recordkeeping and inspection requirements, and every puppy or kitten sold typically requires a state or breed-specific health certificate signed by a licensed veterinarian within a defined window before the sale — a certificate that must be reconciled against the litter's disposition record or the sale is technically undocumented under both the state statute and the federal AWA framework. A small commercial breeding operation running several breeding females and multiple litters a year manages per-animal acquisition/disposition recordkeeping, per-litter health-certificate reconciliation, and staggered federal/state license renewal with no dedicated compliance function — and a litter record missing a required field, or a puppy sold without a reconciled health certificate on file, is exactly the gap an APHIS biannual inspection or a state breeder-license audit is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-animal acquisition and disposition record tracker (source, acquisition date, disposition date/manner) matching APHIS's required recordkeeping fields; a per-litter record linking sire/dam identification, birth date, and each puppy/kitten's sale disposition to its signed veterinary health certificate, with a hard flag for any sold animal missing a reconciled certificate; and a federal APHIS license and state commercial-breeder license renewal tracker with tiered alerts ahead of each inspection cycle.
**Skill fit:** The Animal Welfare Act's recordkeeping requirements and APHIS's inspection checklist are public federal regulatory sources with a well-defined, checkable per-animal and per-litter record structure, and the disposition-to-health-certificate reconciliation is the same deterministic "confirm every required record is on file" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a federally licensed animal-commerce category most small commercial breeders currently track on paper litter cards with no systematic cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the acquisition/disposition tracker, the per-litter health-certificate reconciliation, and the federal/state license renewal tracker; land the first 5-8 breeders through outreach to USDA-licensed breeder associations and regional kennel clubs.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state and regional breeder association meetings and USDA APHIS-licensee outreach lists, where small commercial breeders already navigate biannual inspection prep and litter-record reconciliation by hand.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $99/mo base + $8/mo per breeding female tracked. Average client (a facility with 12 breeding females) = $99 + $96 = $195/mo. 55-64 breeders × $195/mo = **$10,725-12,480/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every litter opens its own disposition-to-health-certificate reconciliation chain, acquisition/disposition records accumulate continuously as animals are bought, bred, and sold, and federal/state license renewal recurs on independent cycles — a per-litter, per-animal compliance stream tied directly to breeding volume.
**Biggest risk:** A litter record missing a required acquisition/disposition field, or a puppy or kitten sold without a reconciled veterinary health certificate on file, is exactly the gap an APHIS biannual inspection or a state commercial-breeder license audit is built to catch — the disposition-to-certificate reconciliation is the core protection.
**Growth path:** Side project → acquisition/disposition tracker, litter reconciliation, and license renewal tracker deployed, first 5-8 breeder retainers through association outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 26-32 breeders. Replaces-full-time-salary → 55-64 breeders, pushing past $10.73-12.48K/mo.
