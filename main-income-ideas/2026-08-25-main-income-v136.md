### 1. Non-Depository Trust Company State Fiduciary Charter, Capital-Adequacy & Trust Examination Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Non-depository trust companies — chartered under a state banking department's trust statute (South Dakota, Nevada, Wyoming, and New Hampshire have built especially active charter regimes, though most states offer some form of trust charter) to serve as corporate trustee for revocable and irrevocable trusts, directed trusts, and trust-held business interests without ever taking bank deposits — are examined by their chartering state's banking or financial-institutions department on a periodic safety-and-soundness cycle modeled directly on a bank examination, checking the company's minimum statutory capital and liquid-asset ratio, its fidelity/fiduciary bond currency, and the accuracy of its books against every individual trust account it administers. Because the trust company holds actual legal title to each trust's assets, it must maintain per-trust-account principal-and-income accounting under the state's version of the Uniform Principal and Income Act, reconcilable on demand to a beneficiary, a co-trustee, or a court — an account whose principal-and-income allocation drifts out of reconciliation, or a capital ratio that slips below the statutory minimum between examinations, is exactly the finding a trust examiner's periodic review is built to catch, with consequences that can include a corrective-capital order or, in a severe case, charter revocation that would force the company to resign every trusteeship it holds. A growing trust company managing dozens to hundreds of individual trust accounts across multiple grantor families tracks per-account principal-and-income reconciliation, capital/liquidity-ratio computation against the statutory minimum, fiduciary bond renewal, and the periodic state trust-examination calendar on a mix of a trust accounting system built for transaction posting rather than regulatory-ratio monitoring and a compliance officer's spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming the company's capital position and every account's reconciliation are both actually current before the next examination arrives.

**What to build:** A capital-adequacy and liquid-asset-ratio tracker computing the company's position against its state's statutory minimum from logged balance-sheet inputs, with an early-warning alert well before a ratio slips out of compliance; a per-trust-account principal-and-income reconciliation tracker flagging any account whose allocation hasn't been reconciled within the state's required interval; and a fiduciary bond renewal and periodic trust-examination-readiness calendar aggregating account-level reconciliation status into a single examiner-facing report.

**Skill fit:** State trust-charter statutes' capital-ratio formulas and principal-and-income accounting rules are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-account reconciliation requirement, and the ratio-computation-plus-early-warning pattern is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a bank-adjacent fiduciary niche this repo has never addressed and that shares no regulator, license type, or examination cycle with the RIA or individual court-appointed fiduciary ideas already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the capital/liquidity-ratio tracker with the early-warning alert, the per-account reconciliation tracker, and the bond/examination-readiness calendar. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small independent and single-family-office-adjacent trust companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Bankers Association's Trust & Wealth Management group and its National Trust School alumni network, alongside state trust-company association meetings in the most active charter states, reach exactly the small independent trust companies currently reconciling per-account principal-and-income allocations against a capital ratio with no dedicated compliance system.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every trust account the company administers generates its own recurring principal-and-income reconciliation obligation for as long as the trust remains in force — often decades — the capital-ratio computation recurs continuously as the company's asset base grows, and every new grantor family the company onboards multiplies the number of simultaneously running account-reconciliation cycles — a continuous, account-portfolio-driven compliance stream tied directly to the trust company's client growth.

### 2. Qualified Opportunity Zone (QOZ) Fund Sponsor 90% Asset-Test & Form 8996 Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) sponsors — real estate and operating-business fund managers who accept investors' rolled-over capital gains under IRC §1400Z-2 in exchange for the fund's deferral and eventual basis-step-up tax benefits — must self-certify the fund's QOF status annually on IRS Form 8996 and, critically, satisfy a 90% asset test measured on two specific testing dates every single year the fund exists (the last day of the fund's first six-month period and the last day of its taxable year), confirming at least 90% of the fund's assets are invested in qualifying Opportunity Zone property; a fund that falls short of the 90% threshold on either testing date owes a penalty computed against the shortfall for every month the deficiency persists, a penalty that compounds silently if nobody is running the calculation between testing dates. Where the fund's investment is an existing building rather than ground-up new construction, the fund must additionally complete a "substantial improvement" — capital expenditure exceeding the building's adjusted basis, excluding land — within a strict 30-month window from acquisition, and a project that stalls past that window loses its qualifying-property status for the underlying asset entirely. Every investor's own deferred-gain and basis-step-up benefit further depends on the fund maintaining continuous QOF qualification across a 10-year holding period, meaning a single missed testing-date computation years into the fund's life can retroactively jeopardize the tax benefit every investor in the fund was promised at closing. A fund sponsor running several QOFs across different vintage years and property types manages per-fund semi-annual asset-test computation, per-property substantial-improvement-deadline tracking, and annual Form 8996 filing on a mix of a fund administrator's spreadsheet and a tax advisor's periodic memo, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific fund's asset composition is actually above 90% before the next testing date arrives.

**What to build:** A per-fund 90% asset-test calculator computing current qualifying-versus-total asset composition against both annual testing dates with an early-warning alert well before a shortfall; a per-property substantial-improvement 30-month countdown tracker flagging any project falling behind its required capital-expenditure pace; and an annual Form 8996 filing tracker aggregating every fund's certification status into a single sponsor-facing dashboard.

**Skill fit:** IRC §1400Z-2's twice-yearly asset-test formula and the 30-month substantial-improvement clock are public, structured federal tax data with concrete computational triggers, and the asset-test calculator with its early-warning alert is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fund-compliance niche explicitly distinct from the historic tax credit's Part 2/3 review-and-recapture structure and the 1031 exchange's one-time 45/180-day deadline pair already covered in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the 90% asset-test calculator, the substantial-improvement countdown tracker, and the Form 8996 filing tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small independent QOF sponsors.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every fund's 90% asset test recurs twice a year for the entire 10-year holding period regardless of how routine the portfolio, every property acquisition opens its own independent 30-month substantial-improvement clock, and every new fund vintage the sponsor launches multiplies the number of simultaneously running testing cycles — a continuous, fund-portfolio-driven compliance stream tied directly to the sponsor's fundraising cadence.

**Biggest risk:** A sponsor running a single small fund with an already-stabilized, comfortably-qualifying asset base has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. ### 3. Stored-Commodity Grain Fumigation Contractor EPA Fumigant RUP & Aeration-Clearance Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Contractors performing phosphine fumigation of stored grain — treating bins, elevators, rail cars, and ship holds with aluminum or magnesium phosphide tablets to control stored-product insects for commodity preservation and export-quarantine compliance — operate under an EPA Restricted Use Pesticide certification specific to fumigants under 40 CFR 156.208, a substantially more detailed applicator credential than the general "fumigation" category a structural pest control license covers, requiring a written, site-specific Fumigation Management Plan (FMP) for every treatment confirming the structure's gas-tightness, the placarding and perimeter-notification distance, and the emergency-response procedure before a single tablet is placed. Because phosphine gas is lethal at low concentrations, every fumigation ends with a mandatory aeration-and-clearance gas-concentration test that must read below a safe threshold before the treated grain can be released for shipment or workers can re-enter the space — a lot released, or a bin re-entered, before its clearance test actually confirms a safe reading is exactly the finding an OSHA or state pesticide-regulatory inspection is built to catch, with consequences ranging from applicator-license suspension to a fatality investigation in the worst case. Every treatment site also requires OSHA permit-required confined-space entry procedures for bin entry separate from the fumigation permit itself, and a grain elevator or export terminal relying on a contractor's clearance test to certify a shipment ready for loading is trusting a gate the contractor has to be able to prove was actually met. A small fumigation contracting crew running a compressed harvest-season schedule across dozens of client bins and elevators manages per-site FMP documentation, applicator-certification currency, and per-treatment clearance-test results on a mix of a paper fumigation log and a shop binder, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific bin's clearance test actually cleared before the grain is marked releasable.

**What to build:** A per-site Fumigation Management Plan generator and documentation log capturing gas-tightness confirmation, perimeter/placarding distance, and emergency-response plan; an applicator RUP-fumigant certification renewal tracker; and a per-treatment aeration-and-clearance gas-test log with a "cleared to release/re-enter" gate blocking any site from being marked safe until a passing clearance reading is on file.

**Skill fit:** EPA's fumigant-specific RUP requirements and OSHA's confined-space entry rules are public, structured federal regulatory data with a concrete per-site clearance gate, and the "cleared to release" gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a stored-commodity fumigation niche explicitly distinct from the general structural pest control idea's per-category applicator tracker already in this repo, which never builds a grain-specific FMP or gas-clearance test of any kind.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every treatment generates its own FMP-and-clearance-test event regardless of how routine the bin, every harvest season reopens the entire multi-site fumigation calendar from scratch, and every new elevator or export-terminal client the contractor adds multiplies the number of simultaneously scheduled treatments — a continuous, site-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the contractor's client roster.
