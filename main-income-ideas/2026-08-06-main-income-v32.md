### 1. Manufactured Housing Community State Lot Lease & Utility Billing Compliance
**Problem:** Manufactured housing community (MHC) operators — who own the land and lease individual lot spaces to manufactured home owners — face a regulatory environment that is materially different from standard residential rentals: 42 states have enacted specific manufactured housing landlord-tenant acts that govern lot rent increases (requiring 60-90 days advance written notice in most states — significantly longer than standard residential lease change notice periods), lot lease termination (most states require 180-365 days written notice and specific cause for termination of a homeowner's lot lease, compared to 30-60 days for standard residential tenancies), and utility billing practices. MHC operators who master-meter utilities (water, electric, gas) and pass through costs to residents must comply with each state's utility pass-through billing rules — which vary significantly: some states require itemized billing showing actual consumption, some cap the pass-through at the actual utility cost with no markup, some require a resident disclosure statement before utility billing begins, and some require the MHC to offer sub-metering if requested by residents. Small to mid-size MHC operators (communities of 50-500 lots, typically 1-10 communities under management) frequently manage lot rent increase notices and utility bills manually with no systematic tracking of state-specific requirements, generating regulatory complaints from residents and state manufactured housing program enforcement actions.
**What to build:** An MHC lot lease and utility billing compliance service — a state manufactured housing landlord-tenant act compliance database (the specific lot rent increase notice period, required notice method, lot lease termination notice and cause requirements, and utility billing rules for each of the 42 states with state manufactured housing statutes), a lot rent increase notice management system (tracking each community's lot rent increase cycle, calculating the required notice date based on the state's advance notice requirement and the planned rent increase effective date, generating the state-specific required written notice to each homeowner, and maintaining proof of notice delivery), a utility billing compliance tracker (for communities with master-metered utility pass-through: documenting the billing methodology against each state's pass-through billing requirements, generating itemized resident utility bills with required disclosures, and flagging any billing methodology that may not comply with the state's utility billing rules), a lot lease termination documentation system (when an MHC operator must terminate a homeowner's lot lease for cause, generating the required cause documentation and notice in compliance with the state's specific termination cause requirements and notice period), and a resident notification compliance calendar (tracking all required resident communications — annual disclosure of community rules and regulations, utility billing methodology disclosure, and any state-mandated resident meeting requirements — with 90/60/30-day alerts before each required communication deadline).
**Skill fit:** State manufactured housing landlord-tenant statutes are publicly available — building a 42-state compliance requirement database from these statutes is a regulatory research and data modeling problem. The lot rent increase notice generator (a parameterized state-specific notice letter with required content), utility billing methodology compliance checker (a structured comparison of the operator's billing practice against each state's specific requirements), and resident notification calendar are document generation and compliance calendar problems the builder's engineering skills address directly. Manufactured Housing Institute (MHI) publishes state legislative tracking for manufactured housing regulations that provides a starting framework for the state compliance database.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a compliance database for the 15 states with the largest manufactured housing communities (TX, FL, MI, IN, NC, OH, PA, CA, WA, SC, GA, AZ, TN, AL, CO), a lot rent increase notice generator with state-specific advance notice calculation, a utility billing disclosure template for each state's requirements, and a resident notification calendar; first 5-8 MHC operator clients through Manufactured Housing Institute (MHI) member outreach and state manufactured housing association networks while the lot rent increase notice generator is validated against real operator notice cycles.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through MHI (Manufactured Housing Institute) state affiliate networks (every state with significant manufactured housing inventory has a state MHI affiliate with active member directories of MHC operators), manufactured housing property management company communities (the National Communities Council within MHI specifically represents MHC operators and is the most concentrated buyer community), and manufactured housing investment firm networks (private equity and family office investors acquiring MHC portfolios have compliance management needs across multiple communities and multiple states that exceed single-operator complexity).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per MHC operator (covering all owned communities): $400-1,000/mo (depending on lot count and state count — a single-community operator with a 100-lot community in one state: $400/mo; a multi-community operator with 500 lots across 4 states: $1,000/mo). Managing 20-30 operator retainers at $600/mo average = **$12,000-18,000/mo**. Institutional MHC portfolio operators (acquiring and operating multiple communities) represent enterprise accounts at $2,000-5,000/mo for portfolio-wide compliance management. Reaching 20-30 operator retainers requires 3-5 active MHI state affiliate relationships and NCC (National Communities Council) conference presence.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Lot rent increase notices are required every time an MHC operator raises rents — most communities raise rents annually, creating a permanent annual compliance cycle. Utility billing is monthly and continuous. State manufactured housing enforcement actions have increased as tenant advocacy organizations in high-cost housing markets challenge MHC operators' billing and termination practices, making systematic compliance documentation more urgent. Institutional investors acquiring MHC portfolios are expanding rapidly and need compliance infrastructure for multi-community, multi-state operations.
**Biggest risk:** A lot rent increase notice that fails to provide the state-mandated advance notice period — or a lot lease termination notice that doesn't cite legally sufficient cause — can be challenged by the resident and invalidated by a manufactured housing hearing officer or court. Build the notice generator with an explicit operator review step before delivery, and document in the engagement letter that the MHC operator's attorney reviews any termination for cause before the service generates the termination notice.
**Growth path:** Side project → state compliance database for 15 target states, lot rent increase notice generator and utility billing disclosure templates deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through MHI state affiliate outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-12 operator retainers, NCC conference attendance generating direct acquisition, first institutional portfolio client acquired (~$5-9K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-30 operator retainers plus 2-3 institutional portfolio accounts, state compliance database expanded to all 42 states, pushing past $12-18K/mo.

### 2. FINRA Broker-Dealer Supervisory Procedures & Annual Compliance Program Management
**Problem:** FINRA-registered broker-dealers are required under FINRA Rules 3110 and 3120 to establish and maintain written supervisory procedures (WSPs) tailored to their business lines, implement a supervisory control system that tests and verifies those WSPs, and certify annually (via a FINRA Rule 3120 annual compliance report prepared by the designated principal) that the firm's supervisory system is reasonably designed to achieve compliance with applicable securities laws and FINRA rules. Small FINRA member firms — independent broker-dealers with 10-50 registered representatives focused on a specific product set (fixed annuities, variable life insurance, direct participation programs, equity trading, or municipal securities) — frequently have WSP manuals that were drafted when the firm registered and have not been updated for subsequent FINRA rule changes, lack documented supervisory control testing (the specific trade reviews, email surveillance, and branch office inspections required by the WSP), and conduct Rule 3120 annual reviews that are cursory recitations rather than genuine supervisory control assessments. FINRA examiners (Regulatory Operations Examinations) review WSPs, supervisory control testing records, annual report documentation, and registered representative supervision records during cycle exams — findings of inadequate supervision are FINRA's most frequent enforcement category.
**What to build:** A FINRA broker-dealer supervisory procedures and annual compliance program management service — a WSP maintenance and update system (maintaining the firm's written supervisory procedures for each business line, tracking FINRA regulatory notices and rule amendments that require WSP updates, generating WSP amendment drafts for the CCO/designated principal's review when FINRA issues new supervisory guidance), a supervisory control testing documentation system (for each supervisory control specified in the WSP — trade review, email surveillance, branch office inspection, registered representative fingerprint and background check — maintaining the testing frequency schedule, documentation of completed testing, findings from testing, and corrective action taken on any deficiencies), a Rule 3120 annual compliance report preparation workflow (gathering the required information for the annual compliance report: the supervisory control testing results, any compliance matters identified during the year, any changes to the firm's business lines or supervisory structure, and the designated principal's certification — generating a draft annual report for the CCO's review and sign-off), a registered representative supervision file management system (maintaining each RR's supervision record: U4/U5 filing currency, annual compliance training completion, heightened supervision status if applicable, and annual review documentation from the supervising principal), and a FINRA examination readiness dashboard (generating the examination documentation package that FINRA cycle examiners typically request: WSP, supervisory control testing records, annual compliance report, RR supervision files, and any regulatory correspondence).
**Skill fit:** FINRA's Rulebook (Rules 3110, 3120, 3130) is publicly available and defines each supervisory requirement precisely — building a WSP template and supervisory control testing schedule from these rules is a regulatory mapping and documentation problem. FINRA publishes annual examination findings letters and regulatory notices that identify the most common WSP deficiencies, providing a specific checklist framework for the compliance program. The testing documentation system, annual report preparation workflow, and examination readiness package generator are structured compliance documentation and deadline management problems.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a WSP template for two target business lines (fixed annuity/variable life insurance independent BD, and equity trading BD — the two most common small FINRA member firm structures), a supervisory control testing schedule and documentation system, a Rule 3120 annual compliance report template, and a FINRA examination readiness checklist; first 3-5 small BD clients through FINRA compliance attorney referral networks while the WSP template is validated against current FINRA rule requirements.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through FINRA compliance attorneys and consultants (attorneys who represent small BDs during FINRA examinations are the natural referral source — a 3-4 attorney referral relationship generates a consistent client pipeline), SIFMA (Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association) member communities (SIFMA's compliance and legal community includes CCOs at small and mid-size BDs who actively seek systematic WSP management tools), and independent broker-dealer industry conferences (FSI — Financial Services Institute — represents independent BD firms and hosts annual events where small BD CCOs discuss compliance resource needs).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per FINRA member firm: $600-1,800/mo (depending on RR count and business line complexity — a 15-RR BD focused on fixed annuity sales: $600/mo; a 40-RR BD with multiple product lines including variable products, direct participation programs, and public securities: $1,800/mo). Managing 8-12 BD retainers at $1,100/mo average = **$8,800-13,200/mo**. FINRA examination preparation packages (one-time $2,000-5,000 for comprehensive examination documentation for firms notified of an upcoming cycle exam) generate significant supplemental revenue. Reaching 8-12 BD retainers requires 3-4 active FINRA compliance attorney referral relationships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** FINRA Rule 3120 annual compliance certification is required every year for every FINRA member firm — the annual compliance program review is a permanent regulatory obligation. WSP maintenance is ongoing as FINRA continuously issues rule amendments and regulatory notices requiring WSP updates. FINRA examination cycles (every 2-4 years for most small BDs) create high-urgency preparation engagements that generate fees significantly above the monthly retainer rate.
**Biggest risk:** A WSP that has a material gap — a business activity the firm conducts that is not addressed in the WSP — discovered during a FINRA examination generates a finding that the firm failed to establish adequate supervisory procedures, a serious FINRA violation. Build the WSP coverage mapping tool as a cross-reference: each business activity the firm conducts is mapped to a WSP section, and any activity without a corresponding WSP section is flagged as a gap requiring a WSP amendment before the next examination.
**Growth path:** Side project → WSP templates for two target BD business lines, supervisory control testing schedule, Rule 3120 annual report template deployed, first 3-5 BD retainers through FINRA compliance attorney outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 5-6 BD retainers, FSI conference presence generating direct acquisition, first FINRA examination preparation packages completed (~$4-7K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 8-12 BD retainers, WSP templates expanded to additional product lines, annual exam cycle concentrated in predictable seasons generating revenue spikes, pushing past $8-13K/mo.

### 3. Construction Site Safety Program Management for General Contractors
**Problem:** General contractors on commercial construction projects must maintain OSHA-compliant safety and health programs under 29 CFR Part 1926 (OSHA's construction standards): a project-specific written safety and health plan documenting site hazard identification and controls; documented weekly toolbox talks (safety meetings covering specific hazards relevant to current site activities — OSHA requires regular safety meetings but many GCs conduct them without documentation that satisfies OSHA inspectors); site-specific worker safety training records (fall protection, scaffolding, excavation, electrical hazards, PPE — OSHA requires site-specific training and documentation for high-hazard activities); incident reporting (OSHA requires electronic reporting of work-related fatalities within 8 hours and of hospitalizations, amputations, and eye losses within 24 hours through OSHA's online reporting portal); and OSHA 300 log maintenance (required for employers with 10+ employees: the injury and illness log, summary, and incident report must be maintained throughout the calendar year and the 300A summary posted February 1 through April 30). Small to mid-size GCs (10-50 employees, running 3-10 projects simultaneously) frequently have a safety coordinator or project superintendent handling safety documentation across multiple active sites, producing inconsistent toolbox talk records, incomplete site-specific safety plans, and OSHA 300 logs that aren't maintained in real time.
**What to build:** A construction site safety program management service — a project-specific written safety and health plan generator (building a site-specific safety plan template from the GC's project characteristics: site type, subcontractor trades on site, high-hazard activities, site layout, emergency response procedures, and designated safety contacts — generating a CSSP document that addresses OSHA's expected plan content for a multi-employer construction site), a toolbox talk documentation system (a weekly toolbox talk record: the topic covered, date, trainer's name, and attendee sign-in list for each active project — generating a monthly toolbox talk compliance report showing which projects have current weekly documentation and which are falling behind), a site safety training record tracker (per-employee training records for each OSHA-required high-hazard training: fall protection, scaffolding, excavation, hazard communication, PPE — tracking who has completed which training and when, and which employees need training before starting work on specific activities), an OSHA incident reporting compliance system (tracking every work-related injury or illness, determining the OSHA reportability based on injury type and severity, and managing the 8-hour and 24-hour reporting window alerts for OSHA-reportable events — with the online reporting submission workflow), and an OSHA 300 log maintenance tool (maintaining the OSHA 300 injury and illness log in real time throughout the calendar year, generating the 300A summary for the February 1 posting, and archiving the complete 300 record package for the required 5-year retention period).
**Skill fit:** OSHA 29 CFR Part 1926 and OSHA's published guidance on construction safety program requirements define the specific documentation required for each element — building a safety plan template, toolbox talk log, training tracker, incident reporting workflow, and OSHA 300 log system from these specifications is a regulatory mapping and document generation problem. OSHA's online injury reporting portal (ITA — Injury Tracking Application) accepts electronic submission for required reportable events. The OSHA 300 log format and 300A summary are publicly available OSHA forms.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a project-specific safety plan template covering OSHA's expected construction safety plan content, a toolbox talk weekly documentation system with compliance tracking across multiple active projects, a training record tracker for the 5 highest-citation OSHA construction standards (fall protection, scaffolding, hazard communication, electrical, PPE), an OSHA incident reporting window alert system, and an OSHA 300 log tool; first 5-8 GC clients through Associated General Contractors (AGC) chapter outreach while the safety plan template is validated against OSHA construction safety program requirements.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through AGC (Associated General Contractors) chapter networks and ABC (Associated Builders and Contractors) chapter networks (the two largest general contractor professional associations with active member directories — the concentrated buyer community for construction safety management services), construction safety consultant communities (safety consultants who serve GCs on specific high-risk projects refer ongoing documentation management services to their clients between project engagements), and construction insurance broker referral networks (commercial contractors' insurance brokers see OSHA safety documentation gaps in every renewal and actively refer safety program management services to their clients to reduce loss ratios).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per GC: $400-900/mo (depending on employee count and active project count — a 15-employee GC running 3 projects: $400/mo; a 40-employee GC running 8 simultaneous projects with multiple subcontractor trades generating complex site safety plan requirements: $900/mo). Managing 20-30 GC retainers at $600/mo average = **$12,000-18,000/mo**. OSHA inspection response packages (one-time $500-1,500 for documentation packages when a GC receives an OSHA site inspection citation) generate supplemental revenue. Reaching 20-30 GC retainers requires 4-6 active AGC/ABC chapter relationships and construction insurance broker referral partnerships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Toolbox talks are required weekly on every active project — a GC running 5 active projects generates 5 toolbox talk documentation events per week continuously. The OSHA 300 log must be maintained throughout the calendar year and the 300A summary must be posted every February. New project starts generate new site-specific safety plan requirements. OSHA construction citations are among the most frequent enforcement actions — GCs with active projects are under continuous inspection risk, making systematic documentation more urgently demanded.
**Biggest risk:** A failure to file an OSHA reportable event (a fatality, hospitalization, amputation, or eye loss) within the required 8-hour or 24-hour window results in an OSHA late reporting citation even if the event is ultimately reported. Build the incident reporting system with a push notification (not just email) triggered at 4 hours and 7 hours post-incident for fatality/hospitalization events, providing two escalating alerts before the 8-hour deadline.
**Growth path:** Side project → construction safety plan template, toolbox talk documentation system, and OSHA 300 log tool deployed, first 5-8 GC retainers through AGC chapter outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-15 GC retainers, ABC chapter presence generating direct acquisition, construction insurance broker referral channel established (~$5-8K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-30 GC retainers, OSHA inspection response packages supplementing retainers, pushing past $12-18K/mo.

### 4. Multi-State Annual Report & Registered Agent Maintenance for Operating Businesses
**Problem:** Businesses incorporated in one state but operating in multiple states must maintain foreign qualification and file annual (or biennial) reports with each state's Secretary of State where they are registered — paying annual report fees that vary by state from $10 to $800+, keeping registered agent address information current (a registered agent whose address is outdated results in service of process being missed, which can result in default judgments against the business), and since January 1, 2024, filing Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reports with FinCEN under the Corporate Transparency Act (CTA) for most domestic entities. Multi-state businesses typically discover missed annual report deadlines when they receive a state notice of administrative dissolution — at which point the business must file reinstatement documents and pay penalty fees to restore its good standing in that state. A business in administrative dissolution in a state may be unable to enforce contracts in that state, may have its bank account flagged by compliance reviews, and may face personal liability exposure for transactions conducted while dissolved. Small businesses operating in 5-15 states (regional service companies, staffing agencies, franchise operators, real estate investors with multi-state entity structures) have the highest complexity: different annual report deadlines in each state, different registered agent requirements, and different fee schedules — often managed through a mix of registered agent service providers and manual calendar reminders that fall through the cracks.
**What to build:** A multi-state annual report and registered agent maintenance service — a state annual report deadline calendar (a real-time calendar of each state's annual report deadline for the client's registered entities — some states use the entity's anniversary date, some use a fixed calendar date — with 90/60/30-day alerts and fee amounts by state), a registered agent currency management system (confirming registered agent information is current and accurate in each state of qualification, tracking registered agent renewal fees, and flagging any state where the registered agent service requires renewal or reconfirmation), a Corporate Transparency Act BOI report management system (tracking each entity's CTA BOI initial filing status, the required 30-day update window when beneficial owner information changes, and the annual update requirement — maintaining the BOI filing record for each covered entity), an annual report preparation and filing coordination workflow (collecting the required information for each state's annual report — officer names and addresses, registered agent confirmation, and any state-specific disclosure fields — and coordinating the filing submission, whether through the state's online filing portal, by mail, or through the registered agent's filing service), and a good standing monitoring dashboard (regularly querying each state's Secretary of State database to confirm the entity's current standing, and alerting immediately when any entity's status changes from Good Standing to Administrative Dissolution or Delinquent — providing advance notice before the dissolution causes operational problems).
**Skill fit:** Secretary of State annual report filing portals for most states provide public entity status searches and in many cases online filing — building a multi-state annual report deadline calendar from each state's filing requirements is a regulatory research and data management problem. The CTA BOI filing system (FinCEN's BOI E-Filing System) accepts electronic submissions — building a BOI report tracking and filing workflow from FinCEN's published reporting requirements is a data management problem. The good standing monitoring dashboard (automated periodic queries to state SOS databases) is a web automation problem the builder's engineering skills address directly.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a 30-state annual report deadline calendar (covering the states where most multi-state businesses are foreign-qualified: DE, CA, TX, FL, NY, IL, GA, WA, CO, OH, NC, VA, NJ, AZ, MI, MN, PA, MA, TN, MD, OR, NV, UT, SC, MO, IN, WI, KS, CT, LA), a registered agent renewal tracker, a CTA BOI filing status tracker, and a good standing monitoring tool; first 5-10 business clients through business attorney and registered agent service referral networks while the annual report calendar is validated against real multi-state filing deadline scenarios.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through business attorney referral networks (attorneys who handle business entity formation and expansion regularly refer annual report maintenance services to clients who have formed in multiple states), business registered agent service provider partner programs (National Registered Agents and Registered Agents Inc. are large registered agent providers whose clients pay for registered agent service but not for annual report preparation — a white-label or referral partnership generates a direct pipeline), and franchise system compliance communities (franchisors and multi-unit franchisees operating in multiple states have exactly the multi-state annual report complexity this service addresses — franchise lawyer associations are a concentrated referral channel).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per business (all entities): $200-600/mo (depending on entity count and state count — a 3-entity structure in 5 states: $200/mo; a 12-entity franchise holding structure in 15 states: $600/mo). Managing 35-60 business retainers at $350/mo average = **$12,250-21,000/mo**. Annual report filing season (most states have Q1 deadlines) generates concentrated project volume that justifies premium per-filing fees for clients who need expedited reinstatement. Reaching 35-60 retainers requires 4-6 business attorney referral relationships and 1-2 registered agent service provider partnerships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Annual report filings are required every year in every state where the business is registered — a 10-state operation generates 10 annual report deadlines per year continuously. CTA BOI reports require 30-day updates whenever beneficial ownership changes — a growing business with investor or management changes generates BOI update obligations throughout the year. Administrative dissolution is a crisis event that creates immediate urgency and willingness to pay for remediation services — clients who discover a dissolution through the monitoring dashboard generate one-time reinstatement assistance fees.
**Biggest risk:** A missed annual report filing that results in administrative dissolution — discovered by a third party (a lender, counterparty, or plaintiff) rather than by the service's monitoring before it causes harm — creates significant liability. Build the good standing monitoring with automated daily SOS database queries for each registered entity (not just manual calendar-based checks), so status changes are detected within 24 hours of the state updating its records.
**Growth path:** Side project → 30-state annual report calendar, CTA BOI tracker, and SOS good standing monitor deployed, first 5-10 business retainers through attorney and registered agent partner outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 15-20 retainers, franchise operator client tier added, first administrative dissolution reinstatement assistance packages completed (~$5-8K/mo). ### 5. Continuing Care Retirement Community State Licensing & Actuarial Disclosure Compliance
**Problem:** Continuing care retirement communities (CCRCs), also called Life Plan Communities, provide independent living, assisted living, and skilled nursing care within a single campus for residents who pay large entrance fees (typically $100,000-$500,000+) in exchange for a guaranteed continuum of care as their needs increase. CCRCs are regulated in most states by the Department of Insurance or Department of Health under state-specific CCRC licensing acts. Compliance obligations vary by state but commonly include: annual disclosure statements (prepared on a state-mandated form) showing the CCRC's audited financials, actuarial reserve analysis confirming the entrance fee reserves are adequate to fund expected future care costs, resident and applicant fee schedules, and refund policy disclosures; entrance fee escrow requirements (some states require a portion of each entrance fee to be held in escrow until the resident occupies the unit); annual actuarial study submissions (many states require an independent actuary's certification that the CCRC's existing reserves are sufficient to meet long-term care obligations under the current resident population's demographic assumptions); and disclosure of any material changes to the CCRC's financial condition, ownership, or continuing care agreements between annual disclosure filing dates. Small to mid-size CCRCs (50-300 units, operated by faith-based organizations, nonprofit community foundations, or small regional operators) have a CFO and administrator managing these obligations alongside daily operations, frequently with outdated disclosure statements, lapsed actuarial study submissions, and inadequate escrow tracking.
**What to build:** A CCRC state licensing and actuarial disclosure compliance management service — a state CCRC licensing requirement database (the specific annual disclosure statement format, actuarial study requirements, entrance fee escrow rules, and material change disclosure requirements for each of the 38 states that regulate CCRCs under a CCRC or continuing care licensing statute), an annual disclosure statement preparation workflow (collecting the required financial and operational data for the state disclosure form — resident census, entrance fee revenue and refunds, audited financial summary, actuarial reserve status, and fee schedule disclosures — and generating the completed disclosure statement for the CCRC's review and regulatory submission), an entrance fee escrow tracking system (tracking entrance fee receipts, the escrow obligation for each entrance fee under the state's escrow requirement, current escrow account balance, and the escrow release schedule as occupancy triggers are met), an actuarial study coordination workflow (managing the actuarial study engagement timeline: selecting the actuarial engagement scope, collecting the resident demographic and care cost data the actuary requires, coordinating the actuarial report review, and managing the state submission of the completed actuarial study within the required filing deadline), and a material change monitoring and disclosure system (tracking events that trigger a material change disclosure obligation — significant changes in financial condition, ownership changes, modifications to continuing care agreements, and changes to the fee schedule — and generating the state-required material change disclosure within the required filing window).
**Skill fit:** State CCRC licensing statutes and disclosure statement forms are publicly available from each state's insurance or health department — building a state-by-state CCRC compliance requirement database from these sources is a regulatory research problem. The disclosure statement preparation workflow (collecting structured financial and operational data and mapping it to each state's disclosure form fields), entrance fee escrow tracking, and material change monitoring system are data management and compliance calendar problems the builder's engineering skills address directly. LeadingAge (the national association for nonprofit aging services providers, including CCRCs) publishes state regulatory tracking for CCRC licensing requirements that provides a starting framework for the state compliance database.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a compliance requirement database for the 15 states with the largest CCRC markets (FL, PA, OH, CA, IL, NC, VA, TX, MD, MA, MN, IN, MI, MO, WA), an annual disclosure statement data collection template for those states, an entrance fee escrow tracking system, a material change disclosure workflow, and an actuarial study coordination timeline; first 3-5 CCRC clients through LeadingAge state affiliate outreach while the disclosure statement preparation workflow is validated against real state disclosure form requirements.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through LeadingAge state affiliate networks (LeadingAge's member directory includes CCRCs operated by nonprofit and faith-based organizations — the most compliance-burdened tier because they frequently lack the administrative infrastructure of larger for-profit CCRC chains), CCRC accounting firm referral networks (CPA firms that audit CCRCs and prepare their annual financial statements see the disclosure statement compliance gaps firsthand and actively refer compliance management services to their CCRC clients), and state continuing care administrator association networks (CARF — Commission on Accreditation of Rehabilitation Facilities — accredits CCRCs and maintains networks of CCRC administrators who actively seek compliance management resources).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per CCRC: $600-1,500/mo (depending on unit count and state regulatory complexity — a 75-unit CCRC in a single state: $600/mo; a 250-unit CCRC with multiple care levels, complex actuarial study requirements, and multi-state operating licenses: $1,500/mo). Managing 10-15 CCRC retainers at $950/mo average = **$9,500-14,250/mo**. Actuarial study coordination engagements (one-time $1,000-2,500 for coordinating the annual actuarial study data collection and submission process) generate significant supplemental revenue annually. Reaching 10-15 CCRC retainers requires 3-4 LeadingAge state affiliate relationships and 2-3 CCRC accounting firm referral partnerships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** CCRC annual disclosure statements are required every year in every state where the CCRC is licensed — the disclosure preparation and submission workflow is permanent. Actuarial studies are required annually in most states, creating a predictable annual engagement. Entrance fee collections are continuous as new residents enter the community, generating ongoing escrow tracking obligations. State regulators increasingly scrutinize CCRC actuarial reserve adequacy as the population ages and long-term care costs increase, making systematic compliance documentation more urgently demanded.
**Biggest risk:** An annual disclosure statement that materially misrepresents the CCRC's financial condition or reserve adequacy — whether from inaccurate data provided by the CCRC or from a disclosure preparation error — creates regulatory exposure and potential civil liability to residents and their families. Build the disclosure preparation workflow with an explicit three-party review: the completed disclosure draft is reviewed by the CCRC's CFO, its independent CPA, and its actuarial consultant before submission — documenting each reviewer's sign-off as part of the submission record.
