### 1. Rural Health Clinic (RHC) CMS Certification, Shortage-Designation & Cost Report Compliance
**Problem:** Rural Health Clinics — independent, physician/NP/PA-owned outpatient practices certified under Medicare's Conditions for Certification (42 CFR Part 491) — receive an all-inclusive-rate (AIR) reimbursement well above standard Part B fee-schedule payment, but certification depends on the clinic remaining located in a federal Health Professional Shortage Area, Medically Underserved Area, or Governor-designated shortage area, and these designations expire and require redesignation on their own multi-year cycle set by HRSA — a clinic that lets its underlying shortage designation lapse without a redesignation petition on file is exactly the finding a CMS or state survey-agency review is built to catch, and can trigger decertification with retroactive AIR clawback, reverting every recent claim to the far lower standard fee schedule. Every RHC must also staff a nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or certified nurse midwife on site seeing patients at least 50% of the clinic's operating hours (42 CFR 491.8(a)(3)) — a staffing gap on any given week is exactly the deficiency a periodic Conditions-for-Certification survey checks first — and must file the CMS-222-17 Independent Rural Health Clinic Cost Report to its Medicare Administrative Contractor within 5 months of fiscal year-end, allocating costs to true up the interim AIR payment; a late or rejected cost report can suspend Medicare payments outright. A small 2-4 provider RHC has no CFO or compliance officer and reuses a binder to track a shortage designation, a staffing schedule, and a cost report deadline that all move on independent clocks.
**What to build:** A HRSA shortage-designation current-status tracker cross-referencing the clinic's HPSA/MUA designation against HRSA's public data with an alert issued well before redesignation is due; a mid-level extender staffing-hours ratio calculator confirming the 50% coverage rule is met week over week from a schedule import; and a CMS-222-17 cost report data-collection tracker mapped to the report's worksheets with a filing-deadline countdown.
**Skill fit:** HRSA publishes HPSA/MUA designation status and scoring through a public data feed refreshed on a fixed cycle — pulling a specific clinic's designation and flagging its expiration window is a straightforward data-pipeline problem; the staffing-ratio calculator is deterministic math against an imported schedule; and cost report line-item tracking is structured document and deadline management — all squarely within a data engineer's skill set, with no clinical or CPA credential required, since a licensed accountant still signs and files the report itself while this tool organizes the underlying data.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the HRSA designation tracker with expiration alerting, the staffing-ratio calculator ingesting a weekly schedule import, and the cost report line-item collection template; land the first 5-8 clinics through National Association of Rural Health Clinics (NARHC) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — NARHC's national conference and member directory (the single dedicated professional association built around RHCs), state Offices of Rural Health (every state runs one under HRSA's State Office of Rural Health Program and maintains outreach lists of certified RHCs), and RHC-focused CPA firms and healthcare consultants who prepare the annual cost report and have every incentive to refer a client whose underlying data is already organized.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $25/mo per provider (physician/NP/PA) on staff. Average client (4 providers) = $299 + $100 = $399/mo. 24-30 clinics × $399/mo = **$9,576-11,970/mo**. NARHC estimates roughly 5,000 certified RHCs nationally; reaching 30 is well under 1% penetration.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The shortage-designation redesignation clock runs continuously regardless of anything the clinic does day to day, the mid-level staffing ratio is a rolling weekly requirement for as long as the clinic wants to keep its certification, and the cost report cycle repeats every single fiscal year — a clinic that drops the service loses visibility into the exact certification it depends on for its entire reimbursement premium.
**Biggest risk:** A shortage designation that lapses without a redesignation petition on file triggers CMS decertification and retroactive AIR clawback — exactly what the tracker's early-warning alert, timed to HRSA's typical redesignation review window, is built to prevent by surfacing the deadline well before the clinic would otherwise learn about it from a CMS notice.
**Growth path:** Side project → HRSA tracker, staffing calculator, and cost report template deployed, first 5-8 clinic retainers through NARHC outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-17 clinics. Replaces-full-time-salary → 24-30 clinics, pushing past $9.58-11.97K/mo.

### 2. Video Gaming Terminal (VGT) Route Operator State Gaming Board License & Revenue Reporting Compliance
**Problem:** In states that have legalized video gaming terminals in bars, restaurants, truck stops, and fraternal or veterans clubs — Illinois under the Video Gaming Act (230 ILCS 40, administered by the Illinois Gaming Board), Pennsylvania under Act 42 of 2017, and comparable route-based programs in Montana, Louisiana, and Georgia — a route operator holds its own state gaming board terminal-operator license while every individual host establishment and every technician who services a terminal independently holds and renews a separate license, each on its own background-investigation cycle. Every terminal's net terminal income must be electronically transmitted through the state's Central Communications System (CCS), and the operator's internal accounting must reconcile exactly against the CCS-reported figures before the statutory revenue split (state, municipality, establishment, operator) is paid out — a discrepancy between an operator's own records and the CCS feed is exactly the finding a state gaming board field audit is built to catch, and unresolved revenue-reporting mismatches have led to operator license suspensions. Dispatching a technician who isn't independently licensed to service a terminal is itself a separate violation. A small route operator running terminals across 15-40 establishments manages per-establishment license renewal, per-terminal CCS reconciliation, and per-technician license currency with nothing but a spreadsheet the owner reconciles by hand against the state's monthly report.
**What to build:** A per-establishment and per-technician license renewal tracker spanning each license class's independent renewal cycle; a CCS revenue reconciliation tool importing the state's monthly net-terminal-income report and flagging any establishment where internal terminal-count or revenue records don't match; and a per-technician license currency tracker with a "cleared to dispatch" gate before a service ticket can be assigned.
**Skill fit:** State gaming board licensing cycles are public, structured per-license-class data, and several states' CCS platforms publish downloadable location- and terminal-level monthly reports — building an import pipeline that reconciles that report against the operator's internal terminal roster is a data-engineering problem, and the technician-dispatch gate is the same deterministic license-check pattern this series builds repeatedly. No gaming license of any kind is required to sell compliance software to a licensed operator.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license tracker across establishment/technician classes, the CCS report import with reconciliation flagging, and the technician dispatch gate; land the first 5-8 route operators through state coin-machine and gaming-route trade associations ahead of a licensing renewal cycle.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state gaming-route trade associations (such as the Illinois Coin Machine Operators Association, whose membership is almost entirely licensed VGT route operators), the public state gaming board licensee roster (a built-in prospecting list of every licensed operator by name), and licensed-establishment trade groups (state restaurant, tavern, and fraternal associations whose venue members are exactly the host locations route operators service, useful for warm introductions).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $10/mo per active host establishment. Average client (25 establishments) = $299 + $250 = $549/mo. 17-22 operators × $549/mo = **$9,333-12,078/mo**. Illinois alone licenses several hundred route operators serving thousands of establishments statewide, and Pennsylvania, Montana, Louisiana, and Georgia add further reachable markets.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Revenue reconciliation reruns every single CCS reporting cycle without exception, license renewals recur on independent clocks per license class, and every new establishment added to the route opens its own location-license and terminal-reconciliation setup — a continuous compliance stream that scales directly with route growth.
**Biggest risk:** A CCS reconciliation mismatch left unresolved past the state's audit window is exactly the finding that has triggered gaming board license suspensions — the reconciliation tool's monthly flag-before-filing design is the core protection, surfacing a discrepancy report the operator can resolve before the state ever sees one.
**Growth path:** Side project → license tracker, CCS reconciliation tool, and dispatch gate deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through trade-association outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 operators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 17-22 operators, pushing past $9.33-12.08K/mo.

### 3. Bulk Fuel & Chemical Storage Facility EPA SPCC Plan & Aboveground Storage Tank Compliance
**Problem:** Any facility storing oil in aboveground containers exceeding 1,320 gallons aggregate capacity with a reasonable expectation of discharge reaching navigable waters — bulk heating-oil and fuel distributors, farms with on-site diesel storage (explicitly brought into scope by EPA's 2016 farm-rule clarification), construction-equipment fueling yards, and standby-generator fuel depots — must maintain a written Spill Prevention, Control, and Countermeasure (SPCC) Plan under 40 CFR Part 112. Facilities storing more than 10,000 gallons aggregate must have that Plan certified by a licensed Professional Engineer, but every covered facility regardless of size must operate the Plan continuously: a documented visual integrity inspection of each container and its secondary containment on a monthly or facility-defined "regular" schedule, an annual employee spill-prevention training session logged by attendee, and a mandatory 5-year Plan review-and-recertification whether or not anything about the facility changed. A facility that can't produce its container-inspection log during an EPA site visit, or whose Plan is past its 5-year review date, is exactly the finding an SPCC inspection is built to catch, and EPA's civil penalty authority runs into tens of thousands of dollars per day of violation. A small bulk-fuel distributor or farm running 4-10 aboveground tanks across one or more yards manages per-tank inspection currency, the facility's own 5-year review clock, and annual training documentation with nothing but a clipboard hung on the fence at the tank farm.
**What to build:** A per-tank monthly visual-inspection log with mobile-friendly entry and a completion-gap alert; a facility SPCC Plan 5-year review-cycle tracker; and an annual employee spill-prevention training roster with a per-employee currency check.
**Skill fit:** 40 CFR Part 112's inspection frequency, training cadence, and 5-year review cycle are public, structured federal requirements with a well-defined per-tank and per-employee record model, and the inspection-log tracker plus the review-cycle tracker are the same deterministic "log the recurring check on schedule / compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a bulk-storage trade most small operators currently track on a clipboard with no systematic reminder before the 5-year Plan review lapses.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the mobile per-tank inspection log with gap alerting, the Plan review-cycle tracker, and the training roster; land the first 5-8 facilities through Petroleum Marketers Association of America (PMAA) state-affiliate outreach and state Farm Bureau agribusiness networks.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state petroleum marketers associations affiliated with PMAA (whose member directories are small and mid-size heating-oil and bulk-fuel distributors), state Farm Bureau chapters and agribusiness retailer associations (farms crossing the 1,320-gallon threshold are a large, newly-in-scope segment since the 2016 farm-rule clarification), and environmental-compliance consultants who write the initial PE-certified Plan and have every incentive to refer ongoing operational tracking rather than take on monthly inspection liability themselves.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $229/mo base + $18/mo per tank tracked. Average client (6 tanks) = $229 + $108 = $337/mo. 28-35 facilities × $337/mo = **$9,436-11,795/mo**. PMAA's state affiliates alone represent thousands of small bulk-fuel distributors before farm and equipment-yard operators are even counted.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every tank's monthly inspection recurs on its own clock indefinitely, the facility's 5-year Plan review is a standing recurring event regardless of any operational change, and every new tank added to the yard opens its own inspection-log line — a continuous compliance stream tied directly to the facility's storage footprint.
**Biggest risk:** A missing month in the inspection log, or a Plan past its 5-year review date, is exactly the paperwork gap an EPA inspector checks first and the one that turns a routine site visit into a penalty action — the inspection-log gap alert and the review-cycle tracker are the core protections, flagging both well before any announced or unannounced EPA visit.
**Growth path:** Side project → inspection log, review-cycle tracker, and training roster deployed, first 5-8 facility retainers through PMAA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 facilities. Replaces-full-time-salary → 28-35 facilities, pushing past $9.44-11.80K/mo.

### 4. USDA National Organic Program (NOP) Certification Maintenance for Organic Farms, Processors & Handlers
**Problem:** Any operation selling more than $5,000/year in products labeled "organic" — crop farms, livestock operations, and food handlers or processors — must be certified by a USDA-accredited certifying agent under 7 CFR Part 205, maintaining a written Organic System Plan (OSP) documenting every input and process, passing an annual on-site inspection, and submitting an annual OSP update before the certifier will renew certification for the coming year — miss the update deadline and certification lapses, meaning every sale made afterward under an "organic" label is a false claim. NOP requires a documented audit trail linking every organic input purchased to every unit of organic product sold, so a certifier — or, since USDA's 2024 Strengthening Organic Enforcement (SOE) rule tightened supply-chain traceability, an NOP compliance investigator — can trace any lot back to its source; an operation that can't produce that trail during an inspection is exactly the finding that generates a formal Notice of Noncompliance, and repeat findings can escalate to certification revocation and a 5-year disqualification from reapplying. Every purchased input must also be verified against the National List of Allowed and Prohibited Substances (commonly via OMRI listing), since an unapproved input found in storage is treated the same as one found in use. A small diversified organic farm or handler running several product lines through a season tracks OSP currency, its mass-balance audit trail, and input-approval documentation with nothing but a filing folder the owner brings to the annual inspection.
**What to build:** An OSP annual-update deadline tracker tied to the operation's specific certifier's renewal cycle; a mass-balance audit trail linking input purchase records to output sales lots with a gap flag when a lot can't be traced to an approved input; and an input-approval documentation library checking each purchased input against the National List/OMRI status before it's logged as usable.
**Skill fit:** The National List and OMRI's approved-input database are public, structured reference data, and the mass-balance trail is fundamentally a ledger-reconciliation problem — input purchased in, output sold out, matched by lot — the same "cross-check two records that must match" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an agricultural-certification trade most small organic operations currently track in a filing folder with no systematic link between a specific purchased input and the sold lot it went into.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the OSP deadline tracker, the mass-balance ledger with lot-matching and gap alerts, and the input-approval library cross-checked against OMRI's public list; land the first 5-8 operations through state organic farming association outreach ahead of a certification renewal season.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state organic farming associations (MOSES, CCOF, and NOFA state chapters are dense, active communities of small certified organic operations that already discuss OSP updates and inspection prep constantly), certifying agent referral relationships (accredited agents like Oregon Tilth and QAI see the same audit-trail gaps at every inspection and have every incentive to refer a tool that makes their own inspections faster), and state department of agriculture organic cost-share program administrators (the offices that process NOP certification cost-share reimbursement interact with every certified operation in the state annually).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $14/mo per active product line or lot category tracked. Average client (7 lines) = $199 + $98 = $297/mo. 33-42 operations × $297/mo = **$9,801-12,474/mo**. USDA counts roughly 17,000+ certified organic operations nationally; reaching 42 is a small fraction of that base.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The OSP renews every single year without exception, every new harvest or production lot reopens its own mass-balance trail, and every new input purchase reopens its own approval-documentation check — a continuous compliance stream tied to the operation's growing season and product mix.
**Biggest risk:** An output lot that can't be traced back to an approved input during the annual inspection is exactly the finding that generates a formal Notice of Noncompliance and, on repeat, can trigger a 5-year disqualification from recertifying — the mass-balance gap flag is the core protection, catching a broken trail before the certifier's inspector does.
**Growth path:** Side project → OSP tracker, mass-balance ledger, and input library deployed, first 5-8 operation retainers through state organic association outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-23 operations. Replaces-full-time-salary → 33-42 operations, pushing past $9.80-12.47K/mo.

### 5. CPA Firm AICPA Peer Review Program & Multi-State CPA Mobility/CPE Compliance
**Problem:** Any CPA firm performing audits, reviews, or compilations under SSARS/SAS standards must undergo an external Peer Review under the AICPA Peer Review Program, administered on the firm's behalf by its state CPA society acting as the Administering Entity, on a mandatory triennial cycle — a firm whose peer review lapses past its due date is reported to its state Board of Accountancy as noncompliant, which in most states independently jeopardizes the firm's license to practice, not just its ability to keep doing attest work. A review that comes back "pass with deficiencies" or "fail" further obligates the firm to complete and document a formal Corrective Action Plan (CAP) within a fixed window, and a firm that can't demonstrate CAP completion at its next review is escalated to Enhanced Oversight — a materially more expensive and disruptive review track. Separately, every individual CPA on staff must track continuing professional education against their home state board's specific triennial hour requirement (commonly 120 hours per 3-year rolling period, with state-specific minimums on Ethics and A&A hours), and firms registered in more than one state under the Uniform Accountancy Act's mobility provisions must still track each state's own permit-to-practice renewal independently, since mobility privileges cover individual practice, not the firm's own registration. A small 3-8 partner attest firm manages its own triennial peer-review clock, CAP documentation, and per-CPA multi-state CPE currency with nothing but the office manager's shared spreadsheet.
**What to build:** A firm-level peer review triennial-cycle tracker with a Corrective Action Plan documentation workflow when a review isn't a clean pass; a per-CPA multi-state CPE hour tracker mapping completed credits against each state board's category minimums; and a multi-state firm-permit renewal calendar for firms registered in more than one state.
**Skill fit:** State board CPE requirements and the AICPA/NASBA peer review cycle rules are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-firm and per-individual record model, and the CPE-hour tracker plus the peer-review-cycle tracker are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / cross-check completed hours against required category minimums" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a licensed-professional-services trade most small attest firms currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic alert before the triennial review or a specific CPA's CPE period comes due.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the peer review cycle tracker with the CAP workflow, the per-CPA CPE tracker mapped to the firm's licensed states, and the multi-state permit renewal calendar; land the first 5-8 firms through state CPA society small-firm and sole-practitioner member sections.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state CPA society small-firm and sole-practitioner sections (nearly every state society runs a dedicated practice-management community for firms under 10 partners, where peer review anxiety is a constant discussion topic), Peer Review Administering Entity referral relationships (the state society staff running the peer review program see every firm's compliance gap directly and have every incentive to refer a tool that reduces late or failed reviews on their books), and CPA firm succession and practice-brokerage networks (brokers helping small firms merge or sell see peer review and CPE gaps as a recurring due-diligence finding and refer tracking tools to firms cleaning up before a sale).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $22/mo per licensed CPA on staff. Average client (5 CPAs) = $249 + $110 = $359/mo. 26-33 firms × $359/mo = **$9,334-11,847/mo**. There are tens of thousands of CPA firms performing attest work nationally under mandatory peer review; reaching 33 is a negligible fraction.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The triennial peer review clock resets every 3 years without exception, every CPA's individual CPE period runs on its own rolling clock, and every new state registration opens its own permit-renewal cycle — continuous, layered compliance that scales directly with firm headcount and multi-state footprint.
**Biggest risk:** A peer review that lapses past its due date is reported to the state Board of Accountancy as noncompliant and can independently jeopardize the firm's license — the cycle tracker's early alert, set to the AICPA's own recommended review-scheduling window, is the core protection, giving the firm months to schedule the review instead of discovering the lapse from a Board notice.
**Growth path:** Side project → peer review tracker, CPE tracker, and permit calendar deployed, first 5-8 firm retainers through state CPA society outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 firms. Replaces-full-time-salary → 26-33 firms, pushing past $9.33-11.85K/mo.
