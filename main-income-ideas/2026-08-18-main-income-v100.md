# Main Income Ideas - 2026-08-18 (Session 100)

## Who this is for
A 29-year-old with software engineering and data analytics skills who wants to replace a full-time salary with a solo-buildable SaaS business. Target: $8-15K+/mo within 12-24 months, built evenings and weekends with no outside funding, with a realistic path to first paying customer inside 90 days.

## Ground rules for every idea in this series
- Solo-buildable start. One person, no co-founder, no funding round, nights-and-weekends MVP.
- Realistic path to $8-15K+/mo through a monthly-retainer SaaS model with a defensible niche.
- Time to first dollar < 90 days from the day coding starts.
- Named distribution channel — a specific professional association, licensing board mailing list, trade publication, or conference where the first cohort of paying customers can actually be found.
- No repeats. This session covers five regulated industries never addressed in Sessions 1-99: (1) medical spas (med spas) offering laser hair removal, IPL, chemical peels, and injectables under physician-delegation/collaborative-practice-agreement supervision and state Class IIIb/IV laser device registration — distinct from every prior aesthetics-adjacent idea (tattoo/permanent cosmetics in Session 95 tracks state body-art licensure and bloodborne pathogen rules for needle-based ink work, not physician oversight of energy-based devices or Rx-only injectables); (2) substance use disorder (SUD) treatment facilities and opioid treatment programs (OTPs) under SAMHSA 42 CFR Part 2 confidentiality rules and DEA registration for medication-assisted treatment — a behavioral-health category never touched by this repo, distinct from the IDD residential group home idea in Session 99 (which tracks HCBS waiver/ISP compliance for developmental disability services, not substance-use confidentiality or narcotic-dispensing DEA registration); (3) CLIA-certified clinical laboratories (blood draw labs, reference labs, point-of-care testing sites) under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments — an entirely new regulatory framework (CMS CLIA certificate levels, proficiency testing, personnel qualification rules) never addressed, distinct from the rural health clinic idea in Session 97 (CMS cost-report and shortage-designation compliance for the clinic as a whole, not laboratory-specific CLIA certificate and PT compliance); (4) crane and rigging contractors under OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart CC and NCCCO operator/signalperson/rigger certification — a new construction-trade category distinct from every prior contractor idea in this series (asbestos abatement in Session 98 tracks NESHAP notification and worker air-monitoring, not crane certification currency or annual inspection/load-test documentation); (5) state-licensed cannabis retailers and cultivators under state cannabis control boards and seed-to-sale track-and-trace systems (e.g., METRC, BioTrackTHC) — a first-time category for this repo, distinct from every prior retail/agriculture idea (the fireworks retailer in Session 96 tracks state fire marshal storage-quantity limits, not plant-tagging chain-of-custody or cannabis excise tax filings).

## Ideas

### 1. Med Spa Physician Oversight & Laser Device Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Medical spas offering laser hair removal, IPL photofacials, chemical peels, microneedling, and Botox/filler injectables operate in a legal gray zone that varies wildly by state: most states require a supervising physician (or, in delegation states, a medical director) to sign a written collaborative practice agreement or standing order authorizing each aesthetic RN, NP, PA, or laser technician to perform specific procedures, and that authorization has to be renewed and kept on file for state medical board audits. Separately, Class IIIb and Class IV lasers (used for hair removal and skin resurfacing) require state registration under state radiation control programs (many states model this on the FDA's 21 CFR 1040 performance standard) with device-specific certificates, annual output verification, and operator training logs. A med spa running five treatment rooms with a rotating roster of contract RNs and a part-time medical director has no single system tracking which staff member is credentialed for which procedure, whether the medical director's supervision agreement has lapsed, or whether laser registrations are current — and a lapsed physician agreement voids the entire practice's ability to legally perform Rx-only procedures, which is exactly what state medical boards check first during a complaint-triggered inspection.
**What to build:** A staff credential matrix mapping each aesthetician/RN/NP to the specific procedures they're authorized to perform under the current collaborative practice agreement, with expiration alerts before the medical director agreement or individual scope-of-practice addendum lapses; a laser/device registry tracking each machine's state registration number, annual calibration/output verification date, and required operator-training completion per device; an injectable Rx-tracking log linking each Botox/filler lot number to the ordering physician and the treating injector for adverse-event traceability; and a pre-inspection packet generator that compiles the current medical director agreement, device registrations, and staff scope documents into one exportable file.
**Skill fit:** State laser/radiation-device registries and medical board delegation-of-services rules are published, structured public data (per-state radiation control program websites, medical board practice acts) that map cleanly onto a relational credential-matrix schema — exactly the kind of many-to-many licensing/authorization problem a data-oriented engineer can model well, without needing clinical training.
**MVP scope:** Build the staff-credential matrix and laser-device registry with expiration alerting for a single state in the first two weekends (state med-spa regulation research plus a Postgres schema and renewal-alert cron job), then add the injectable lot-tracking log and inspection-packet export in weekend three. Get the first 5-8 med spas from a state's independent med spa owner Facebook group and by cold-emailing spas listed on the American Med Spa Association (AmSpa) member directory, offering a 30-day free pilot in exchange for a case study.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribution through the American Med Spa Association (AmSpa), which runs regional conferences and a large online community of independent med spa owners specifically anxious about compliance audits after several high-profile state board crackdowns.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $179-249/mo per location depending on device/staff count. 55 med spas × $220/mo average = $12,100/mo. AmSpa alone represents thousands of member locations, and most metro areas have 10-30 independent (non-franchise) med spas, so 55 clients across a few states is a realistic 18-24 month target for one operator working referrals and conference booths.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Staff turnover is high in this industry (contract RNs rotate between spas constantly), so the credential matrix needs continuous updating every time a new hire or contractor is onboarded; laser registrations and physician agreements renew annually or biennially depending on state, generating recurring alert-driven logins; and injectable lot-tracking is a per-treatment workflow, not a set-and-forget filing — all of which make this a daily-use operational tool rather than an annual-renewal checkbox.
**Biggest risk:** A med spa might treat this as a nice-to-have rather than something they'll pay $200+/mo for, since many currently track this informally in spreadsheets or not at all. The product counters this by leading sales conversations with a specific state medical board enforcement action (most states have had at least one high-profile med spa license revocation for unsupervised injectables in the last two years) and positioning the tool as cheaper than a single day of legal consultation after a board complaint.
**Growth path:** Side project → 15 clients ($3,300/mo) → replaces-part-time-income at 35 clients ($7,700/mo) → adds multi-state device-registry rule packs → replaces-full-time-salary at 55-65 clients ($12-14K/mo).

### 2. Substance Use Disorder Treatment Facility 42 CFR Part 2 & DEA Registration Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Outpatient and residential substance use disorder (SUD) treatment programs, including opioid treatment programs (OTPs) dispensing methadone or buprenorphine, operate under one of the strictest confidentiality regimes in healthcare: 42 CFR Part 2, which governs how patient records can be disclosed and requires specific consent-form language for every third-party release (far stricter than standard HIPAA), plus state SUD facility licensure, DEA registration renewal for any site dispensing controlled substances for MAT, and SAMHSA OTP certification renewal (42 CFR Part 8) which requires accreditation body (CARF or Joint Commission) survey documentation on file. A 20-bed residential SUD facility or a three-location OTP network has counselors handling dozens of consent-to-release forms per client, a DEA registration that must be renewed and posted per dispensing location, and an accreditation survey cycle that determines whether SAMHSA certification stays active — miss the accreditation renewal deadline and the facility loses its ability to bill Medicaid for services, which is often the majority of a nonprofit SUD provider's revenue.
**What to build:** A per-client 42 CFR Part 2 consent-form tracker that flags which releases are active, expired, or missing required elements (purpose of disclosure, specific recipient, revocation right) before an audit or accreditation survey; a facility-level DEA registration and state SUD license renewal calendar per physical location; a SAMHSA/CARF/Joint Commission accreditation cycle tracker with survey-prep checklists tied to the specific standards being assessed; and a staff certification tracker for counselors requiring state-specific SUD counselor credentials (CADC, LADC, etc.) with continuing-education-hour tracking.
**Skill fit:** This is fundamentally a document-lifecycle and deadline-tracking problem layered over publicly available regulatory text (42 CFR Part 2, Part 8, and state SUD licensing rules) — well suited to an engineer who can build structured checklists and expiration-alert logic without needing clinical judgment, since the product never touches actual patient treatment content, only the metadata (dates, consent status, registration numbers) around it.
**MVP scope:** First two weekends: build the DEA/state-license renewal calendar and the accreditation-cycle tracker for a single state's SUD facility licensing rules. Third weekend: add the consent-form metadata tracker (status/expiration only, no PHI content, to keep the MVP outside Part 2's own disclosure restrictions). Recruit first 5-8 clients from a state association of addiction treatment providers, offering a free pilot to a facility administrator willing to give feedback.
**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — distribution through the National Association of Addiction Treatment Providers (NAATP) and state-level behavioral health provider associations, which run active compliance-focused newsletters given how frequently SUD facilities face licensing actions.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-399/mo per facility depending on bed count/location count. 40 facilities × $300/mo average = $12,000/mo. There are thousands of licensed SUD treatment facilities nationally, and a niche tool addressing accreditation-and-DEA deadline risk is a straightforward sell to administrators who have lost certification before.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** DEA registrations renew every three years per location but require annual attestation checks in many states, accreditation surveys cycle every 1-3 years with extensive document-prep work each time, and staff counselor certifications typically require continuing education tracked annually — creating a steady cadence of renewal events that keep facility administrators logging in rather than a one-time setup.
**Biggest risk:** SUD facilities are often underfunded nonprofits sensitive to new software spend. The product counters this by pricing per-facility rather than per-bed and leading with the cost of a single lapsed accreditation cycle (which can mean months of Medicaid billing suspension) versus the subscription cost.
**Growth path:** Side project → 12 clients ($3,600/mo) → replaces-part-time-income at 25 clients ($7,500/mo) → adds OTP-specific DEA dispensing-site module → replaces-full-time-salary at 40 clients ($12K/mo).

### 3. CLIA-Certified Clinical Laboratory Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Any facility performing lab testing on human specimens — blood-draw labs, urgent-care point-of-care testing sites, employer drug-testing collection sites doing on-site screening, dermatology-practice pathology labs, fertility clinic andrology labs — must hold a CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments) certificate from CMS at the level matching its test complexity (Certificate of Waiver, Certificate of Compliance, or Certificate of Accreditation), and moderate/high-complexity labs must enroll in a CMS-approved proficiency testing (PT) program for every regulated analyte, with PT samples run and results submitted on a strict quarterly schedule. Labs also need documented personnel qualification records (director, technical supervisor, testing personnel each have specific CLIA-defined education/experience requirements under 42 CFR 493 Subpart M) and a quality control/quality assurance plan reviewed on a set cycle. A small independent lab or a multi-site urgent care chain running its own point-of-care testing has no unified way to track which CLIA certificate covers which location, whether this quarter's PT samples were submitted on time for every analyte, or whether a newly hired technician's qualifications have been verified and filed before they run their first test — and a missed PT event or an unqualified tester running an assay is exactly what triggers CMS certificate revocation.
**What to build:** A per-location CLIA certificate tracker with certificate level, covered test menu, and renewal date (CLIA certificates renew every two years); a quarterly proficiency-testing calendar per regulated analyte with submission-deadline alerts and pass/fail history; a personnel-qualification file per tester mapping their credentials against the specific CLIA complexity category they're authorized to test at; and a QC/QA review-cycle tracker generating the documentation packet CMS or a state survey agency requests during an on-site inspection.
**Skill fit:** CLIA's personnel-qualification tables and PT-analyte requirements are published, tabular federal regulation (42 CFR 493) that translates directly into a rules engine — a strong fit for a developer who can encode structured eligibility logic and build calendar-driven alerting without clinical lab expertise.
**MVP scope:** Weekends one and two: build the certificate tracker and PT-analyte calendar for moderate-complexity labs (the largest segment). Weekend three: add the personnel-qualification matching engine. Land first 5-8 clients by reaching out to independent reference labs and multi-site urgent care groups running in-house point-of-care testing, offering a pilot ahead of their next PT cycle.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribution through the American Association of Bioanalysts (AAB) and COLA (a CMS-approved lab accreditor with a large member base of small/independent labs), both of which run compliance-focused webinars and newsletters this product could sponsor or be referenced in.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-349/mo per lab site depending on complexity level and analyte count. 50 lab sites × $260/mo average = $13,000/mo. There are tens of thousands of CLIA-certified labs nationally (including every urgent care and many specialty practices running in-house testing), making 50 sites a realistic multi-year target through association channels.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Proficiency testing runs quarterly for every regulated analyte, personnel files need updates every time a lab hires new testing staff, and CLIA certificates renew every two years with a full re-attestation — a rhythm of recurring, audit-critical events that keeps lab managers checking in well beyond initial setup.
**Biggest risk:** Larger hospital-affiliated labs already use enterprise LIS/QMS platforms with built-in CLIA modules, so this product needs to stay focused on the independent and small-multi-site segment that can't justify that spend. The MVP and marketing should explicitly target labs under 10 locations where a $250/mo point solution beats a $50K+ enterprise QMS contract.
**Growth path:** Side project → 15 clients ($3,900/mo) → replaces-part-time-income at 30 clients ($7,800/mo) → adds high-complexity/CAP-accreditation rule pack → replaces-full-time-salary at 50 clients ($13K/mo).

### 4. Crane & Rigging Contractor NCCCO Certification & OSHA Subpart CC Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Crane operation on construction sites is governed by OSHA's 29 CFR 1926 Subpart CC, which requires every operator to hold current certification from an accredited body (most commonly NCCCO — the National Commission for the Certification of Crane Operators) specific to the crane type and capacity they operate, plus separate signalperson and rigger certifications for the ground crew coordinating each lift. Cranes themselves require annual inspections by a qualified person, more frequent (monthly) inspections with documented findings, and a pre-lift plan for critical or complex lifts (capacity near the crane's rated limit, multiple cranes, or lifts near power lines). A crane rental/operating company running a fleet of 10-15 cranes across multiple job sites has operators whose NCCCO certifications (which expire every five years and require recertification testing) can lapse mid-project, annual and monthly inspection paperwork scattered across job-site binders, and no centralized way to confirm before dispatching a crew that every operator and rigger assigned to tomorrow's lift is currently certified for that specific equipment — and OSHA cites operator-certification and inspection-documentation gaps as leading causes of crane-related violations and site shutdowns.
**What to build:** An operator/signalperson/rigger certification registry tracking each worker's NCCCO certification type, capacity class, and five-year expiration with recertification-window alerts; a per-crane inspection log tracking annual qualified-person inspections and monthly documented inspections against OSHA's required checklist items; a pre-lift plan template generator for critical lifts requiring engineering sign-off; and a dispatch-eligibility check that flags before a job is scheduled if the assigned operator's certification doesn't match the crane class or has lapsed.
**Skill fit:** NCCCO's certification categories and OSHA Subpart CC's inspection-interval requirements are structured, publicly documented rules that map directly onto an eligibility-matching engine — the same pattern as license-to-assignment gating used across compliance tools, well suited to a data-oriented engineer without construction-safety credentials.
**MVP scope:** First two weekends: build the certification registry with expiration alerting and the per-crane inspection log. Third weekend: add the dispatch-eligibility check matching operator certification to crane class. Get first 5-8 clients from crane rental and heavy-lift contractor companies found through state crane operator associations, offering a free pilot ahead of an upcoming OSHA-mandated inspection cycle.
**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — distribution through the Specialized Carriers & Rigging Association (SC&RA), the primary trade association for crane rental and rigging contractors, which runs an annual conference and active safety-compliance publications this audience already reads.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-499/mo per contractor company based on fleet size. 30 contractors × $380/mo average = $11,400/mo. Crane rental and heavy-rigging is a specialized but nationally distributed trade with thousands of operating companies, and safety/certification tracking is a well-recognized pain point given how directly it ties to OSHA liability.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** NCCCO certifications recertify every five years but require operators to log continuing experience hours, monthly crane inspections are a recurring documented event for every piece of equipment in the fleet, and every new critical lift needs a fresh pre-lift plan — a genuinely continuous operational workflow rather than an annual filing.
**Biggest risk:** Larger national crane operators already run fleet-management software with safety modules bundled in. The product should target the mid-size regional crane rental/rigging contractor (5-25 cranes) who is too small for enterprise fleet software but has real OSHA exposure without dedicated safety staff.
**Growth path:** Side project → 10 clients ($3,800/mo) → replaces-part-time-income at 20 clients ($7,600/mo) → adds multi-state DOT/oversize-load permit module → replaces-full-time-salary at 30 clients ($11.4K/mo).

### 5. Licensed Cannabis Retailer & Cultivator Seed-to-Sale Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** State-licensed cannabis businesses — dispensaries, cultivators, and processors — operate under dual compliance burdens that don't exist in almost any other retail category: a state cannabis control board license (with its own renewal cycle, often annual, tied to specific premises and ownership disclosures) and mandatory participation in a state-mandated seed-to-sale track-and-trace system (METRC in most states, BioTrackTHC in a handful of others) where every plant, harvest batch, and product package must be tagged and its chain of custody logged from seed to final retail sale, with daily reconciliation required between physical inventory and the state system. On top of that, most states require detailed monthly or quarterly excise/cannabis tax filings calculated on wholesale or retail transaction value, and dispensaries need current employee "badge" or agent-card registrations for every budtender on the floor. A small cultivator or single-location dispensary manually reconciling METRC tag counts against physical inventory, tracking each employee's state agent-card expiration, and assembling excise tax filings from raw sales data is exposed to steep fines or license suspension for even minor discrepancies, since state cannabis regulators treat inventory variance as a presumptive diversion risk.
**What to build:** A license and premises-registration tracker covering the state cannabis license renewal cycle and any required local municipal permit; an employee agent-card/badge registry with expiration alerts (required before that employee can legally handle product); a daily inventory-reconciliation dashboard comparing expected METRC tag counts against a manual physical count entry to flag variances before a state audit catches them; and an excise/cannabis tax filing calendar with a worksheet pulling from sales totals to prep the state-specific filing.
**Skill fit:** METRC's tag/manifest data model is a well-documented public API in most states, and state cannabis tax rules are structured, published regulation — a strong fit for an engineer comfortable integrating against a government-adjacent API and building reconciliation/variance-detection logic, a pattern common in inventory and financial-controls software.
**MVP scope:** First two weekends: build the license/agent-card registry and the excise tax filing calendar for one state. Third weekend: add manual inventory-reconciliation entry (defer a live METRC API integration to post-MVP, since state API access requires separate registration) and a variance-flagging dashboard. Land first 5-8 clients from single-location dispensaries and small cultivators in one legal state, offering a free pilot tied to their next tax filing deadline.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribution through state-level cannabis trade associations (most legal states have one, such as the Minority Cannabis Business Association nationally or state-specific groups like the California Cannabis Industry Association), which run active compliance-focused Slack/Discord communities given how punitive state audits can be.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-399/mo per license/location. 40 locations × $310/mo average = $12,400/mo. Legal cannabis states have thousands of licensed retail and cultivation operations, and inventory-variance and tax-filing risk is one of the most-discussed pain points in operator communities.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Daily inventory reconciliation against METRC is a literal daily-use requirement, agent-card renewals recur as staff turn over (a high-turnover retail category), and excise tax filings hit monthly or quarterly depending on state — producing constant, non-optional touchpoints rather than a set-and-forget annual license renewal.
**Biggest risk:** Established seed-to-sale POS platforms (Dutchie, Flowhub) already bundle some compliance features. The product should position as a lightweight compliance-only layer for operators who don't want to switch POS systems, and focus on the reconciliation/variance-detection and tax-filing-prep gap those platforms handle poorly.
**Growth path:** Side project → 12 clients ($3,700/mo) → replaces-part-time-income at 25 clients ($7,750/mo) → adds live METRC API integration → replaces-full-time-salary at 40 clients ($12.4K/mo).
