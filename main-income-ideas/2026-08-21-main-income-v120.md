# Main Income Ideas - 2026-08-21 (Session 120)

## Who this is for
You're 29, you write software and know your way around data — pipelines, scrapers, structured reference data, rules engines. You want to replace a full-time salary ($8-15K+/mo) within 12-24 months with something you build solo in evenings and weekends, no outside funding, no co-founder, and you want the first paying customer inside 90 days. The wedge in this series is always the same: small operators in a regulated industry who have non-optional government or accreditation-body obligations — licenses, permits, certifications, filings, inspections — and no compliance department to track them. You sell a monthly-retainer SaaS that turns their compliance binder into a living calendar with alerts and hard gates. The regulator (or accrediting body) creates the deadline; you sell the peace of mind of never missing it.

  - **Marine terminal operator / stevedoring company** is distinct from the Customs Bonded Warehouse & Foreign Trade Zone Operator idea (Session 119, whose compliance core is CBP storage-period and inventory-reconciliation rules for goods held under bond) and from every vessel-operator idea already in this repo (USCG-inspected passenger vessels, commercial fishing vessels, marine dealers) — a terminal operator instead runs the physical port facility itself under the Maritime Transportation Security Act's Coast Guard-approved Facility Security Plan (33 CFR Part 105), gated by per-worker TSA TWIC card currency and OSHA's maritime longshoring standards (29 CFR Parts 1917/1918), none of which any prior CBP-facility or vessel-operator idea's data model touches.
  - **Used oil collector & recycler** is a first appearance — distinct from the Hazardous Waste Generator idea (RCRA Subtitle C, which used oil is specifically exempted from when Part 279's requirements are met), the Fats/Oil/Grease Hauling & Grease Trap Pumping idea (food-service grease, an entirely different waste stream with its own municipal pretreatment-permit regime), and the Scrap Tire Hauler idea (tires, not lubricating oil).   - **Livestock artificial insemination & embryo-transfer center** is distinct from the Livestock Hauler & Commercial Animal Transporter idea (interstate movement of live animals under USDA APHIS health certificates), the Livestock Auction Market & Stockyard idea (Packers and Stockyards Act bonding for sale transactions), and the Mobile/Ambulatory Large-Animal Veterinary Practice idea (a general large-animal vet's own DEA/APHIS accreditation) — a semen or embryo collection center instead operates as a specialized genetic-material production facility under the National Association of Animal Breeders' Certified Semen Services donor disease-testing protocol, a per-donor retest-and-certification regime none of those three prior livestock ideas' data models has any field for.
  - **Sign language interpreting agency** is distinct from the Court Interpreting Agency idea (spoken-language interpreting under state AOC/FCICE certification for court dispatch) and the Healthcare Medical Interpreter Agency idea (NBCMI/CCHI certification under Title VI language-access rules) — a sign language interpreting agency instead certifies its roster through the Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf's national certification system (or a state Quality Assurance screening-level structure), and, for agencies that also provide Video Relay Service or Video Remote Interpreting, layers on the FCC's own Part 64 certified-provider compliance framework — an accrediting body, dispatch-matching rule, and (for VRS/VRI providers) a federal regulator that neither spoken-language interpreting idea in this repo touches at all.

## Ideas

### 1. Short Line & Regional Freight Railroad FRA Track Safety, Bridge Inspection & Hours-of-Service Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** The roughly 600 short line and regional freight railroads operating in the U.S. — small independent lines running a handful of locomotives over anywhere from a few miles to a couple hundred miles of track, often serving a single industrial customer or connecting to a Class I railroad — hold their operating authority subject to Federal Railroad Administration regulation rather than any state DOT or FMCSA framework a trucking company would recognize. Every segment of owned track carries an FRA Track Safety Standards (49 CFR Part 213) classification (Excepted through Class 5) that sets both the maximum authorized speed and the mandatory internal track-inspection frequency — Class 3 track, for instance, requires a documented walking or vehicle inspection twice weekly, and running trains over track whose inspection has lapsed is a strict-liability finding an FRA track inspector is specifically trained to catch. Every bridge on the line requires an FRA-compliant annual inspection (49 CFR Part 237) by a qualified bridge inspector with a current load-rating on file. Train and engine employees are subject to a railroad-specific hours-of-service limit (49 U.S.C. §21103, distinct from FMCSA's trucking hours-of-service) and a separate FRA drug and alcohol testing program (49 CFR Part 219) with its own random-testing-rate and reasonable-suspicion procedures administered outside any highway-carrier consortium. A small independent short line running a two- or three-person operating crew and a handful of bridges tracks per-segment inspection due dates, annual bridge inspections and load-rating documentation, crew hours-of-service, and Part 219 random-testing-pool selection on paper track-inspection cards and a filing cabinet in the depot office, and a segment run past its inspection interval or a bridge inspected a year late is exactly the finding an FRA regional office audit is built to catch — with consequences ranging from a slow order to a full operating suspension.

**What to build:** A per-track-segment inspection tracker computing each segment's FRA-mandated inspection frequency from its track class and flagging any segment approaching or past its due date; a bridge inventory with annual inspection due dates and load-rating document storage; an hours-of-service log for train/engine crew computing remaining on-duty time under Part 228's limits; a Part 219 random drug/alcohol testing pool-selection and result tracker; and an operating-rules efficiency-testing schedule per employee.

**Skill fit:** FRA's track-class inspection-frequency table, bridge-inspection cadence, and hours-of-service/testing rules are public, structured federal regulation with concrete numeric thresholds distinct from the FMCSA trucking framework this series has already built repeatedly for motor carriers — a genuinely new transportation-mode rules engine, applied to a railroad category this repo has never touched.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the track-segment inspection tracker with the due-date computation, the bridge-inspection tracker, and the hours-of-service/Part 219 testing tracker. First 5-8 railroad clients through direct outreach to independent short lines.

**Time to first $:** 30-60 days — the American Short Line and Regional Railroad Association (ASLRRA) runs an active member directory and annual conference reaching the roughly 600 short line and regional railroads nationally, many of them small independent operators without a dedicated compliance officer.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $400-800/mo per railroad depending on track miles, bridge count, and crew size. 20-25 railroads × $550/mo avg = $11,000-13,750/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every track segment runs its own continuous inspection clock (as often as twice weekly for the busiest track classes), every bridge recurs annually, and hours-of-service is logged every shift a crew works — a permanent, mileage-and-crew-driven compliance stream tied directly to the railroad's FRA operating authority.

**Biggest risk:** The buyer population is finite (~600 short lines nationally) and a meaningful share are owned by larger holding companies (Genesee & Wyoming, Watco, Pinsly) that run in-house compliance staff across their portfolio. Mitigate by explicitly targeting independent, single-line short lines not part of a multi-railroad holding company, where the compliance function is still one operations manager.

**Growth path:** Side project → 8-10 railroads ($4.4-5.5K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 16-18 railroads ($8.8-9.9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 22-25 railroads plus a bridge load-rating documentation vault upsell for lines with older bridge inventories.

### 2. Marine Terminal Operator & Stevedoring Company MTSA Facility Security Plan & TWIC Access-Control Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Marine terminal operators and stevedoring companies that load and unload cargo at U.S. ports operate under the Maritime Transportation Security Act, requiring a Coast Guard-approved Facility Security Plan (33 CFR Part 105) with a designated Facility Security Officer, a specified security-drill schedule (a documented drill at least quarterly and a full exercise at least annually), and access-control procedures verifying that every facility worker and visitor holds a current TSA Transportation Worker Identification Credential (TWIC) before unescorted access to a secure area — a worker whose TWIC expired discovered during a Coast Guard Captain of the Port facility inspection is a direct, strict-liability MTSA violation. Stevedoring and longshoring operations separately fall under OSHA's maritime standards (29 CFR Parts 1917/1918), which set periodic inspection and certification requirements for cargo-handling gear, cranes, and vessel/shore-interface equipment. A small independent terminal operator or stevedoring company running a handful of berths and a rotating dock-worker roster tracks per-worker TWIC expiration dates gating facility access, FSP drill and exercise scheduling, cargo-handling-gear certification renewal, and Facility Security Officer/alternate-FSO training currency — usually on a spreadsheet the terminal superintendent maintains by hand, and a lapsed TWIC or a missed quarterly drill discovered during a Coast Guard inspection is exactly the finding that can suspend a facility's ability to operate.

**What to build:** A per-worker TWIC expiration tracker with an access-eligibility gate blocking a worker from being logged into a secure area once their card lapses; an FSP drill/exercise scheduler computing each quarterly-drill and annual-exercise due date with a completion log for Coast Guard inspection readiness; a cargo-handling-gear and crane certification renewal tracker; and a Facility Security Officer/alternate-FSO training-currency tracker.

**Skill fit:** 33 CFR Part 105's drill/exercise cadence and TWIC's card-validity data are structured federal maritime-security regulation, and the access-eligibility gate — blocking a worker's badge-in until their credential clears — is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a port-facility-security category distinct from every CBP-facility and vessel-operator idea already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the TWIC access-eligibility tracker, the FSP drill/exercise scheduler, and the cargo-gear certification tracker. First 5-8 terminal/stevedoring clients through direct outreach.

**Time to first $:** 30-60 days — the National Association of Waterfront Employers (NAWE) represents marine terminal operators and stevedoring companies nationally and runs direct member outreach reaching exactly the small independent operators at secondary and regional ports who lack a dedicated port-security compliance officer.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $400-900/mo per facility depending on berth count and workforce size. 18-22 terminals × $600/mo avg = $10,800-13,200/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every dockworker's TWIC recurs on its own five-year cycle requiring a continuous access-eligibility check, security drills recur quarterly for the life of the facility's MTSA designation, and cargo-handling gear recertifies on its own inspection interval — a permanent, workforce-and-equipment-driven compliance stream tied to the facility's authority to operate under the Coast Guard's approved plan.

**Biggest risk:** Many of the largest terminals at major ports are run by global operators (Ports America, APM Terminals) with in-house security-compliance teams who won't buy. Mitigate by targeting small independent regional and river-port terminal operators and stevedoring companies at secondary ports, where the FSO is doing this by hand alongside operational duties.

**Growth path:** Side project → 8-10 facilities ($4.8-6K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 14-16 facilities ($8.4-9.6K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 18-22 facilities plus an OSHA maritime cargo-gear certification-renewal add-on.

### 3. Used Oil Collector & Recycler EPA Part 279 Used Oil Management Standards Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Small used-oil collection and recycling businesses — pumping out oil-water separators at auto shops, collecting used motor oil from quick-lube chains and marinas on a route, and aggregating loads for delivery to a permitted re-refiner or burner — operate under EPA's Used Oil Management Standards (40 CFR Part 279), a regulatory track deliberately separate from RCRA hazardous waste because used oil is presumptively recyclable, provided the handler meets Part 279's specific requirements: an EPA identification number as a registered used-oil transporter, on-site storage-tank and container labeling ("Used Oil") with secondary-containment requirements, and — the operationally hardest part — a documented rebuttable-presumption record proving each load hasn't been mixed with a listed hazardous waste or exceeds the used-oil specification's halogen/PCB thresholds, since mixing with a chlorinated solvent flips an entire load into hazardous waste with far more expensive disposal obligations. Many states layer their own used-oil handler permit and per-gallon collection-fee reporting on top of the federal framework. A small operator running a route of 20-60 collection stops tracks per-stop pickup volume and date, the halogen-test or source-documentation trail proving each load is clean, the EPA ID and state handler permit renewal, and per-delivery manifests to the re-refiner — on paper pickup tickets and a filing cabinet, and a load later found to lack documented rebuttal evidence for the presumption test is exactly the finding that turns a routine EPA or state inspection into a hazardous-waste enforcement action.

**What to build:** A per-stop pickup log (source, volume, pickup date) building a running collection-route history; a halogen/PCB rebuttable-presumption documentation tracker flagging any load lacking a current test or source-documentation record before it's logged as clean used oil; an EPA ID and state used-oil handler permit renewal tracker; and a per-delivery manifest and volume-reconciliation log to the re-refiner or burner.

**Skill fit:** 40 CFR Part 279's presumption-test thresholds and state used-oil handler permit rules are published, structured federal and state regulatory data with concrete numeric thresholds — the documentation gate blocking a load from being logged as clean until the presumption record is complete is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a petroleum-recycling category distinct from the hazardous-waste generator, FOG/grease-trap hauler, and scrap-tire-hauler ideas already in this repo, none of which handle used lubricating oil under Part 279's separate framework.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-stop pickup log, the presumption-documentation gate, and the EPA ID/permit renewal tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent used-oil collectors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Oil Recyclers Association (NORA) runs an active member directory of independent used-oil collectors and re-refiners reaching exactly this small-operator segment currently tracking presumption documentation by hand.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-499/mo per operator depending on route size and stop count. 30-35 operators × $349/mo avg = $10,470-12,215/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every pickup stop generates a fresh volume log and presumption-documentation event, and every delivery to a re-refiner requires a fresh reconciliation record — continuous, route-volume-driven compliance tied directly to the collector's ability to keep operating as a recycler rather than face hazardous-waste reclassification.

**Biggest risk:** A load that fails the presumption test and gets reclassified as hazardous waste is a severe, expensive event that could unfairly reflect on the tracking tool if a collector treats a missing test as optional — mitigate by making the presumption-documentation gate a hard block before a load is logged as clean, with a clear disclaimer that laboratory testing judgment remains the collector's responsibility.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12-14 operators ($4.2-4.9K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 22-24 operators ($7.7-8.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 30-35 operators plus a multi-route consolidator tier for collectors running several trucks.

### 4. Livestock Artificial Insemination & Embryo-Transfer Center Donor Disease-Testing & NAAB Certified Semen Services Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Livestock semen and embryo collection centers — bull studs collecting and freezing bovine semen for the artificial insemination industry, and equine or livestock embryo-transfer centers — depend on USDA APHIS-accredited veterinarians for health certification, and, for interstate and international shipment of genetic material, must meet the National Association of Animal Breeders' Certified Semen Services (CSS) health and testing protocol (or the equivalent Original Health Certificate standard many importing countries require): each donor animal must pass a documented disease-testing panel — BVD, Johne's disease, trichomoniasis, and other reportable diseases — on a periodic retest schedule before its collected semen can be certified and shipped, and any new donor entering the stud must clear a documented quarantine-and-entry-testing period first. Embryo-transfer centers separately follow International Embryo Transfer Society (IETS) health and processing protocols. A small independent bull stud or embryo-transfer center running a donor herd of a few dozen animals tracks each donor's disease-testing retest due dates gating whether its semen or embryos can be certified for sale, new-donor quarantine documentation, and per-lot or per-straw shipping health-certificate paperwork tied to destination-state or export requirements — usually on paper herd-health cards and a filing cabinet, and semen shipped from a donor whose retest had lapsed, or without the CSS-required documentation, is exactly the finding that can cost the stud its CSS certification and its ability to sell into the interstate or export AI market.

**What to build:** A per-donor disease-testing retest tracker with a "certified for collection/shipment" gate blocking a donor's semen or embryos from being marked sellable once a required retest lapses; a new-donor quarantine and entry-testing documentation log; a per-lot/per-straw shipping health-certificate generator tied to destination-state or country requirements; and a CSS/IETS certification renewal tracker.

**Skill fit:** NAAB's Certified Semen Services health protocol and USDA APHIS's accredited-veterinarian health-certificate requirements are structured, published industry-standard and federal regulatory data, and the per-donor retest gate is the same deterministic "block the sale until the record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a livestock-genetics category distinct from the livestock hauler, livestock auction market, and mobile large-animal veterinary practice ideas already in this repo, none of which track a collection center's own donor disease-testing and germplasm-certification requirements.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the donor retest tracker with the certification gate, the quarantine/entry-testing log, and the shipping health-certificate generator. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent bull studs and embryo-transfer centers.

**Time to first $:** 30-60 days — the National Association of Animal Breeders (NAAB) maintains a member directory of Certified Semen Services production studs and affiliated embryo-transfer centers, a small, well-organized professional community that already navigates the CSS retest schedule as an existential part of the business.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349-699/mo per center depending on donor-herd size, since a lost CSS certification is a business-ending event that supports pricing at the higher end of this series. 22-28 centers × $499/mo avg = $10,978-13,972/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every donor's disease-testing retest recurs on its own periodic cycle for the life of the animal in the stud, and every semen or embryo shipment generates its own health-certificate documentation event — continuous, herd-and-shipment-volume-driven compliance tied directly to the center's CSS and export certification.

**Biggest risk:** The buyer population is a small, specialized niche — a few hundred certified studs and embryo-transfer centers nationally. Mitigate by covering both bovine semen studs and embryo-transfer centers in one product, since many small operations run both, and by pricing at the higher end given the stakes.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10-12 centers ($5-6K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 18-20 centers ($9-10K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 22-28 centers plus an international export health-certificate concierge upsell.

### 5. Sign Language Interpreting Agency RID/NIC Certification, ADA Dispatch-Matching & FCC VRS/VRI Provider Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Sign language interpreting agencies that dispatch ASL interpreters to hospitals, courtrooms, schools, and business meetings — and the growing number that also provide Video Relay Service (VRS) or Video Remote Interpreting (VRI) — operate under a certification and compliance framework entirely distinct from spoken-language interpreting: each interpreter's credential comes from the Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf's National Interpreter Certification (RID NIC) or a state's own Quality Assurance (QA) screening-level system (many states require a specific QA level for legal or medical assignments), renewed through RID's CEU cycle, and an agency dispatching an interpreter below the assignment's required certification level exposes both the agency and the requesting entity — a hospital or court — to an ADA effective-communication-mandate violation. Agencies that additionally operate as or contract with a certified VRS or VRI provider must separately meet the FCC's own compliance framework for those services (47 CFR Part 64), including functional and technical requirements and mandatory call-data reporting to the FCC's Interstate TRS Fund administrator, a distinct provider-certification renewal obligation neither a spoken-language court interpreter agency nor a healthcare interpreter agency in this repo's existing ideas has any field for. A small agency dispatching a roster of a few dozen freelance and staff interpreters across dozens of assignments a week tracks each interpreter's certification level and CEU-renewal currency, an assignment-to-certification-level matching rule, and (for VRS/VRI providers) FCC call-reporting deadlines — usually on a spreadsheet the office coordinator maintains by hand, and an interpreter dispatched below an assignment's required certification level is exactly the gap an ADA complaint or an FCC compliance review is built to catch.

**What to build:** A per-interpreter RID/NIC or state QA-level certification tracker with CEU-cycle renewal alerts; an assignment-dispatch gate cross-checking the requesting entity's required certification level (legal, medical, general) against the assigned interpreter's current credential before dispatch is confirmed; an FCC VRS/VRI provider-certification and call-data reporting-deadline tracker for agencies operating relay services; and an ADA effective-communication documentation log per assignment for audit readiness.

**Skill fit:** RID's national certification structure, state QA-level frameworks, and the FCC's Part 64 VRS/VRI provider rules are published, structured credentialing and federal regulatory data, and the assignment-to-certification dispatch gate is the same deterministic "block the action until the record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a Deaf-services interpreting-agency category distinct from the spoken-language Court Interpreter Agency and Healthcare Medical Interpreter Agency ideas already in this repo, neither of which shares RID's certification body, the state QA-level system, or the FCC's VRS/VRI compliance framework.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-interpreter certification tracker, the assignment-dispatch gate, and the FCC VRS/VRI reporting tracker for agencies that offer relay services. First 5-8 agency clients through direct outreach.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf (RID) maintains a member directory that includes agency members, and state RID chapters and state Deaf-services-commission vendor lists reach exactly the small interpreting agencies navigating the dispatch-matching problem by hand.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-449/mo per agency depending on interpreter roster size and whether it offers VRS/VRI. 35-45 agencies × $299/mo avg = $10,465-13,455/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every assignment triggers a fresh certification-level dispatch check, every interpreter's CEU cycle recurs independently, and VRS/VRI providers face recurring FCC call-reporting deadlines — continuous, assignment-volume-driven compliance tied directly to the agency's ability to keep dispatching legally adequate interpreters.

**Biggest risk:** A dispatch-gate error is a real ADA liability but a comparatively lower-frequency event than the daily-clock-driven ideas elsewhere in this series. Mitigate by pricing around the dispatch-matching feature's risk-reduction value and by bundling CEU-cycle tracking, which every agency needs regardless of assignment volume.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 agencies ($4.5K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 30 agencies ($9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 40-45 agencies plus a spoken-language-agency cross-sell, since many agencies run both ASL and spoken-language interpreter rosters.

## This Session's Pick: #4 (Livestock AI & Embryo-Transfer Center) for the highest per-account value with the clearest existential stakes; #5 (Sign Language Interpreting Agency) for the widest, most accessible buyer pool

Livestock AI and embryo-transfer center compliance stands out because losing CSS certification is a total, immediate loss of the center's ability to sell into the interstate and export market — a single lapsed donor retest can end the revenue line entirely, which supports premium pricing in a series where most ideas cap out well under $700/mo per account. NAAB's tight, well-organized member directory gives direct access to a professional community that already treats disease-testing documentation as core to staying in business, not an afterthought.

Sign language interpreting agency is the most accessible pitch in this session: RID's national member directory reaches small agency owners directly, the ADA stakes are concrete and easy to explain in one sentence ("don't send an underqualified interpreter to a courtroom"), and the CEU-tracking feature alone is valuable to every agency regardless of whether they ever touch VRS or VRI — giving the product a broad base subscription with a clear upsell path into the FCC-regulated relay-service tier as an agency grows.
