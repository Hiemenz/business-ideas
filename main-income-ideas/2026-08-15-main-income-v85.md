### 1. Zipline & Aerial Adventure Course Operator ACCT/ASTM F2959 Certification & State Amusement-Device Inspection Compliance
**Problem:** Zip line and aerial adventure (canopy tour) course operators must maintain compliance with ACCT (Association for Challenge Course Technology) accreditation standards or the ASTM F2959 Standard Practice for Aerial Adventure Courses, and in the growing number of states classifying these courses under state amusement-device statutes — New Hampshire, New York, and Vermont's Department of Labor Amusement Device programs among them — operators must obtain an annual state inspection and permit before opening for the season; a permit lapse or expired inspection is exactly the finding a state labor-department field audit targets first, and a course operated under a lapsed permit can be shut down mid-season, losing an entire weekend of peak-season revenue. Every guide who clips participants into the course and supervises their transit must hold a current ACCT-recognized practitioner-level competency certification, renewed periodically with a hands-on skills-verification requirement, and a guide running the course on a lapsed certification exposes the operator to the same liability exposure as operating an entirely uninspected course — a serious concern given that most course-related litigation centers directly on documented guide competency. Every physical element of the course — cables, platforms, anchors, and braking systems — must also undergo a documented daily pre-opening inspection plus a periodic (commonly annual) comprehensive third-party inspection under ASTM F2959, entirely separate from the state permit inspection, and a course opened for the day without a completed pre-opening checklist on file is exactly the gap a post-incident investigation is built to expose. A small adventure park running several parallel courses and a rotating roster of seasonal guides manages state permit renewal, per-guide certification currency, and per-element daily/annual inspection documentation with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A daily pre-opening element-inspection checklist tool that blocks that day's operation on any course until every cable, platform, anchor, and brake check is logged complete; a per-guide ACCT/practitioner certification tracker with a "cleared to guide" gate blocking a shift assignment once a certification lapses; and a state amusement-device permit and annual third-party inspection renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** ACCT/ASTM F2959's element-inspection cadence and state amusement-device program renewal cycles are public, structured standards-body and state regulatory data with a well-defined per-course, per-element, and per-guide record requirement, and the daily-checklist enforcement plus the guide-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "require the record before the action proceeds / block the action until the credential clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an outdoor-adventure trade most small operators currently track on a paper clipboard hung at the course entrance with no systematic cross-check between a specific guide's certification status and the shift they're assigned.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the daily element-inspection checklist, the guide certification tracker with the guide-eligibility gate, and the state permit renewal tracker; land the first 5-8 operators through Association for Challenge Course Technology (ACCT) member and accredited-vendor directory outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — ACCT's member/accredited-vendor directory and state adventure-tourism association meetings, where small canopy-tour and zip line operators already juggle daily element checklists and staggered guide-certification renewal across a seasonal crew with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per guide tracked. Average client (10 guides) = $249 + $150 = $399/mo. 24-30 operators × $399/mo = **$9,576-11,970/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every operating day generates its own pre-opening element-inspection event across every course, every guide's certification renews on its own independent cycle, and the state permit renews annually ahead of each new season — a continuous, per-day and per-guide compliance stream tied directly to the park's operating calendar.
**Biggest risk:** A course opened without a completed daily element-inspection checklist, or a guide running the line on a lapsed certification, is exactly the finding a post-incident investigation or a state amusement-device field audit is built to catch — the checklist enforcement and the guide-eligibility gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → daily-checklist tool, guide-certification tracker, and permit-renewal tracker deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through ACCT outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-17 operators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 24-30 operators, pushing past $9.58-11.97K/mo.

### 2. Organizational Representative Payee SSA Fiduciary Accounting, Bonding & Conserved-Funds Compliance
**Problem:** Organizational representative payees — nonprofit and for-profit entities appointed by the Social Security Administration under 20 CFR §§404.2001-404.2065 (Title II) and §416.601 et seq. (SSI) to manage benefits on behalf of beneficiaries SSA has determined cannot manage their own funds — must file an annual Representative Payee Report (Form SSA-623 or its online equivalent) for every single beneficiary, accounting for exactly how every dollar received was spent, and a report filed late or missing a required expenditure category is exactly the finding an SSA payee-performance review targets first, with repeat failures triggering payee revocation that strips the organization of every beneficiary it serves at once rather than just the one late file. Many states additionally require an organizational payee managing multiple beneficiaries' funds to carry a fidelity bond sized to its total funds under management, renewed annually, and a bond that lapses — or falls below the required coverage as the organization's total managed funds grow — while beneficiary funds are held is exactly the exposure a state or SSA compliance review is built to catch. SSA rules further require each beneficiary's funds to be tracked in individually identifiable accounting records distinct from any other funds, with any beneficiary whose conserved (unspent) balance crosses a specific threshold required to move into a dedicated interest-bearing savings account — a threshold breach without the required account-conversion is exactly the kind of technical violation an SSA site review flags. A small payee organization managing several dozen beneficiaries' benefit accounts manages per-beneficiary annual accounting deadlines, fidelity-bond coverage currency, and per-beneficiary conserved-funds threshold monitoring with no dedicated compliance system beyond a shared spreadsheet.
**What to build:** A per-beneficiary annual accounting deadline tracker computing each SSA-623 due date and flagging incomplete expenditure-category documentation before submission; a fidelity-bond coverage tracker cross-checked against total funds currently under management with an alert on any shortfall; and a per-beneficiary conserved-funds threshold tracker flagging any balance requiring conversion to a dedicated savings account.
**Skill fit:** SSA's representative payee regulations and reporting requirements are public, structured federal regulatory data with a well-defined per-beneficiary record requirement, and the accounting-deadline tracker plus the conserved-funds threshold tracker are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the cycle / flag the account once a threshold is crossed" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a federal-fiduciary trade most small payee organizations currently track on a spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a beneficiary's running balance and the point at which it requires a dedicated account.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-beneficiary accounting-deadline tracker, the fidelity-bond coverage tracker, and the conserved-funds threshold tracker; land the first 5-8 organizations through American Association of Daily Money Managers (AADMM) member outreach, whose membership significantly overlaps with organizations serving as SSA representative payees.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Association of Daily Money Managers (AADMM) member directory and state Aging & Disability Resource Center networks, where small payee organizations already juggle per-beneficiary SSA-623 deadlines and bond-coverage currency with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $12/mo per beneficiary tracked. Average client (40 beneficiaries) = $199 + $480 = $679/mo. 14-17 organizations × $679/mo = **$9,506-11,543/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every beneficiary's annual accounting deadline recurs on its own independent cycle, every dollar received moves the organization's total funds-under-management figure against its bond coverage, and every beneficiary's conserved balance is a continuously monitored threshold — a continuous, per-beneficiary compliance stream tied directly to the organization's caseload.
**Biggest risk:** A late or incomplete SSA-623 accounting, or a fidelity bond that lapses below required coverage, is exactly the finding an SSA payee-performance review is built to catch — and either can trigger revocation across the organization's entire beneficiary roster at once — the accounting-deadline tracker and the bond-coverage tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → accounting-deadline tracker, bond-coverage tracker, and conserved-funds tracker deployed, first 5-8 organization retainers through AADMM outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 8-10 organizations. Replaces-full-time-salary → 14-17 organizations, pushing past $9.51-11.54K/mo.

### 3. ATV/OHV & Personal Watercraft Rental Operator State Safety-Verification & Rental-Unit Inspection Compliance
**Problem:** Companies renting all-terrain vehicles, side-by-sides, and personal watercraft to the public operate under a patchwork of state rental-specific safety statutes distinct from general dealer licensure — Florida's vessel/PWC rental law (Fla. Stat. §327.395) requires a rental operator to provide a documented pre-rental safety orientation and confirm the renter meets the statutory age and operating requirement before every single rental, and multiple states' OHV statutes require a rental operator to verify a renter under a specific age threshold holds a state or ATV Safety Institute (ASI) safety-course completion card before releasing a unit — a rental issued without documented age/safety-card verification on file is exactly the finding a state parks-and-recreation or marine-patrol field check targets, and is the first document plaintiffs' counsel requests after any rental-related injury. Every rental unit also carries a state-mandated pre- and post-rental inspection requirement, and for watercraft specifically, a rental-boat data-plate and safety-equipment completeness check (PFDs, fire extinguisher, visual distress signals) confirming the unit is fully equipped before it goes back out to the next renter — a unit released for its next rental without a completed post-return inspection is exactly the gap a state field audit or an injured renter's liability claim is built to expose. A small rental operator running dozens of ATVs, side-by-sides, and PWCs across a busy season manages per-rental renter-eligibility verification, per-unit pre/post-rental inspection documentation, and state rental-operator permit currency with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-rental renter-eligibility checker verifying age and safety-course documentation is on file before a rental transaction is logged complete; a per-unit pre- and post-rental inspection and safety-equipment checklist blocking a unit's next rental until the return inspection clears; and a state rental-operator permit renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** State rental-specific safety statutes and pre/post-rental inspection requirements are public, structured state regulatory data with a well-defined per-rental and per-unit record requirement, and the renter-eligibility checker plus the unit-release gate are the same deterministic "confirm the required record is on file before the transaction proceeds / block the action until the inspection clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an outdoor-recreation-rental trade most small operators currently track on a paper waiver clipboard with no systematic cross-check between a specific renter's eligibility documentation and the unit's own return-inspection status.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the renter-eligibility checker, the pre/post-rental unit-inspection checklist, and the permit renewal tracker; land the first 5-8 operators through Specialty Vehicle Institute of America (SVIA)/ATV Safety Institute dealer-rental network outreach and Personal Watercraft Industry Association (PWIA) member contacts.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Specialty Vehicle Institute of America's ASI dealer-rental network and Personal Watercraft Industry Association (PWIA) member directory, where small rental operators already juggle per-rental age/safety-card verification and pre/post-rental unit inspections during peak season with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $6/mo per rental unit tracked. Average client (35 units) = $199 + $210 = $409/mo. 23-29 operators × $409/mo = **$9,407-11,861/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every rental transaction opens its own renter-eligibility verification event, and every unit's return triggers its own post-rental inspection before it can go back out — a continuous, per-transaction compliance stream tied directly to the operator's rental volume through the season.
**Biggest risk:** A rental issued without documented renter-eligibility verification, or a unit released for its next rental without a completed post-return inspection, is exactly the finding a state field check or a rental-injury liability claim is built to catch — the renter-eligibility checker and the unit-release gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → renter-eligibility checker, unit-inspection checklist, and permit tracker deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through SVIA/PWIA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 13-16 operators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 23-29 operators, pushing past $9.41-11.86K/mo.

### 4. Coin-Operated Amusement & Vending Machine Route Operator State Decal, Health-Permit & Tobacco-Lock Compliance
**Problem:** Vending route operators running food and beverage vending machines alongside coin-operated amusement/game machines across dozens of host locations must hold a current per-machine state amusement-device tax decal or registration in states like Louisiana, Illinois, and South Carolina — every machine placed in service without a current-year decal affixed is exactly the finding a state revenue-department field sweep targets first, and can result in on-the-spot machine seizure. Food and beverage vending machines are separately regulated as retail food establishments under state health codes, requiring a per-location or per-route health permit commonly renewed annually and subject to periodic sanitation inspection benchmarked against National Automatic Merchandising Association (NAMA) guidelines — a machine operating on a lapsed health permit is exactly the gap a routine health-department inspection is built to catch, and a failed sanitation check can pull the machine from that location entirely. Any vending machine dispensing tobacco products must separately comply with state tobacco-vending laws requiring either a remote-locking, age-verification-enabled mechanism or placement restricted to an adult-only facility, and a machine found without the required locking device is a direct, independently citable state tobacco-control violation regardless of the machine's other compliance status. A small route operator running machines across 40-80 host locations manages per-machine decal renewal, per-location health-permit currency, and tobacco-lock compliance documentation with no dedicated compliance function beyond a paper route sheet.
**What to build:** A per-machine state decal/registration renewal tracker flagging any machine due for its next annual decal; a per-location health-permit renewal and NAMA sanitation-inspection log; and a tobacco-vending lock compliance checklist confirming the required locking mechanism is documented and current for every applicable machine.
**Skill fit:** State amusement-device decal statutes, health-department vending permit cycles, and tobacco-vending lock requirements are public, structured state regulatory data with a well-defined per-machine and per-location record requirement, and the decal-renewal tracker plus the health-permit tracker are the same deterministic "compute the next-due renewal from the cycle / flag the gap before an inspection finds it" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a route-based retail trade most small vending operators currently track on a paper route sheet with no systematic cross-check between a specific machine's decal status and the host location's own health-permit currency.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the decal renewal tracker, the health-permit/sanitation log, and the tobacco-lock compliance checklist; land the first 5-8 operators through National Automatic Merchandising Association (NAMA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Automatic Merchandising Association (NAMA) member directory and state vending association meetings, where small route operators already juggle per-machine decal renewal and per-location health-permit currency across dozens of host sites with no dedicated compliance staff.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every machine's state decal renews on its own annual cycle, every host location's health permit renews independently, and any tobacco-vending machine's lock compliance requires ongoing verification — a continuous, per-machine and per-location compliance stream tied directly to the operator's route size.
**Biggest risk:** A machine operating without a current-year state decal, or a food-vending machine on a lapsed health permit, is exactly the finding a state revenue-department sweep or a health-department inspection is built to catch — the decal-renewal tracker and the health-permit tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → decal tracker, health-permit log, and tobacco-lock checklist deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through NAMA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 operators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 26-33 operators, pushing past $9.33-11.85K/mo.

### 5. Official Grain Inspection & Weighing Agency USDA FGIS Designation & United States Grain Standards Act Compliance
**Problem:** Independent grain inspection and weighing agencies — hired by elevators, merchandisers, and exporters to issue official grade certificates and weight certifications on grain shipments — operate under a USDA Federal Grain Inspection Service (FGIS) designation granted under the United States Grain Standards Act (7 U.S.C. §71 et seq.), which requires the agency to staff individually FGIS-licensed official inspectors, each subject to periodic proficiency re-testing on a fixed cycle, before any grade certificate the agency issues carries official standing — an inspector working past a lapsed license is exactly the finding an FGIS compliance review targets first, and can result in the agency's entire designation being suspended, cutting off its right to issue official certificates at every elevator and export facility it serves simultaneously, not just the one shipment in question. Every scale used to produce an official weight certificate must separately hold NTEP type-evaluation certification and pass periodic re-verification on its own fixed interval, and a weight certificate issued from equipment past its re-verification date is exactly the gap an FGIS or state weights-and-measures audit is built to expose. Every official inspection and weighing must also be documented against FGIS's specific recordkeeping and retention requirements, and an agency unable to produce a complete, gap-free record for a specific shipment during an FGIS audit faces the same exposure as an inspector working entirely without a license. A small independent inspection agency running several licensed inspectors across multiple elevator and export-facility contracts manages per-inspector license/proficiency currency, per-scale re-verification tracking, and per-shipment recordkeeping with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-inspector FGIS license and proficiency re-test tracker with a "cleared to inspect" gate blocking an assignment once a license lapses; a per-scale NTEP re-verification tracker with a "cleared to certify weight" gate; and a per-shipment recordkeeping completeness tracker built for FGIS audit readiness.
**Skill fit:** FGIS's official inspector licensing cycle and NTEP scale re-verification requirements are public, structured federal regulatory and standards-body data with a well-defined per-inspector, per-scale, and per-shipment record requirement, and the inspect-eligibility gate plus the weight-certification gate are the same deterministic "block the action until the credential clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a legal-metrology-adjacent grain trade most small independent inspection agencies currently track on a paper logbook with no systematic cross-check between a specific inspector's license status and the shipment they're assigned to certify.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the inspector license/proficiency tracker with the inspect-eligibility gate, the scale re-verification tracker, and the shipment recordkeeping tracker; land the first 5-8 agencies through Grain Elevator and Processing Society (GEAPS) and National Grain and Feed Association (NGFA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Grain Elevator and Processing Society (GEAPS) member directory and National Grain and Feed Association (NGFA) independent-agency network, where small inspection agencies already juggle per-inspector FGIS proficiency renewal and per-scale re-verification across multiple facility contracts with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $20/mo per inspector tracked. Average client (6 inspectors) = $249 + $120 = $369/mo. 25-31 agencies × $369/mo = **$9,225-11,439/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every inspector's FGIS license and proficiency status renews on its own independent cycle, every scale's NTEP re-verification recurs on its own interval, and every shipment inspected generates its own recordkeeping obligation — a continuous, per-inspector and per-shipment compliance stream tied directly to the agency's facility-contract volume.
**Biggest risk:** An inspector working past a lapsed FGIS license, or a weight certificate issued from a scale past its re-verification date, is exactly the finding an FGIS compliance review is built to catch — and either can suspend the agency's designation across every facility it serves at once — the inspect-eligibility gate and the weight-certification gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → inspector tracker, scale re-verification tracker, and recordkeeping tracker deployed, first 5-8 agency retainers through GEAPS/NGFA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 15-18 agencies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 25-31 agencies, pushing past $9.23-11.44K/mo.
