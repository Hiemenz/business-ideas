### 1. Commercial Kitchen Exhaust Hood & Fire Suppression System Service Company NFPA 96/17A & IKECA Compliance
**Problem:** Commercial kitchen exhaust hood cleaning companies and fire suppression system service companies operate under NFPA 96 (Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations), which mandates a recurring grease-buildup cleaning interval for exhaust hoods, ductwork, and fans that varies by cooking volume — monthly for high-volume 24-hour operations like solid-fuel or wok cooking, quarterly for moderate-volume kitchens, semi-annually for low-volume operations — and NFPA 17A, which requires every wet chemical fire suppression system (the Ansul-style system mounted over the cooking line) to be inspected and certified semi-annually by a licensed technician, with the certification tag dated and posted at the system. A restaurant operating past its NFPA 96 cleaning interval, or with an expired NFPA 17A suppression-system certification tag, is one of the most common findings a local fire marshal's routine restaurant inspection targets, and in most jurisdictions an expired suppression-system tag is grounds for an immediate correction order that can suspend the kitchen's certificate of occupancy for cooking operations until a certified technician re-inspects — a consequence severe enough that insurers routinely require a current NFPA 17A tag as a condition of a restaurant's property policy. Technicians performing suppression system service typically hold manufacturer-specific certification (Ansul, Kidde, Pyro-Chem) alongside company-level licensure many states or municipalities require for fire protection contractors, and IKECA (International Kitchen Exhaust Cleaning Association) certification has become the de facto credential insurers and property managers check for before accepting a hood-cleaning company's after-service certificate. A small service company running 4-10 technicians across dozens to hundreds of restaurant accounts manages per-property, per-cooking-volume NFPA 96 interval computation, per-system NFPA 17A semi-annual certification scheduling, and per-technician manufacturer/IKECA certification currency with no dedicated compliance function — and a hood cleaned past its due interval, or a suppression system serviced without a current certification tag posted, is exactly the gap a fire marshal inspection or an insurer's post-fire claim investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-property hood/duct cleaning interval engine computing each account's next-due date from its cooking-volume classification, with tiered alerts before the interval lapses; a per-system NFPA 17A semi-annual certification tracker generating the after-service certificate and flagging any system approaching its six-month recertification window; and a per-technician manufacturer certification (Ansul/Kidde/Pyro-Chem) and IKECA credential tracker with a "cleared to service" gate matching each tech's certification to the system type being inspected.
**Skill fit:** NFPA 96's cooking-volume-based interval table and NFPA 17A's semi-annual certification cycle are public, structured code requirements with a well-defined per-property record structure, and the interval-computation engine plus the technician-certification gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the trigger event / block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a restaurant-facility trade most small service companies currently track on a paper route sheet with no systematic per-property due-date engine.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the hood/duct interval engine, the NFPA 17A certification tracker, and the technician certification gate; land the first 5-8 companies through IKECA member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — IKECA (International Kitchen Exhaust Cleaning Association) member directory and regional fire protection contractor association meetings, where small service company owners already navigate cooking-volume-based cleaning intervals and semi-annual suppression certification across a growing restaurant account list.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per technician tracked. Average client (4 technicians) = $249 + $60 = $309/mo. 30-38 companies × $309/mo = **$9,270-11,742/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every serviced restaurant carries its own recurring cooking-volume-driven cleaning interval, every suppression system recertifies on its own independent six-month cycle, and technician manufacturer/IKECA credentials renew on staggered schedules — a continuous, per-property compliance stream tied directly to the service company's account count.
**Biggest risk:** A hood cleaned past its NFPA 96 due interval, or a suppression system carrying an expired NFPA 17A certification tag, is exactly the finding a fire marshal inspection or a post-fire insurance claim investigation is built to catch — the interval-computation engine and the certification tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → hood/duct interval engine, NFPA 17A tracker, and technician certification gate deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through IKECA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 30-38 companies, pushing past $9.27-11.74K/mo.

### 2. Hemp Product Processor & Retailer Farm Bill, USDA Hemp Program & Per-Batch COA Compliance
**Problem:** Hemp product processors and retailers — operations extracting, formulating, or selling CBD and other non-intoxicating hemp-derived products — operate under the 2018 Farm Bill's federal legalization of hemp (7 U.S.C. §1639o) and USDA's Domestic Hemp Production Program (7 CFR Part 990), which requires growers to register with the state or USDA directly, submit to pre-harvest THC compliance sampling within a defined window before harvest, and — critically — dispose of any "hot crop" that tests above the 0.3% delta-9 THC dry-weight federal threshold through a DEA-registered reverse distributor, since possessing hemp biomass that tested hot is treated as an unregistered controlled substance regardless of the grower's intent. Every finished product a processor or retailer sells must be backed by a current Certificate of Analysis (COA) from an accredited lab confirming potency and screening for pesticides, heavy metals, and residual solvents — a growing number of states now require the COA to be posted or scannable (QR code) at point of sale, and a product sold without a current, matching COA on file is one of the most common findings a state department of agriculture compliance sweep or a consumer-protection investigation targets, since an outdated or mismatched COA is functionally identical to selling an untested product. State hemp registration itself must be renewed annually or seasonally depending on the state, and a processor selling into multiple states manages a registration and labeling-requirement grid that varies state by state, since several states impose their own THC-per-serving caps or ingredient restrictions on finished hemp products beyond the federal 0.3% growing threshold. A small processor or retailer running several product lines across a growing multi-state footprint manages per-batch COA currency, pre-harvest THC-compliance-testing deadlines, and multi-state registration/labeling requirements with no dedicated compliance function — and a product sold without a current COA on file, or a batch shipped into a state where its labeling doesn't meet that state's specific THC-per-serving cap, is exactly the gap a state agriculture department sweep is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-batch COA tracker with a "cleared to sell" gate blocking a product from being marked available until its current, matching Certificate of Analysis is confirmed on file; a pre-harvest THC-compliance-sampling deadline calculator computing each grow's required testing window before harvest; and a per-state registration and labeling-requirement tracker flagging any product whose formulation or label doesn't meet the destination state's specific THC-per-serving cap.
**Skill fit:** USDA's hemp production program and state COA-posting requirements are public, structured regulatory sources with a well-defined per-batch record requirement, and the sell-eligibility gate plus the multi-state labeling-requirement tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / reconcile the label against the destination state's rule" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a hemp-commerce category most small processors and retailers currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic per-batch COA cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the COA tracker with the sell-eligibility gate, the pre-harvest testing deadline calculator, and the multi-state labeling tracker; land the first 5-8 processors/retailers through National Hemp Association (NHA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Hemp Association (NHA) and U.S. Hemp Roundtable member directories, where small processors and retailers already navigate per-batch COA currency and a growing multi-state labeling-requirement grid with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $15/mo per active SKU tracked. Average client (10 SKUs) = $199 + $150 = $349/mo. 27-33 processors/retailers × $349/mo = **$9,423-11,517/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every batch produced opens its own COA-currency event, every harvest opens its own pre-sampling deadline, and every new state a business expands into adds its own registration and labeling-requirement footprint — a continuous, per-batch compliance stream tied directly to the business's SKU count and geographic reach.
**Biggest risk:** A product sold without a current, matching COA on file, or a batch shipped into a state where its labeling exceeds that state's THC-per-serving cap, is exactly the gap a state agriculture department compliance sweep is built to catch — the sell-eligibility gate and the labeling-requirement tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → COA tracker, pre-harvest deadline calculator, and labeling tracker deployed, first 5-8 processor/retailer retainers through NHA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-18 businesses. Replaces-full-time-salary → 27-33 businesses, pushing past $9.42-11.52K/mo.

### 3. Armored Car & Cash-in-Transit Company State Carrier Licensure & Chain-of-Custody Manifest Compliance
**Problem:** Armored car and cash-in-transit (CIT) companies hold a state armored car carrier license — issued in most states by a state police agency or department of public safety, distinct from any general trucking or courier authority — renewed on its own cycle and backed by fidelity bonding sized to the company's cash-in-transit volume, and every armed courier crewing a route must separately hold a firearms permit layered on top of the base carrier license, with an annual or biennial requalification requirement in most states. Every pickup — a bank branch's night deposit, a retailer's daily cash drop, an ATM replenishment run — must generate a chain-of-custody manifest documenting the amount collected, the courier and vehicle, and the time of pickup and vault deposit, reconciled against the receiving bank's or client's own deposited total; a manifest that doesn't reconcile, or a pickup performed by a courier whose firearms permit has lapsed, is one of the most common findings a state carrier-license compliance audit targets, and an unreconciled cash discrepancy is exactly the fact pattern that has led states to suspend a carrier's license pending investigation regardless of whether the shortfall was ultimately explained. Armored vehicles themselves typically require state-specific equipment certification (armor rating, safe/vault specifications) verified at licensing and periodically thereafter, and a vehicle dispatched without current equipment certification on file compounds a carrier's exposure during any post-incident review. A small CIT company running 4-8 crews and armored vehicles across dozens of daily pickup routes manages per-courier firearms-permit currency, per-vehicle equipment certification, and per-pickup manifest-to-deposit reconciliation with no dedicated compliance function — and a manifest that fails to reconcile against the deposited total, or a courier dispatched with a lapsed firearms permit, is exactly the gap a state carrier-license audit or a client's own loss investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-courier firearms permit and requalification tracker with a "cleared to crew" gate blocking a dispatch assignment once a permit lapses; a per-vehicle equipment certification tracker; and a per-pickup manifest-to-deposit reconciliation log flagging any route where the collected total doesn't match the receiving institution's confirmed deposit.
**Skill fit:** State armored car carrier licensing and firearms-permit requalification cycles are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-courier and per-route record requirement, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the manifest-reconciliation log are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / reconcile the ledger against reality" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a cash-logistics trade most small CIT companies currently track on a paper route log with no systematic reconciliation cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the firearms permit tracker with the dispatch-eligibility gate, the vehicle equipment certification tracker, and the manifest-reconciliation log; land the first 5-8 companies through ASIS International chapter outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — ASIS International local chapter meetings, where small armored car and CIT company owners already navigate per-courier firearms-permit currency and daily manifest reconciliation across a growing route count with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $25/mo per armored vehicle tracked. Average client (5 vehicles) = $299 + $125 = $424/mo. 22-28 companies × $424/mo = **$9,328-11,872/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every pickup opens its own manifest-reconciliation event, every courier's firearms permit recurs on its own independent requalification cycle, and every new vehicle added expands the tracked equipment-certification footprint — a high-frequency, per-route compliance stream tied directly to the company's daily pickup volume.
**Biggest risk:** A manifest that fails to reconcile against the receiving institution's deposited total, or a courier dispatched with a lapsed firearms permit, is exactly the finding a state carrier-license audit or a client's loss investigation is built to catch — the dispatch-eligibility gate and the manifest-reconciliation log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → firearms permit tracker, vehicle certification tracker, and manifest-reconciliation log deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through ASIS outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-16 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 22-28 companies, pushing past $9.33-11.87K/mo.

### 4. FAA-Certificated Flight School Part 141/61, CFI Currency & TSA Alien Flight Student Program Compliance
**Problem:** Flight schools operating under an FAA Part 141 pilot school certificate — or providing instruction under Part 61 without holding school certification — employ Certified Flight Instructors (CFI, CFII for instrument, MEI for multi-engine) who must maintain currency through either a biennial flight review or, more commonly for active instructors, the FAA's mandatory Flight Instructor Refresher Clinic (FIRC) completed every 24 calendar months to renew the CFI certificate itself — an instructor teaching past their CFI renewal date is conducting instruction under an invalid certificate, invalidating the training time logged by every student they flew with during the lapse, a discovery that can force a student's logbook hours to be re-verified or disqualified entirely. Every training aircraft must be tracked against its own annual inspection and 100-hour inspection requirement (for aircraft used in flight instruction for hire) and any applicable airworthiness directives (ADs), with a school dispatching an aircraft past its inspection due date or with an open AD flying in direct violation of FAA airworthiness regulations — a finding an FAA Flight Standards District Office (FSDO) surveillance inspection or a post-incident investigation is built to catch first. Schools training any non-U.S.-citizen student must obtain TSA Alien Flight Student Program (AFSP) approval — a security threat assessment that must clear before the student's first flight lesson — and training a foreign national before AFSP clearance is a federal violation carrying civil penalties against the school independent of the student's actual background. A small flight school running 4-10 CFIs and several training aircraft manages staggered CFI/FIRC renewal, per-aircraft inspection and AD currency, and per-student AFSP clearance with no dedicated compliance function — and an instructor teaching on a lapsed CFI, an aircraft dispatched past its 100-hour inspection, or a foreign student flown before AFSP clearance, is exactly the gap an FAA FSDO inspection or a TSA compliance review is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-CFI certificate and FIRC renewal tracker with a "cleared to instruct" gate; a per-aircraft inspection and airworthiness-directive tracker computing each unit's next-due annual/100-hour inspection with a hard block preventing dispatch once it lapses; and a per-student TSA AFSP clearance tracker blocking a foreign national's first lesson from being scheduled until clearance is confirmed on file.
**Skill fit:** FAA CFI renewal cycles, aircraft inspection intervals, and TSA's AFSP clearance requirement are public, structured federal regulatory sources with a well-defined per-instructor, per-aircraft, and per-student record requirement, and the instruct-eligibility gate plus the dispatch-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a flight-training trade most small schools currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a CFI's certificate status and the lesson they're scheduled to teach.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the CFI/FIRC tracker with the instruct-eligibility gate, the aircraft inspection/AD tracker, and the AFSP clearance tracker; land the first 5-8 schools through National Association of Flight Instructors (NAFI) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Association of Flight Instructors (NAFI) member directory and Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association (AOPA) flight school outreach, where small school owners already navigate staggered CFI/FIRC renewal and per-aircraft inspection scheduling by hand.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $18/mo per training aircraft tracked. Average client (8 aircraft) = $199 + $144 = $343/mo. 27-35 schools × $343/mo = **$9,261-12,005/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every CFI's certificate renews on its own 24-month FIRC cycle, every aircraft opens its own recurring annual/100-hour inspection clock, and every new foreign-national enrollment opens its own AFSP clearance event — a continuous, per-instructor and per-aircraft compliance stream tied directly to the school's enrollment and fleet size.
**Biggest risk:** An instructor teaching on a lapsed CFI certificate, an aircraft dispatched past its 100-hour inspection due date, or a foreign student flown before TSA AFSP clearance, is exactly the finding an FAA FSDO surveillance inspection or TSA compliance review is built to catch — the instruct-eligibility gate and the dispatch-eligibility gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → CFI/FIRC tracker, aircraft inspection tracker, and AFSP clearance tracker deployed, first 5-8 school retainers through NAFI outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 schools. Replaces-full-time-salary → 27-35 schools, pushing past $9.26-12.01K/mo.

### 5. Independent Clinical Trial Research Site FDA GCP, IRB Continuing Review & Form 1572 Compliance
**Problem:** Independent clinical trial research sites — physician practices and dedicated site management organizations that conduct sponsor-directed investigational drug and device studies — operate under FDA Good Clinical Practice regulations (21 CFR Part 312 for investigational drugs, Part 812 for investigational devices), requiring every active protocol to carry current Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval that must undergo mandatory continuing review at least annually — more frequently for higher-risk studies as the IRB determines — with a protocol that lapses past its continuing-review date required to halt all study-related activity, including further subject enrollment and even continued dosing in most cases, until re-approval clears. Every principal investigator signs an FDA Form 1572 Statement of Investigator for each protocol, committing personally to specific obligations — supervising the investigation, ensuring informed consent is properly obtained, and maintaining adequate records — and a site that cannot produce a signed, current 1572 matching every sub-investigator actually delegated study tasks is one of the most common findings an FDA bioresearch monitoring (BIMO) inspection or a sponsor's for-cause audit targets. Every subject enrolled must have a properly executed informed consent form matching the IRB-approved version current at the time of signature — a subject consented under an outdated consent-form version after an IRB-approved amendment is a serious protocol deviation that can force the sponsor to exclude that subject's data from the study entirely — and sponsor or CRO monitoring visits routinely open by requesting the site's IRB-approval-currency documentation and source-document-to-case-report-form reconciliation before reviewing anything else. A small site running several concurrent protocols across multiple sponsors manages per-protocol IRB continuing-review deadlines, per-investigator 1572 currency, and per-subject consent-version reconciliation with no dedicated compliance function — and a protocol that continued enrolling past its IRB continuing-review deadline, or a subject consented under a stale consent-form version, is exactly the gap an FDA BIMO inspection or sponsor audit is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-protocol IRB continuing-review deadline tracker with a hard block flagging any study approaching or past its re-approval date before further enrollment or dosing can proceed; a per-investigator Form 1572 currency tracker matching every study team member's delegated tasks against a signed, current 1572; and a per-subject informed-consent-version reconciliation log confirming every enrolled subject's signed consent matches the IRB-approved version current at signature.
**Skill fit:** FDA's GCP continuing-review and 1572 requirements are public, structured federal regulatory sources with a well-defined per-protocol and per-subject record requirement, and the enrollment-eligibility gate plus the consent-version reconciliation are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / reconcile the record against the approved version" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to the sponsor-directed research side of clinical practice distinct from every treatment-billing or diagnostic-lab compliance idea already in this repo.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the IRB continuing-review tracker with the enrollment-eligibility gate, the Form 1572 currency tracker, and the consent-version reconciliation log; land the first 5-8 sites through Society for Clinical Research Sites (SCRS) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Society for Clinical Research Sites (SCRS) and Association of Clinical Research Professionals (ACRP) member directories, where small independent site owners already navigate staggered per-protocol IRB continuing-review deadlines across multiple concurrent sponsor studies with no dedicated regulatory-affairs staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base + $30/mo per active protocol tracked. Average client (5 active protocols) = $349 + $150 = $499/mo. 19-24 sites × $499/mo = **$9,481-11,976/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every active protocol carries its own independent IRB continuing-review clock, every new sponsor study adds its own 1572 and consent-version tracking chain, and subject enrollment generates a continuous stream of consent-reconciliation events — a compliance stream that grows directly with the site's protocol count and enrollment volume.
**Biggest risk:** A protocol that continued enrolling subjects past its IRB continuing-review deadline, or a subject consented under a stale consent-form version after an approved amendment, is exactly the finding an FDA BIMO inspection or sponsor for-cause audit is built to catch — the enrollment-eligibility gate and the consent-version reconciliation log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → IRB continuing-review tracker, Form 1572 tracker, and consent-reconciliation log deployed, first 5-8 site retainers through SCRS/ACRP outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 sites. Replaces-full-time-salary → 19-24 sites, pushing past $9.48-11.98K/mo.
