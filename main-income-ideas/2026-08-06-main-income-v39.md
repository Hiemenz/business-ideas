### 1. Community Solar Project Subscriber Management & State Utility Commission Compliance
**Problem:** Community solar (shared solar / solar garden) projects — which sell subscriptions to solar energy output to residential and commercial utility customers without requiring rooftop panels — are regulated by each state's Public Utilities Commission under state-specific community solar program rules. Community solar project developers and operators must comply with: state subscriber eligibility requirements (in most states, subscribers must be customers of the same electric distribution utility as the project, and may be subject to income thresholds or other eligibility criteria depending on the state program), subscriber agreement terms that conform to state PUC model subscriber agreements, monthly bill credit calculation and submission to the distribution utility for bill crediting on subscriber accounts (the utility credits subscribers' bills based on their allocated share of the project's monthly energy output, and the calculation method is regulated), state program capacity tracking (many state community solar programs cap total installed capacity — the project must track its position relative to state program caps and any waitlist requirements), and subscriber assignment and transfer procedures when a subscriber moves to a different utility service area or cancels their subscription (the project must follow state-mandated procedures for reassigning the canceled subscription to the next subscriber on the waitlist). Small community solar project operators (managing 1-3 projects with 100-500 subscribers each) process monthly bill credit calculations across large subscriber lists while managing subscriber onboarding, transfers, and cancellations manually.
**What to build:** A community solar project subscriber management and state PUC compliance service — a subscriber database and eligibility verification system (maintaining each subscriber's name, utility account number, service address, utility service area confirmation, eligibility status, subscription start date, allocated project capacity share in kW, and monthly email contact — with an eligibility re-verification workflow when a subscriber's service address or utility account changes), a monthly bill credit calculation engine (for each subscriber: calculating the monthly solar production credit based on the subscriber's allocated kW share, the project's metered monthly output in kWh, and the applicable state utility credit rate — and generating the monthly credit report in the format required by the distribution utility for bill crediting submission), a subscriber agreement compliance library (maintaining current subscriber agreement templates that conform to the applicable state PUC requirements, version-tracking agreement amendments when state PUC program rules change, and confirming that all active subscribers have executed a current agreement version), a subscription waitlist and transfer manager (maintaining the project's subscription waitlist, processing subscriber cancellations and reassigning the released capacity to the next eligible waitlist subscriber following state-mandated procedures, and documenting each transfer with the required state-mandated notification timeframes), and a state program capacity compliance dashboard (tracking the project's current subscribed capacity against state program limits, monitoring state program cap utilization across all active state programs, and alerting when program cap proximity triggers waitlist management requirements).
**Skill fit:** State community solar program rules are publicly available from each state's PUC — the subscriber eligibility requirements, bill credit calculation methodologies, and subscriber agreement content requirements are documented in state PUC orders and program guidelines. The subscriber database (a roster management system with eligibility tracking), monthly bill credit calculator (a per-subscriber energy allocation arithmetic tool), agreement compliance library, waitlist manager, and capacity dashboard are data management problems the builder's engineering and data analytics skills address directly. Community Solar Access Project and SEPA (Solar Electric Power Association) networks provide access to the buyer community.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a subscriber database with eligibility verification, a monthly bill credit calculator for the 5 largest community solar markets (NY, MA, MN, IL, CO — all with active state community solar programs and clear bill credit calculation rules), a subscription waitlist and transfer manager, and a state program capacity dashboard; first 5-8 community solar project operator clients through Interstate Renewable Energy Council (IREC) and SEPA member outreach while the bill credit calculator is validated against real project monthly output data and state utility credit rate schedules.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through SEPA (Solar Electric Power Association) member network and national community solar developer networks (the concentrated professional communities for community solar project operators — SEPA's annual Community Solar Summit is the primary industry event), state community solar program administrator referral networks (state PUC program administrators who manage community solar programs see subscriber management gaps in every project they oversee and refer systematic management tools to project operators), and clean energy investment fund referral networks (funds that finance community solar projects require their portfolio projects to have systematic subscriber management and billing processes as a portfolio monitoring requirement, creating consistent referral pathways).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per community solar project: $400-900/mo (depending on subscriber count and state complexity — a 150-subscriber single-state project with a straightforward bill credit calculation: $400/mo; a 400-subscriber project in a state with complex subscriber eligibility tiers, income-qualified subscriber set-asides, and frequent state PUC program rule updates: $900/mo). Managing 20-30 project retainers at $600/mo average = **$12,000-18,000/mo**. State program compliance audit documentation packages (one-time $500-1,200 per project when a state PUC program administrator requests subscriber eligibility documentation) generate concentrated supplemental revenue. Reaching 20-30 project retainers requires 3-4 SEPA chapter relationships and 1-2 clean energy fund referral partnerships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Monthly bill credit calculations are required every month for every subscriber in every project — a 300-subscriber project generates 300 monthly credit calculation events continuously. Subscriber onboarding and cancellation events occur throughout the year as subscribers move and new subscribers join from the waitlist. State community solar programs are expanding rapidly — new state programs are being established and existing programs are growing in subscriber counts, expanding the buyer pool continuously.
**Biggest risk:** A monthly bill credit calculation that is incorrect — either due to a formula error in the credit calculation or a subscriber allocation error — results in subscriber under- or over-crediting that may require correction with the distribution utility and can trigger PUC complaints. Build the bill credit calculation engine with a cross-validation check: total credits distributed must equal the project's total metered output for the month times the applicable credit rate, and any discrepancy between the sum of individual subscriber credits and the total expected credit pool triggers an alert before the credit report is submitted to the utility.

### 2. FAA Part 145 Aircraft Repair Station Certificate & Quality System Compliance
**Problem:** FAA-certificated aircraft repair stations — companies authorized to perform maintenance, preventive maintenance, and alterations on certificated aircraft — must maintain their FAA Part 145 Repair Station certificate through continuous compliance with FAA regulations (14 CFR Part 145). Part 145 compliance includes: maintaining a current Repair Station Manual (RSM) that describes the repair station's procedures, organization, and authorized ratings — the RSM must be submitted to the FAA for approval when established and must be updated whenever procedures change (unauthorized deviations from the approved RSM are a common FAA enforcement finding); maintaining a current Quality Control Manual (QCM) that describes the quality control system, inspection procedures, and recordkeeping requirements; maintaining a current list of rated personnel (any employee who performs inspection or signs off maintenance records must meet FAA qualification standards for the applicable rating, and the repair station must maintain qualification records); and passing FAA district office surveillance visits (the local FSDO conducts unannounced and scheduled surveillance visits reviewing RSM/QCM currency, rated personnel records, work order documentation, and tooling and equipment calibration records). Small Part 145 repair stations (10-50 technicians) have the same RSM/QCM documentation and personnel qualification obligations as large MRO facilities but without a dedicated quality and compliance department.
**What to build:** An FAA Part 145 repair station compliance management service — an RSM and QCM document management system (maintaining the repair station's current approved RSM and QCM with version control, documenting every change submitted to the FAA for approval and the FAA's approval response, and generating a revision notice for every RSM/QCM amendment — with an alert when any procedure documented in the manuals has changed in practice but the manual has not been updated), a rated personnel qualification tracker (for each rated employee: their FAA airman certificate number and ratings, repair station rating authorizations, training completions, currency requirements, and qualification documentation — with alerts when any employee's currency lapses or a training requirement is approaching its renewal date), a work order and return-to-service documentation compliance monitor (confirming that each maintenance work order has been completed with the required documentation: description of work performed, parts used with manufacturer part numbers, airworthiness directive compliance when applicable, and the authorized inspector's name, certificate number, and signature — the elements that FAA surveillance reviewers verify during FSDO visits), a calibrated tooling and equipment tracking system (maintaining the calibration status of all precision tooling and test equipment used in maintenance operations — calibration expiration date, calibration service provider, and calibration certificate — with alerts when any calibrated tool is approaching its calibration expiration), and an FAA surveillance readiness dashboard (generating the documentation package that FSDO surveillance reviewers typically request: current RSM and QCM, rated personnel qualifications, work order sample, tooling calibration records, and any outstanding corrective action items from prior surveillance).
**Skill fit:** FAA Part 145 requirements are publicly available in 14 CFR Part 145 and FAA Order 8900.1 Volume 2 Chapter 5 — the RSM/QCM content requirements, rated personnel qualification standards, and work order documentation requirements are clearly specified. Building the RSM/QCM document management system (a version-controlled document store with amendment tracking), personnel qualification tracker (a per-employee certification database with currency monitoring), work order documentation monitor, calibrated tooling tracker, and surveillance readiness dashboard are data management problems the builder's engineering skills address directly. Aviation Technical Education Council (ATEC) and Aeronautical Repair Station Association (ARSA) member directories provide defined buyer communities.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build an RSM/QCM document management system with version control and amendment tracking, a rated personnel qualification tracker with currency alerts, a calibrated tooling tracking system with expiration alerts, and an FAA surveillance readiness documentation package generator; first 5-8 Part 145 repair station clients through ARSA member outreach while the RSM/QCM management system is validated against real repair station document revision processes.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through ARSA (Aeronautical Repair Station Association) member network and ATEC (Aviation Technical Education Council) outreach (the concentrated professional communities for Part 145 repair station operators — ARSA's annual conference is the primary industry event), FAA FSDO regional office referral networks (aviation maintenance inspectors at FSDOs who conduct Part 145 surveillance visits see RSM/QCM currency gaps and personnel qualification documentation deficiencies in every surveillance visit and informally refer systematic compliance tools to repair stations they've found deficient), and aviation insurance broker referral networks (aviation insurers who write product liability and hangar keepers insurance for repair stations see documentation gaps in every policy renewal and actively refer compliance management tools to their repair station clients).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per Part 145 repair station: $400-900/mo (depending on rating count and personnel count — a 15-technician repair station with airframe and powerplant ratings: $400/mo; a 40-technician facility with multiple limited ratings, avionics authorization, and complex tooling calibration requirements: $900/mo). Managing 20-30 repair station retainers at $600/mo average = **$12,000-18,000/mo**. FAA surveillance preparation documentation packages (one-time $600-1,500 per station when a FSDO schedules a formal surveillance visit) generate significant supplemental revenue. Reaching 20-30 retainers requires 3-4 ARSA chapter relationships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** RSM/QCM revisions must be submitted to the FAA whenever procedures change — an active repair station processing new aircraft types or equipment has RSM/QCM amendment events continuously. Rated personnel qualifications must be maintained continuously for every active technician. Calibrated tooling expires throughout the year on different schedules for different instruments. FAA Part 145 surveillance visits occur on an unpredictable schedule, creating continuous readiness requirements.
**Biggest risk:** A repair station that uses a procedure that differs from its FAA-approved RSM — because the RSM wasn't updated when the procedure changed — is operating outside its approved certificate limits, which can result in FAA certificate suspension. Build the RSM/QCM document management system with a procedure change alert: when the repair station manager logs a procedure change in the system, the system generates a draft RSM amendment for FAA submission and marks the affected section of the RSM as "pending update" until the amended version receives FAA approval confirmation.
**Growth path:** Side project → RSM/QCM document management, personnel qualification tracker, and calibrated tooling system deployed, first 5-8 repair station retainers through ARSA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-12 repair station retainers, FAA surveillance documentation packages generating supplemental revenue (~$4-7K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-30 repair station retainers, tooling calibration tracking becoming a core operational tool for every client, pushing past $12-18K/mo.

### 3. Private Community Water System EPA Safe Drinking Water Act Monitoring & CCR Compliance
**Problem:** Small community water systems (CWSs) and non-transient non-community water systems (NTNCWSs) — private water systems serving schools, factories, subdivisions, campgrounds, mobile home parks, and other communities with their own wells or surface water sources — must comply with EPA's Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) regulations administered by state drinking water programs. Key compliance obligations include: maintaining a certified water system operator (state certification required for the system, with operator certification class matched to the system's type and complexity), completing routine monitoring for coliform bacteria (total coliform rule — monthly sampling for systems above a certain connection count), monitoring for regulated chemical contaminants on the required monitoring schedule (some contaminants monitored annually, some every 3 years, some every 6 years), reporting monitoring results to the state drinking water agency within required timeframes, taking public notification action when a monitoring result exceeds a maximum contaminant level (MCL) — with specific notification methods and timeframes by violation type, and preparing and distributing the annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR) — which must be mailed to all customers by July 1 each year for the prior calendar year, containing the system's monitoring results, any violations, and required public health language. Small private water systems (serving 25-500 connections) have the same federal SDWA compliance obligations as large municipal water systems but with no professional water utility administration staff.
**What to build:** A private community water system SDWA compliance management service — an operator certification and compliance calendar (tracking the system's certified operator: certification number, class level, expiration date, continuing education requirements for renewal — with alerts before certification expiration, plus the system's compliance schedule: monthly coliform sampling dates, chemical contaminant monitoring schedule by contaminant and period, and state sampling plan confirmation), a monitoring results log and violation tracking system (recording each sample result: sample date, sample location, parameter tested, result, and the applicable MCL — automatically flagging any result that exceeds an MCL as a violation requiring immediate state notification and public notification actions, generating the required state violation report in the applicable state's format), a public notification compliance workflow (when a monitoring result triggers a public notification requirement: generating the required Tier 1, 2, or 3 public notification based on the violation type, the notification delivery method required by state, and a confirmation record of notification delivery), a Consumer Confidence Report generator (assembling the annual CCR from the prior year's monitoring results: source water information, regulated contaminant results, any violations and their explanations, required public health language statements — in the format required for the system's state and size tier), and a state drinking water program reporting compliance calendar (tracking all required state reporting deadlines: annual operator certification renewal filings, monitoring data submission deadlines, and any state-required system capacity assessment or sanitary survey preparation).
**Skill fit:** EPA SDWA regulations (40 CFR Parts 141-143) are publicly available — the monitoring frequency schedules, MCL standards, public notification requirements, and CCR content requirements are clearly documented by EPA. State drinking water program requirements are available from each state's primary drinking water agency. Building the monitoring schedule calculator (a per-contaminant, per-system monitoring calendar based on system type and size), violation tracking and public notification workflow, CCR generator (a templated annual report for each system's monitoring data), and state reporting calendar are data management and document generation problems the builder's engineering skills address directly.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build an operator certification and monitoring schedule calendar for the 15 states with the highest number of small private community water systems (TX, PA, CA, FL, OH, NC, VA, MI, NY, WI, GA, MN, IL, IN, KY), a monitoring results log with automatic MCL violation detection, a public notification template generator by violation tier, and a CCR generator pre-formatted for those 15 states; first 5-8 small water system clients through National Rural Water Association (NRWA) member outreach while the monitoring schedule calendar is validated against real system monitoring plan data.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through National Rural Water Association (NRWA) member network and state rural water association outreach (the concentrated professional communities for small water system operators — NRWA's annual conference is the primary industry event), state drinking water program technical assistance provider referral networks (state-funded circuit rider programs and technical assistance providers who help small water systems achieve compliance see monitoring schedule gaps and CCR preparation struggles at every site visit and refer compliance management tools to the systems they assist), and rural water system attorney referral networks (attorneys who defend small water systems in state enforcement actions encounter monitoring schedule failures and CCR deficiencies in every case and refer systematic compliance tools to systems in their network that are not yet under enforcement).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per water system: $150-350/mo (depending on system complexity and connection count — a 50-connection subdivision well system with standard monitoring requirements: $150/mo; a 400-connection mobile home park with multiple source water types and a complex state monitoring plan: $350/mo). Managing 50-80 system retainers at $250/mo average = **$12,500-20,000/mo**. Annual CCR preparation packages (one-time $150-300 per system for the annual Consumer Confidence Report assembly and state filing) generate concentrated May-June revenue. Reaching 50-80 system retainers is achievable given an estimated 50,000+ small community water systems in the US — a very small market fraction. Reaching 50-80 requires 4-6 NRWA chapter relationships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Monthly coliform sampling is required for every CWS regardless of size — a continuous monthly compliance event for every active client. Chemical contaminant monitoring occurs on rolling schedules throughout the year. Annual CCR preparation is required every year by every CWS. EPA and state drinking water enforcement actions targeting small systems with monitoring schedule violations are increasing — creating consistent urgency for systematic compliance documentation.
**Biggest risk:** A water system that misses a required monitoring sample — and does not report the missed sample to the state within the required timeframe — incurs a monitoring violation even if the water would have met all MCL standards. Build the monitoring schedule calendar with a completion confirmation workflow: each scheduled monitoring event requires a result entry or a missed-sample notation in the system before the monitoring period closes, and any monitoring period that closes without a logged result automatically generates a missed-sample violation flag and a draft state notification.
**Growth path:** Side project → monitoring schedule calendar, results log with MCL violation detection, and CCR generator for 15 target states deployed, first 5-8 system retainers through NRWA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 20-25 system retainers, CCR preparation packages generating concentrated June revenue (~$4-7K/mo). ### 4. Auto Dealer Finance & Insurance Office FTC Safeguards Rule & CFPB Credit Compliance
**Problem:** Auto dealers' finance and insurance (F&I) offices that arrange financing for vehicle purchasers are regulated as financial institutions under the FTC's Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act Safeguards Rule — and the FTC's 2023 expanded Safeguards Rule has significantly increased compliance requirements for auto dealers. The updated Safeguards Rule requires dealers to implement a written Information Security Program (ISP) that includes: a designated qualified individual responsible for the ISP, an annual risk assessment documenting information security risks in the F&I office's customer data handling, implementation of administrative, technical, and physical safeguards (multi-factor authentication for any employee accessing customer financial information remotely, encryption of customer financial data in transit and at rest, access controls limiting employee data access to need-to-know), annual employee training documentation, service provider oversight agreements with any vendor accessing customer data, and an incident response plan. Beyond the Safeguards Rule, dealers that arrange credit must comply with FTC Holder Rule requirements (the required FTC Holder Notice must appear in every consumer credit contract), ECOA adverse action notice requirements when a credit application is denied or approved on different terms than requested, and FCRA requirements for handling credit report information. Small and mid-size dealerships (selling 200-1,000 vehicles per year) face these compliance requirements without a dedicated compliance officer.
**What to build:** An auto dealer F&I office FTC Safeguards Rule and CFPB compliance management service — a Safeguards Rule ISP compliance framework (maintaining the dealer's written ISP document with all required sections: qualified individual designation, risk assessment documentation, safeguard implementation records, employee training log, and service provider assessment records — with annual review and update alerts), an annual risk assessment workflow (guiding the dealer through the required annual information security risk assessment: documenting each type of customer financial data collected in the F&I office, identifying risks in each data handling process, and documenting the security controls implemented to mitigate each identified risk), a FTC Safeguards employee training tracker (for each F&I office employee: annual information security training completion records, training content coverage of required topics, and training delivery confirmation — with alerts when annual training is due for each employee), a service provider oversight documentation system (for each vendor or service provider that accesses or handles customer data — DMS providers, credit bureau access, dealer management platform — maintaining a current contract review, data handling assessment, and oversight monitoring record as required by the Safeguards Rule), and an ECOA and FCRA compliance checklist generator (generating a per-credit-application compliance checklist: ECOA adverse action notice requirement triggered when applicable, FTC Holder Notice inclusion in financing contracts, FCRA consumer disclosure obligations when credit information is used, and regulatory agency contact information disclosure).
**Skill fit:** The FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314, as amended effective June 2023) is publicly available — the specific required ISP elements, qualified individual requirements, risk assessment components, and service provider oversight requirements are clearly documented in the FTC's Safeguards Rule and the FTC's compliance guidance for auto dealers. CFPB ECOA adverse action notice requirements and FCRA consumer notice requirements are publicly available in the applicable regulations and CFPB compliance bulletins. Building the ISP document framework (a structured ISP document generator), risk assessment workflow, employee training tracker, service provider oversight system, and ECOA/FCRA checklist generator are data management and document generation problems the builder's engineering skills address directly.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build an FTC Safeguards Rule ISP document generator with all required sections, an annual risk assessment workflow, an employee training completion tracker, a service provider oversight documentation system, and an ECOA/FCRA per-application compliance checklist; first 5-10 dealer F&I clients through NADA (National Automobile Dealers Association) dealer compliance conference outreach and dealer twenty-group networks while the ISP document generator is validated against FTC Safeguards Rule requirements.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through NADA Dealer Compliance Conference and state automobile dealer association outreach (the concentrated professional communities for dealer F&I managers and compliance officers), auto dealer DMS platform referral networks (Reynolds & Reynolds, CDK Global, and Dealertrack whose F&I modules touch the same customer data covered by the Safeguards Rule and whose users are actively seeking Safeguards compliance tools), and auto dealer insurance company referral networks (dealers' commercial lines insurers who write cyber liability policies for dealerships are increasingly asking dealers about FTC Safeguards compliance during policy applications and refer compliance management tools to dealerships that lack documented ISPs).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per dealership: $250-600/mo (depending on F&I volume and vendor complexity — a single-point dealership selling 200 vehicles/year with standard F&I workflow: $250/mo; a multi-point dealer group selling 800 vehicles/year across 3 locations with complex DMS and credit bureau vendor oversight requirements: $600/mo). Managing 30-50 dealership retainers at $375/mo average = **$11,250-18,750/mo**. Annual ISP compliance review packages (one-time $300-600 per dealership for the annual Safeguards Rule compliance review and risk assessment update) generate concentrated revenue. Reaching 30-50 dealership retainers requires 4-6 NADA regional association relationships.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** FTC Safeguards Rule ISP annual reviews are required every year for every dealer. Employee training must be completed annually for every F&I office employee. Service provider oversight assessments are triggered whenever a new vendor is onboarded. Every credit application processed by the F&I office generates an ECOA and FCRA compliance verification event. ### 5. Behavioral Health Telehealth Platform Multi-State Therapist Licensing & Supervision Compliance
**Problem:** Behavioral health telehealth platforms — companies that connect clients with licensed therapists, counselors, and psychologists via video sessions — must manage a unique multi-state licensing compliance challenge: a therapist must be licensed in the state where the client is located at the time of the session (not the state where the therapist is physically located or licensed as their home state). A therapist based in Texas who serves a client who is traveling in California must be licensed in California for that session to be legal. Managing this obligation across a platform with 50-500 therapists, each licensed in different combinations of states, serving clients who may travel, requires systematic matching logic and therapist license monitoring. The PSYPACT compact (for psychologists) and the Counseling Compact (for LPCs/LCPCs) allow participating therapists to practice across compact member states with an additional "authority to practice interjurisdictional telepsychology" credential — tracking each therapist's compact participation status and authorization level is an additional compliance monitoring requirement. Platforms that allow pre-licensed therapists (provisionally licensed counselors and MFTs supervised toward full licensure) to serve clients must also maintain systematic clinical supervision documentation: each pre-licensed therapist's supervision hours by week, supervisor name and credentials, and progression toward the full licensure hour requirement.
**What to build:** A behavioral health telehealth platform therapist licensing and supervision compliance service — a multi-state therapist license portfolio manager (for each therapist on the platform: all current state licenses with license numbers, expiration dates, license type, and any restrictions or probationary conditions — with alerts when any license is approaching expiration and a flag when a license is in a state where the platform has clients who might be served by that therapist), a client-therapist state match compliance gate (for each scheduled session: verifying that the assigned therapist has an active license in the state where the client is located — if not, generating an automatic notification that the session requires re-assignment or the therapist needs to obtain a temporary license), a PSYPACT and Counseling Compact participation tracker (for each participating therapist: compact membership status, authority to practice interjurisdictional telepsychology credential status and expiration, and the current list of compact member states — updated when new states join the compact), a pre-licensed therapist supervision documentation system (for each pre-licensed therapist: supervisor name and license, weekly supervision hours, supervision modality — individual vs. group, face-to-face vs. remote, the running cumulative hour total toward the state's full licensure requirement, and any documentation required by the state board for the supervision relationship), and a therapist credential verification and onboarding workflow (confirming each therapist's state licenses through primary source verification with the applicable state board before the therapist is approved to see clients on the platform — and maintaining the verification record as required by CMS telehealth credentialing requirements for any platform participating in telehealth reimbursement programs).
**Skill fit:** State behavioral health licensure requirements, PSYPACT compact states and requirements, Counseling Compact states and requirements, and pre-licensure supervision requirements are publicly available from PSYPACT, the Counseling Compact Commission, and each state's licensing board. Building the multi-state therapist license portfolio manager (a per-therapist, per-state license database with expiration monitoring), client-therapist state match compliance gate (a pre-session license state verification check), compact participation tracker, supervision documentation system, and credential verification workflow are data management problems the builder's engineering skills address directly. American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy (AAMFT) and NBCC (National Board for Certified Counselors) networks provide access to the buyer community.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a multi-state therapist license portfolio manager with expiration alerts and state coverage visualization, a client-therapist state match gate for the 15 highest-telehealth-utilization states (CA, TX, FL, NY, PA, IL, OH, NC, GA, WA, CO, VA, AZ, MI, MA), a PSYPACT and Counseling Compact status tracker, and a pre-licensed therapist supervision hour log; first 5-8 telehealth platform clients through AAMFT member network and behavioral health telehealth platform community outreach while the therapist license portfolio manager is validated against real platform therapist roster data.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through American Telemedicine Association (ATA) mental health telehealth community and AAMFT member networks (the concentrated professional communities for behavioral health telehealth platform operators), state board of psychology and counseling association referral networks (state boards that receive license renewal applications from telehealth therapists inform therapists about multi-state practice licensing requirements and refer compliance management tools to therapists building multi-state practices), and behavioral health venture capital community networks (investors in behavioral health telehealth platforms require systematic licensing compliance as a due diligence checkpoint and refer compliance management tools to their portfolio companies that lack systematic licensing oversight).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Monthly retainer per telehealth platform: $500-2,000/mo (depending on therapist count — a 30-therapist platform operating in 10 states: $500/mo; a 200-therapist platform with PSYPACT participation, pre-licensed therapists, and multi-state client populations: $2,000/mo). Managing 10-15 platform retainers at $1,100/mo average = **$11,000-16,500/mo**. Therapist onboarding credential verification packages (one-time $50-100 per therapist for initial primary source license verification across all states the therapist holds licenses in) generate consistent per-onboarding revenue as platforms grow their therapist rosters. Reaching 10-15 platform retainers requires 3-4 ATA mental health community relationships.

**Biggest risk:** A telehealth session completed by a therapist who is not licensed in the client's state — because the state match gate wasn't checked for a session scheduled while the client was temporarily in a different state — creates an unlicensed practice of therapy finding against both the therapist and the platform. Build the state match gate as a mandatory pre-session check: no session can be confirmed in the scheduling system without the system verifying that the assigned therapist has an active license listed for the client's current state.
**Growth path:** Side project → multi-state therapist license manager, client-therapist state match gate, and compact participation tracker deployed, first 5-8 platform retainers through ATA mental health community outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 6-8 platform retainers, per-therapist onboarding verification packages generating supplemental revenue (~$4-7K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 10-15 platform retainers, pre-licensed therapist supervision tracking becoming core operational infrastructure for platforms hiring pre-licensed staff, pushing past $11-16K/mo.
