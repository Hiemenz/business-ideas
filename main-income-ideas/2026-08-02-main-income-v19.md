### 1. Funeral Home Post-Death Insurance & Benefit Claims Coordination
**Problem:** When a funeral home provides services to a family, payment often comes from life insurance death benefits, Veterans Affairs burial benefits ($2,000-4,000 for eligible veterans), and the Social Security death benefit ($255). Families routinely assign these benefits to the funeral home at the time of arrangement, but the funeral home must then file the actual claims with each insurer and agency — a process involving different claim forms, death certificate requirements, and processing timelines for every insurer. Small independent funeral homes (which account for the majority of US funeral home locations) handle this manually: one staff member tracking 10-20 open benefit claims at any time across dozens of different life insurance carriers, the VA Benefits Administration, and the Social Security Administration. Missed filing deadlines, incorrect documentation, and carrier follow-up calls that go unreturned cause delays of weeks or months that affect the funeral home's cash flow directly. A claims coordination service manages the entire benefit collection workflow on behalf of the funeral home, reducing uncollected or delayed receivables.
**What to build:** A funeral home death benefit claims coordination service — an arrangement intake workflow (collecting the list of assigned benefits at the time of funeral arrangements: insurer names, policy numbers, and estimated benefit amounts), a claim filing workflow (preparing and submitting the specific claim form required by each insurer, with death certificate and funeral director certification attached), a carrier-specific documentation tracker (maintaining a database of each carrier's claim form, document requirements, and processing timeline to eliminate repetitive research), a VA burial benefit filing service (preparing and submitting VA Form 21P-530 with the required military discharge documentation and death certificate), a Social Security death benefit notification (submitting the death notification through the SSA's funeral director process), a claims status tracking and follow-up calendar (flagging pending claims that exceed each insurer's published processing window and initiating follow-up), and a proceeds distribution confirmation (verifying that carrier payments are credited correctly to the funeral home's account) — charged as a percentage of collected benefits plus a flat monthly coordination fee.
**Skill fit:** The carrier documentation database, claim filing workflow, and follow-up tracking calendar are directly the builder's structured data and workflow automation strengths — managing 10-20 concurrent benefit claims across 15-30 different carrier forms is exactly the kind of multi-entity, multi-status tracking problem the builder systematizes faster than a funeral home administrator juggling it manually between phone calls. The VA benefit filing and SSA notification processes are well-defined federal procedures with published form instructions and explicit documentation requirements that map cleanly into a filing checklist.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a claim intake form, a carrier documentation database (populating the 25-30 most common life insurance carriers' claim form and documentation requirements, which are available publicly on each carrier's funeral director claims portal), and a claim status tracking dashboard; the first 3-5 funeral home clients are onboarded manually while the carrier database is validated against real claim submissions.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through state funeral director associations (each state has a licensed funeral director association with regular member communications and a vendor directory — the NFDA and state affiliates are the primary channel), funeral home accounting and business management consultants (who already work with funeral homes on financial operations and know which homes have receivables problems), and mortuary schools' alumni networks (where new funeral home operators are finding their first professional resources).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** 3-5% of benefits collected as a coordination fee; average benefit claim package per death case: $8,000-25,000 (one or more life insurance policies + VA + SS); coordination fee: $240-1,250 per completed case. A funeral home serving 150-200 families/year has 150-200 benefit coordination cases: managing 5-6 funeral home clients each with 150 cases/year = 750-900 cases/year × $450 average fee = **$337,500-405,000/year**, but realistic first-year billing is 2-3 clients × 150 cases × $400 = **$90,000-180,000/year** gross ($7,500-15,000/mo), with expenses being primarily labor (the builder's own time initially) and technology. The fixed monthly coordination fee per funeral home adds $300-700/mo per client regardless of case volume.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Funeral home case volume is steady and recession-proof — mortality rates don't change with economic cycles, and a funeral home that assigns all benefit coordination to this service doesn't reassign it back in-house without significant operational disruption. Each funeral home relationship generates a predictable recurring case flow: a 150-case/year home generates a case every 2.4 days, creating a constant workflow rather than a lumpy project pipeline.
**Biggest risk:** Insurance carrier claim denials or delays are outside the service's control but land on the funeral home's cash flow — make clear in all client agreements that the service manages the filing and follow-up process but does not guarantee payment timelines or claim approval, since denial disputes may require the funeral home's attorney for resolution. Carrier denial rates are typically low for death benefit claims (1-3%), but establishing a denial dispute escalation protocol from the start prevents client frustration when it does occur.
**Growth path:** Side project → carrier documentation database built for 25 most common insurers, first 1-2 funeral home clients onboarded, first VA and life insurance claims filed and collected. Replaces-part-time-income → 3-4 funeral home clients, 50-60 active cases at any time, carrier follow-up workflow reducing average collection time below industry baseline (~$4-8K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 5-7 funeral home clients, 100+ concurrent cases, automated carrier follow-up reducing per-case labor, pushing past $8-15K/mo.

### 2. Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) Contract Audit for Self-Insured Employers
**Problem:** Mid-size employers who self-insure their employee health benefits (a common structure for businesses with 50-500 employees) contract with a Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) — typically CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, or OptumRx — to administer their prescription drug benefit. PBM contracts are notoriously opaque: the PBM earns revenue through spread pricing (charging the employer more for a drug than the PBM pays the pharmacy), DIR fees (Direct and Indirect Remuneration rebates that reduce the PBM's reported cost but may not be fully passed to the employer), and generic substitution discrepancies (billing at brand price while dispensing generic). Employers routinely overpay their PBMs by 10-25% of total pharmacy spend due to these practices, but most lack the claims data access and methodology knowledge to identify the overcharges. A PBM audit service analyzes the employer's pharmacy claims data against the PBM contract terms to quantify overcharges and support contract renegotiation or carrier switching.
**What to build:** A PBM contract audit service — a contract review and term extraction tool (analyzing the employer's PBM contract to identify spread pricing tolerance bands, rebate pass-through guarantees, audit rights provisions, and pricing benchmark definitions such as AWP, WAC, or MAC), a claims data extraction and normalization workflow (collecting the employer's pharmacy claims feed, normalizing drug identifiers to NDC codes, and mapping each claim to the applicable contract pricing term), a pricing discrepancy analysis model (comparing each claim's billed amount against the benchmark pricing the contract specifies, calculating the per-claim and cumulative overcharge), a DIR fee reconciliation service (comparing the PBM's reported DIR fee credits against contractual pass-through guarantee terms), a generic substitution accuracy check (identifying brand drugs billed at brand rates where a generic equivalent was available and should have been substituted under the formulary terms), and an audit findings report and renegotiation support package (summarizing overcharges by category with supporting claim-level evidence, formatted for use in a contract dispute or carrier-switching RFP) — charged as a flat audit fee plus a contingency fee on overcharges recovered through PBM settlement or contract renegotiation.
**Skill fit:** The pharmacy claims data normalization, pricing discrepancy analysis model, and DIR fee reconciliation are directly the builder's core data engineering and analytics skill set — this is a pharmaceutical billing data pipeline and financial analysis problem where the builder's ability to build the claims-to-contract comparison model faster and more accurately than a manual auditor using Excel is the primary value driver. The PBM contract term extraction layer is a document analysis problem that maps contract language to pricing formula parameters.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the claims data normalization pipeline (mapping NDC codes to drug names, therapeutic categories, and brand/generic status using publicly available NDC databases), the AWP-based pricing comparison model (AWP pricing data is commercially available through Red Book or Medi-Span), and the audit findings report template; the first 2-3 audit engagements are completed manually while the pricing model is validated against real PBM claims data.
**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — distribute through employee benefits brokers and consultants (who advise self-insured employers on health plan design and have strong financial incentive to deliver PBM savings that justify their own fees), the Self-Insurance Institute of America (SIIA) member network, and CFOs at self-insured companies in the 100-500 employee range (reachable through LinkedIn outreach by industry and employer size in target geographies).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Flat audit fee: $3,500-8,000 per audit (depending on claims volume); contingency fee: 20-25% of overcharges recovered through PBM settlement. A 200-employee company with $600,000/year in PBM pharmacy spend has a 15% overcharge rate = $90,000 in annual overcharges; 22% contingency fee on a 12-month recovery = $19,800 per engagement. Combined flat + contingency = **$23,000-28,000 per completed audit engagement**. Processing 1-2 audits/month = **$23,000-56,000/mo** at scale; realistic 12-month target of 6-8 audits/year = **$12,000-20,000/mo** average.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Self-insured employer PBM contracts run 3-year terms; every contract renewal is an audit event (the employer should audit before renewing to establish the overcharge baseline for renegotiation). A completed audit generates a natural 3-year renewal touchpoint that maintains the client relationship without active re-selling. Benefits broker referral relationships generate annual audit referrals as brokers renew clients' benefit designs each year — one active benefits broker relationship generates 3-5 audit referrals per year.
**Biggest risk:** PBM audit rights vary by contract — some PBM contracts contain limited audit rights or require advance written notice with a narrow audit window that the PBM controls. Before accepting an audit engagement, review the employer's contract audit rights clause to confirm access to the claims data level needed for the analysis, and build into the audit scope a preliminary contract audit rights assessment that identifies any data access limitations before full engagement fees are committed.
**Growth path:** Side project → claims normalization pipeline and AWP pricing model validated, first 1-2 audit engagements through benefits broker introductions, first PBM settlement recovered and contingency fee collected. Replaces-part-time-income → 3-4 benefits broker referral relationships active, 4-6 audit engagements/year, contingency fees supplementing flat audit income (~$6-12K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 8-10 audits/year, benefits broker referral pipeline producing consistent annual engagement volume, contract renegotiation support service added as a premium tier, pushing past $15-25K/mo.

### 3. Virtual Family Office Administration for Emerging Wealth
**Problem:** High-income professionals — doctors, attorneys, corporate executives, and tech employees with $1-5M in net worth — need the functions of a family office but can't access one: single-family offices (which provide comprehensive financial life management) typically require $20M+ in assets, and the $1-5M segment is served by financial advisors who manage investments but don't coordinate the full financial picture. These clients have 3-6 financial relationships (CPA, financial advisor, estate attorney, insurance agent, mortgage lender) that never talk to each other, pay bills across 15-30 accounts that are never aggregated, receive insurance renewal notices that never get reviewed, and have estate documents that were drafted five years ago and haven't been updated since the second child was born. The virtual family office fills this coordination gap: one relationship that ensures all the financial and legal pieces connect, nothing falls through the cracks, and the client isn't managing their own financial life in stolen hours between patient appointments.
**What to build:** A virtual family office service — a financial relationship inventory and coordination system (cataloging every financial relationship, account, insurance policy, and legal document the client holds, with renewal dates and contact information for each advisor), a monthly financial dashboard (aggregating net worth, cash flow, insurance coverage summary, and upcoming financial events in a single report the client reviews in 30 minutes/month), a vendor coordination service (acting as the liaison between the client's CPA, financial advisor, estate attorney, and insurance agent — scheduling their annual financial review meeting, ensuring the CPA has the data they need before tax season, ensuring the estate attorney is notified of life events that trigger document updates), a bill payment and account oversight service (monitoring that recurring bills are paid, flagging unusual charges or lapsed policies, coordinating with the financial advisor on cash management), and an insurance coverage review service (reviewing all P&C, life, disability, and umbrella policies annually for coverage gaps and premium optimization) — charged as a monthly retainer tiered by asset level and complexity.
**Skill fit:** The financial inventory system, multi-advisor coordination workflow, and monthly dashboard are structured data management and relationship coordination problems — the builder's data skills directly apply to building a net worth aggregation and financial event tracking system that provides more comprehensive visibility than any single financial advisor's client portal. The value delivered is primarily organizational and coordination intelligence, not financial advice, which means the service operates outside the regulatory scope that would require investment advisor registration.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a client financial inventory template (cataloging accounts, policies, advisor contacts, and document currency), a monthly dashboard report template, and a vendor coordination calendar; the first 2-3 client engagements are delivered manually while the systems are refined against real client financial complexity.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through CPA firms that serve high-income professionals (the CPA is the most trusted financial relationship for most of this demographic and is well-positioned to identify clients whose financial lives are fragmented), estate planning attorneys (who frequently see clients with outdated estate plans and no one managing the update cycle), and independent financial advisors who want to offer a premium advisory experience but can't deliver the operational coordination themselves.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $750-2,500/mo per client, tiered by asset level and coordination complexity. 20 active client relationships at $1,200/mo average = **$24,000/mo**. Reaching 20 clients requires building referral relationships with 3-5 CPA firms and financial advisors — a realistic 18-24 month accumulation, with the first 5 clients achievable within 6 months at one strong CPA referral source. The service is sticky by design: a client who has outsourced their financial coordination doesn't voluntarily add it back; every life event (new child, home purchase, job change, inheritance) generates additional coordination value that deepens the relationship.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Each client generates compounding value over time — the coordinator becomes the institutional memory of the client's financial life, accumulating context that makes switching to a different service increasingly costly. CPA referral relationships generate new client flow annually as the CPA's client base accumulates more wealth over time, creating an organic growth path without cold prospecting. The $1-5M wealth segment is underserved by genuine comprehensive financial coordination and growing rapidly as tech industry wealth, healthcare consolidation, and professional income compound for a large cohort of high earners.
**Biggest risk:** The virtual family office service skirts the boundary of investment advice in some jurisdictions — reviewing insurance coverage and coordinating financial advisors can be interpreted as financial planning that requires RIA registration. Structure the service explicitly as financial organization and vendor coordination (not investment advice or financial planning), maintain clear written scope of service agreements, and engage a financial services attorney to review the service design before onboarding the first client to ensure the coordination-only model is properly bounded.
**Growth path:** Side project → first 2-3 clients onboarded through CPA introductions, financial inventory and monthly dashboard workflow validated, vendor coordination calendar refined against real multi-advisor scenarios. Replaces-part-time-income → 8-10 clients, 2 CPA referral relationships active, monthly report templates systematized to reduce per-client time (~$8-12K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 18-22 clients, financial advisor and estate attorney referral channels both active, premium tier (asset management coordination for $5M+ clients) generating higher per-client revenue, pushing past $20-24K/mo.

### 4. Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) Review & Summarization Service
**Problem:** Every prospective franchise buyer receives a Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) of 200-500 pages that they must review during a mandatory 14-day "waiting period" before signing a franchise agreement. The FDD contains 23 standardized Items required by the FTC, but the most consequential sections — Item 19 (financial performance representations, if any), Item 20 (outlet table showing how many franchises have opened, closed, and been transferred), Item 21 (audited financial statements of the franchisor), and the litigation history in Items 3-4 — are dense, formatted inconsistently across franchise systems, and require specific knowledge to interpret correctly. Most buyers either rely entirely on the franchise broker who earns a commission on the sale, or hire an attorney at $400-600/hour who produces a general legal review rather than a business-focused comparative analysis. A flat-fee FDD review and summarization service produces a structured summary report that extracts the commercially significant findings (unit economics if Item 19 is provided, franchisee validation data from Item 20, financial health of the franchisor from Item 21, and warning signs in the litigation history) within the 14-day review window.
**What to build:** An FDD review and summarization service — an FDD intake workflow (receiving the FDD file and identifying which franchise system it is and what state's addendum is included), an Item-by-Item extraction template (systematically reviewing each of the 23 FDD Items against a checklist of commercially significant facts: royalty and fee structure, renewal and termination rights, protected territory definition, supply chain restrictions and markups, and any earnings claim methodology in Item 19), a unit-level economics calculator (if Item 19 is provided, building a normalized P&L model from the disclosed revenue percentages and benchmarking against industry operating cost structures for the franchise category), an Item 20 trend analysis (computing open, closed, and transferred outlet counts by year to identify whether the system is growing, contracting, or experiencing high franchisee turnover), a red-flag identification checklist (flagging litigation patterns, termination without cause clauses, unilateral renewal terms, and mandatory vendor restrictions with potential markup), and a structured summary report (a 5-10 page executive summary with a decision framework: what to validate with existing franchisees, what to negotiate in the franchise agreement, and which risk items require further diligence) — charged as a flat fee per FDD review.
**Skill fit:** The Item-by-Item extraction, unit economics calculator, and Item 20 trend analysis are exactly the builder's data analysis and financial modeling skill set applied to a standardized document format (all FDDs follow the same 23-Item structure by FTC regulation, making the extraction workflow highly systematizable). The red-flag identification checklist is a rule-set-applied-to-document-content problem — the FTC's Item-specific disclosure requirements define exactly what should be present and what omissions are legally significant, mapping directly into a structured review checklist.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the 23-Item extraction template, a unit economics P&L model for 3-5 common franchise categories (food service, home services, fitness), and the Item 20 outlet trend calculator; the first 5-10 FDD reviews are completed manually while the extraction template is refined across different franchise systems and their varying disclosure quality.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through franchise brokers (who earn 40-50% of the franchise fee as commission and benefit from clients who are better-prepared — a confused buyer who backs out at signing costs the broker their entire commission, making a good FDD review a deal-saver rather than a competitor), franchise buyer communities (r/Entrepreneur franchise discussions, Franchise Times reader communities, and the Vetfran community for veteran franchise buyers who move through the FDD process with less prior business experience), and YouTube channels that cover franchise evaluation and due diligence.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $400-800 per FDD review. Processing 25-30 FDD reviews/month (each taking 6-10 hours of structured review time) = $10,000-24,000/mo. Reaching 25-30 reviews/month requires 5-8 active franchise broker referral relationships (each broker closes 3-8 franchise sales/year, generating 3-8 FDD review referrals/year) plus direct buyer acquisition through YouTube and community content that demonstrates the value of a structured FDD review. At 10 hours per review and 25 reviews/month = 250 hours — unsustainable solo; build a parameterized extraction workflow that reduces per-review time to 4-5 hours, making 25/month achievable as a solo operation at full utilization.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every FDD filing expires annually (franchisors must update their FDD each year and re-register in registration states), so a franchise system that had 100 franchisee candidates last year generates another 100+ prospective buyers this year — the review volume for any popular franchise system is recurring and growing as the franchise expands. Franchise broker relationships are particularly high-leverage: a broker who refers 5-6 FDD reviews per year and sees every client better-prepared for their validation calls and franchise agreement negotiations will continue referring indefinitely.
**Biggest risk:** The FDD review service must be explicitly positioned as a business analysis and summarization service, not legal advice — franchise attorneys provide legal review of the franchise agreement (the binding contract), which is different from this service's analytical review of the disclosure document. Any client who confuses the two and makes a legal claim based on the summary report creates liability; build clear scope-of-service agreements that define the service as commercial due diligence analysis, not legal counsel, and recommend the client engage a franchise attorney for the agreement review.
**Growth path:** Side project → 23-Item extraction template and unit economics calculator validated across 5-10 different franchise systems, first 5-10 reviews completed through direct buyer outreach in franchise communities. Replaces-part-time-income → 5-8 franchise broker referral relationships, 12-15 reviews/month, Item 20 trend analysis database growing as the same franchise systems recur across multiple review engagements (~$5-10K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 25-30 reviews/month with extraction workflow reducing per-review time, broker referral network generating consistent deal flow, pushing past $10-24K/mo.

### 5. Healthcare Practice Acquisition Due Diligence Service
**Problem:** Primary care clinics, urgent care centers, physical therapy practices, dermatology groups, and behavioral health practices sell frequently — to other practitioners, to private equity-backed management companies doing roll-ups, and to health systems acquiring ambulatory capacity. The buyer in each transaction needs comprehensive due diligence before closing: payor mix analysis (what percentage of revenue is Medicare/Medicaid vs. commercial insurance vs. self-pay), billing quality review (AR aging, denial rates, write-off patterns), credentialing status verification (confirming each provider's credentialing with every payor before the transaction closes, since credentialing gaps in the acquired practice become the buyer's problem immediately post-close), HIPAA compliance posture assessment, malpractice claims and tail coverage review, and lease and facility assessment. Most small practice buyers navigate this without a dedicated due diligence service because they assume their attorney handles it — but attorneys review the legal documents, not the operational and billing data.
**What to build:** A healthcare practice acquisition due diligence service — a practice data room request workflow (specifying the exact data package needed from the selling practice: 3 years of billing system export data, payor contracts, AR aging by payor and age bucket, credentialing files for each provider, malpractice certificates, and lease documents), a payor mix and revenue concentration analysis (building the revenue waterfall by payor, identifying any payor-specific dependence risk, and calculating the net collection rate and days-in-AR for each major payor), a billing quality assessment (reviewing the denial rate by payor and denial code category, identifying systematic billing problems that will persist post-acquisition, and calculating the recoverable revenue from the AR aging), a credentialing gap analysis (verifying each provider's active credentialing status with each payor before closing, identifying any lapsed or suspended credentials that would interrupt billing post-close), a HIPAA and EHR data migration assessment (reviewing whether the practice's EHR system supports data export and whether there are active BAA agreements with all relevant vendors), and a due diligence findings report (a structured summary of findings by risk category: billing and revenue, credentialing, compliance, and operational) — charged as a flat project fee per engagement.
**Skill fit:** The payor mix analysis, AR aging waterfall, denial rate analysis, and credentialing gap tracking are directly the builder's data engineering and analytics strengths — healthcare billing data is structured (CPT codes, payor IDs, claim status codes), and the analysis of a practice's billing performance is a quantitative modeling problem that the builder solves faster and more systematically than a healthcare attorney reviewing the same data manually. Credentialing gap tracking is a structured data management problem: each provider's active credentialing status with each payor is a dataset that must be verified against the payor's provider roster.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a data room request template, a payor mix and AR aging analysis model (using publicly available CMS payor identifier data to normalize insurer names across different billing systems), a denial category analysis template, and a credentialing gap tracker; the first 2-3 due diligence engagements are managed manually while the analysis models are validated against real practice billing data.
**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — distribute through healthcare M&A attorneys (who handle the legal side of every practice acquisition and know the buyer lacks operational due diligence support), healthcare practice brokers (who bring buyers and sellers together and need the deal to close cleanly — a post-close billing problem that surfaces an undisclosed credentialing gap kills the broker's referral reputation), and private equity-backed management companies doing roll-up acquisitions in physician practice specialties (who close multiple acquisitions per year and need a repeatable due diligence vendor).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $4,500-9,000 per engagement (depending on practice size and number of providers; a 3-provider primary care practice warrants a different fee than an 8-provider urgent care center). Processing 2-3 engagements/month = **$9,000-27,000/mo**. Realistic 12-month target: 15-20 engagements at $6,000 average = **$7,500-10,000/mo**. PE-backed roll-up clients add volume: a platform making 8-10 acquisitions/year becomes a recurring client generating 8-10 engagements/year from a single relationship.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** PE-backed healthcare roll-ups are the highest-volume buyer in this market, and each platform company maintains an acquisition pipeline of 5-15 target practices at any time — landing one platform client means 5-15 due diligence engagements/year from a single relationship. Each completed engagement also generates a dataset of post-close billing outcomes that can be compared against the pre-close due diligence findings, building a predictive model for which billing metrics are the best leading indicators of post-close performance — a proprietary insight no competitor can replicate without the same longitudinal dataset.
**Biggest risk:** Healthcare due diligence touches PHI (protected health information) — billing data exports from medical practices are covered by HIPAA, and receiving this data as a due diligence service provider requires a properly executed Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with each acquiring practice. Establish a standard BAA template before the first engagement, ensure all data handling infrastructure is HIPAA-compliant, and carry appropriate professional liability insurance that covers healthcare data engagements — a data breach during due diligence would be both a regulatory and reputational catastrophe.
**Growth path:** Side project → payor mix analysis model and AR aging waterfall validated against first 2-3 engagements through healthcare M&A attorney referrals, credentialing gap tracker refined across multiple payor types. Replaces-part-time-income → 1-2 engagements/month, first PE roll-up client relationship established, billing quality assessment findings database growing (~$6-10K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 2-3 engagements/month, PE platform client generating recurring annual volume, post-close outcome tracking model generating a proprietary due diligence accuracy dataset, pushing past $12-20K/mo.

## This Session's Pick: #3 for Income Architecture; #2 for Deal Size

**Strongest income architecture:** Idea #3 (virtual family office) has the highest per-client lifetime value of any idea in this series — a client relationship that generates $1,200/mo for 10+ years is worth $144,000 in lifetime revenue, and a 20-client book produces $24,000/mo in fully recurring income. The CPA referral distribution channel is particularly efficient: CPAs have annual touchpoints with every client, making each referral relationship a renewable annual source rather than a one-time lead. The service is also the most defensible in this session — the coordinator becomes the institutional memory of the client's financial life, creating switching costs that deepen with every year of accumulated context.

**Highest single-engagement ceiling:** Idea #2 (PBM audit) has the largest per-engagement revenue of any idea in this session. A 200-employee company with $600K in pharmacy spend generates $23,000-28,000 per engagement including the contingency fee — and the contingency is earned by quantifying real money that the employer was already losing, making the value proposition quantifiable before the engagement starts rather than speculative. Benefits broker distribution is the strongest channel in this idea: a broker who refers one successful PBM audit becomes a repeat referral source as their book of self-insured clients rotates through contract renewal cycles.

**Recommended pairing:** Start with #3 (virtual family office) as the primary income vehicle — the monthly retainer model provides the most predictable income trajectory, and the CPA distribution channel is accessible within the first 30-45 days through warm outreach to CPAs who work with high-income professionals. Build #4 (FDD review) as a volume-driven parallel income stream — the structured extraction workflow makes it systematizable at a pace of 2-3 reviews/week, each generating $400-800 in flat-fee income that supplements the retainer base while the retainer book is being built.
