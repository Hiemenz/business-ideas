### 1. Funeral Home & Crematory FTC Funeral Rule, State Licensure & Preneed Trust Compliance
**Problem:** Funeral directors and embalmers hold a state funeral board license earned through an apprenticeship and the national board exam administered via the International Conference of Funeral Service Examining Boards (ICFSEB), renewed on a 1-2 year cycle with continuing education. Crematories additionally require a separate state crematory license and, in most cases, an operator credentialed through the Cremation Association of North America's (CANA) crematory operator certification program, and every cremation legally requires a signed cremation authorization form from the deceased's legally designated next-of-kin on file before the cremation may proceed — a missing or improperly executed authorization is among the most consequential documentation failures in the profession, since a wrong-body or unauthorized cremation cannot be undone and routinely triggers both a licensing-board complaint and civil litigation. The FTC's Funeral Rule (16 CFR Part 453) requires every funeral home to provide a General Price List (GPL) to anyone who inquires about arrangements in person, and to provide an itemized statement of the goods and services actually selected before payment is accepted — the FTC has run repeated undercover "mystery shopper" compliance sweeps against small funeral homes nationally, and a missing or incomplete GPL is consistently the top violation cited. Preneed funeral contracts (prepaid arrangements) are governed by state preneed trust or insurance-funding statutes requiring the funeral home to deposit a defined percentage of funds into a trust or insurance product within a statutory window — commonly 30 days of receipt — and a firm that fails to timely trust preneed funds risks state funeral board license revocation, a far more severe consequence than an ordinary at-need billing dispute. A small funeral home running 1-3 funeral directors and a crematory manages staggered license/CE renewal, per-case GPL and itemized-statement documentation, per-case cremation authorization verification, and per-contract preneed trust deposit deadlines with no dedicated compliance function — and a case closed without a GPL on file, or a preneed contract whose trust deposit missed its statutory window, is exactly the gap an FTC sweep or state funeral board audit is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-director/embalmer license and CE tracker with a "cleared to serve" gate, plus a separate crematory operator certification tracker; a per-case FTC compliance checklist confirming the GPL was provided and an itemized statement of selected goods/services is signed and on file before a case can be marked closed; a cremation authorization document tracker verifying the legally designated next-of-kin's signed authorization is on file before a cremation case can be marked ready; and a preneed contract trust-funding tracker computing each contract's statutory deposit deadline from the receipt date with a hard alert before it's missed.
**Skill fit:** The FTC Funeral Rule and state preneed trust statutes are public regulatory text with well-defined, computable per-case triggers, and the GPL-compliance checklist plus the preneed deposit-deadline countdown are the same deterministic "block the case close / compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a profession most small, family-owned homes currently run on paper case files with no systematic FTC or trust-deadline cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license/certification tracker, the per-case FTC compliance checklist, and the preneed trust-deposit deadline tracker; land the first 5-8 funeral homes through National Funeral Directors Association (NFDA) state affiliate outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Funeral Directors Association (NFDA) member directory and state funeral directors association meetings, where independent home owners already navigate GPL documentation and preneed trust-deposit deadlines by hand.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $15/mo per licensed director/embalmer tracked. Average client (2 directors) = $249 + $30 = $279/mo. 40-45 homes × $279/mo = **$11,160-12,555/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every case opens its own GPL and cremation-authorization compliance event, every preneed contract generates its own trust-deposit deadline, and crematory operator certification renews on its own independent cycle — a continuous, per-case compliance stream tied directly to case volume.
**Biggest risk:** A case closed without a GPL on file, or a preneed contract whose trust deposit missed its statutory window, is exactly the gap an FTC undercover sweep or a state funeral board audit is built to catch — the FTC compliance checklist and the trust-deposit deadline tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → license/certification tracker, FTC checklist, and preneed trust tracker deployed, first 5-8 home retainers through NFDA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 homes. Replaces-full-time-salary → 40-45 homes, pushing past $11.16-12.56K/mo.

### 2. Fire Alarm & Fire Sprinkler System Contractor NICET Certification, NFPA 72/25 Inspection-Interval Compliance
**Problem:** Fire alarm technicians hold NICET (National Institute for Certification in Engineering Technologies) certification in fire alarm systems, and fire sprinkler technicians typically hold NICET certification in Inspection and Testing of Water-Based Systems, both layered on top of a state fire alarm/fire protection contractor license in the states requiring one, each renewed on independent multi-year cycles with continuing education. NFPA 72 (the National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code) mandates recurring inspection and testing intervals for fire alarm system components — commonly quarterly or semiannual visual inspection depending on device type, and an annual full functional test — while NFPA 25 (Standard for the Inspection, Testing, and Maintenance of Water-Based Fire Protection Systems) mandates its own layered schedule for sprinkler systems — weekly or monthly control-valve checks, monthly gauge inspections, quarterly alarm-device tests, and an annual main drain test — and a missed interval on any single component is consistently the top citation category in state fire marshal system-compliance audits, with the contractor of record who signed the most recent inspection tag bearing direct liability exposure if a deficiency contributes to fire loss. The local Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) requires every completed inspection report filed and a system Impairment Log maintained whenever a fire alarm or sprinkler system is taken out of service for repair — failing to file the impairment notification before shutting a system down, even briefly, is independently citable regardless of how quickly the repair is completed. A small contractor running 3-8 technicians and inspection contracts across dozens to hundreds of buildings manages per-building, per-component NFPA 72/25 interval computation, staggered per-technician NICET certification currency, and AHJ report/impairment-log filing with no dedicated compliance function — and a sprinkler control valve missed on its weekly check, or an alarm system shut down without a filed impairment notice, is exactly the finding an AHJ compliance audit or a post-fire investigation is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-building system inventory (fire alarm devices and sprinkler components by type) with an interval-computation engine generating each component's exact NFPA 72/25-required next-due inspection date; a per-technician NICET certification tracker with a "cleared to inspect" gate matching each tech's certification scope to the system type being serviced; an AHJ inspection-report filing tracker confirming each completed inspection's report was actually submitted; and a system impairment log with a hard block preventing a system shutdown from being logged without a filed impairment notification.
**Skill fit:** NFPA 72 and NFPA 25's interval tables are public, highly structured, per-component schedules (weekly through annual) — a natural data-modeling and deadline-computation problem, and the interval-computation engine plus the technician-certification gate are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a recurring-inspection trade most small contractors currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic per-component due-date engine.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the system inventory with the interval-computation engine, the per-technician NICET certification tracker, and the AHJ filing/impairment-log tracker; land the first 5-8 contractors through National Fire Sprinkler Association (NFSA) chapter outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Fire Sprinkler Association (NFSA) member directory and National Association of Fire Equipment Distributors (NAFED) chapter meetings, where small contractors already navigate NFPA 72/25's layered component-level intervals across hundreds of serviced buildings.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $25/mo per certified technician tracked. Average client (5 technicians) = $199 + $125 = $324/mo. 32-38 contractors × $324/mo = **$10,368-12,312/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every serviced building generates its own multi-component, multi-frequency inspection schedule, NICET certifications renew on staggered independent cycles per technician, and every impairment event opens its own filing requirement — a high-frequency compliance stream tied directly to the contractor's inspection-contract portfolio, not an annual filing.
**Biggest risk:** A sprinkler component missed on its NFPA 25-mandated interval, or a system shut down without a filed impairment notice, is exactly the finding an AHJ compliance audit or post-fire investigation is built to catch — the interval-computation engine and the impairment log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → system inventory, interval engine, and technician tracker deployed, first 5-8 contractor retainers through NFSA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 contractors. Replaces-full-time-salary → 32-38 contractors, pushing past $10.37-12.31K/mo.

### 3. Massage Therapy Establishment State Licensure & Anti-Human-Trafficking Ordinance Compliance
**Problem:** Massage therapists hold a state license, most commonly earned via the MBLEx exam administered through the Federation of State Massage Therapy Boards (FSMTB), renewed on a 1-2 year cycle with continuing education, and in nearly every state the establishment itself must separately hold a massage business permit or license distinct from any individual therapist's credential. A rapidly expanding wave of municipal anti-human-trafficking ordinances — adopted in hundreds of cities and counties specifically in response to illicit massage businesses operating as fronts — imposes establishment-level requirements layered on top of state licensing: mandatory verification and on-file retention of every employee's government ID and proof of massage license, state-mandated human-trafficking-hotline signage (now required by statute in a growing number of states regardless of local ordinance), operating-hour restrictions in some jurisdictions, and criminal background-check documentation for every employee. Because a legitimate, fully licensed massage studio operating near or resembling an illicit operation cannot be distinguished at a glance, local licensing inspectors' spot-checks target exactly the paperwork this compliance gap creates, and a missing employee ID file or an expired individual therapist license discovered during a sweep is enough to trigger a shutdown order regardless of the business's underlying legitimacy. A small studio running 3-8 therapists manages staggered individual license/CE renewal, establishment permit renewal, and per-employee background-check/ID-file compliance across whatever combination of state and local anti-trafficking requirements applies with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-therapist state license and CE tracker with a "cleared to work" gate; an establishment permit renewal tracker; a per-employee background-check and ID/license-file compliance checklist confirming every required document is on file; and a jurisdiction-specific anti-trafficking ordinance checklist (required signage, ID-verification records, hour restrictions) since requirements vary meaningfully by city and county.
**Skill fit:** State massage board licensing rules and the specific municipal anti-trafficking ordinance requirements are public, structured, per-jurisdiction regulatory data, and the employee-file compliance checklist is the same deterministic "confirm every required record is on file" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a licensed wellness trade most small studios currently run on a paper employee binder with no systematic cross-check against a growing, jurisdiction-specific ordinance landscape.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the therapist license tracker with the work-eligibility gate, the establishment permit tracker, and the jurisdiction-specific anti-trafficking checklist; land the first 5-8 studios through American Massage Therapy Association (AMTA) chapter outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Massage Therapy Association (AMTA) member directory and state massage therapy association chapter meetings, where independent studio owners already navigate the growing patchwork of local anti-trafficking ordinance requirements on top of state licensing.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $99/mo base + $15/mo per therapist tracked. Average client (5 therapists) = $99 + $75 = $174/mo. 62-72 studios × $174/mo = **$10,788-12,528/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new hire opens its own background-check/ID-file compliance event, individual therapist licenses renew on staggered independent cycles, and jurisdiction-specific ordinance requirements accumulate as a studio expands locations — a per-employee, per-location compliance stream tied directly to staffing and growth.
**Biggest risk:** A missing employee ID/background-check file or an expired individual license discovered during a local licensing sweep is exactly the finding that triggers a shutdown order regardless of the business's legitimacy — the employee-file checklist and the license tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → therapist license tracker, establishment permit tracker, and ordinance checklist deployed, first 5-8 studio retainers through AMTA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 30-36 studios. Replaces-full-time-salary → 62-72 studios, pushing past $10.79-12.53K/mo.

### 4. Pawnbroker & Secondhand/Precious-Metal Dealer State License, Police-Reporting & Holding-Period Compliance
**Problem:** Pawnbrokers and secondhand/precious-metal dealers operate under a state pawnbroker license backed by a surety bond, and nearly every jurisdiction requires every pawn transaction and secondhand or precious-metal purchase reported to local law enforcement within a short statutory window — commonly 24-48 hours — through systems like LeadsOnline or a state RAPID reporting platform, matching item description and serial number against the seller's verified ID. A transaction not reported within the window is independently citable, and more consequentially, a shop found holding stolen property it failed to report promptly can lose its license entirely regardless of good-faith intent, since the reporting requirement exists specifically to give police a matching window against active theft reports. Every state also imposes a minimum holding period — commonly 15-30 days — before a pawned or purchased item may legally be resold, or a defaulted pawn forfeited and melted, and selling or melting an item before its holding period expires is one of the most common findings in a police compliance audit, since it directly defeats the reporting requirement's purpose. Pawn loans are separately subject to Truth in Lending Act (Regulation Z) disclosure requirements — annual percentage rate, finance charge, and payment schedule — distinct from the disclosure obligations of an ordinary retail purchase. A small shop processing dozens of transactions a day manages per-transaction police-reporting deadlines, per-item holding-period tracking before resale eligibility, and per-loan TILA disclosure documentation with no dedicated compliance function — and an item resold before its holding period cleared, or a transaction reported past its statutory window, is exactly the gap a police compliance audit is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-transaction police-reporting deadline tracker computing the exact due date/time from intake and flagging any transaction approaching its statutory reporting window before it's missed; a per-item holding-period tracker blocking a "cleared to resell/melt" status until the state's minimum holding period has fully elapsed; and a per-loan Truth in Lending Act disclosure checklist (APR, finance charge, payment schedule) confirming the required disclosure was provided before a pawn loan is finalized.
**Skill fit:** State pawnbroker statutes' reporting windows and holding periods are public, computable regulatory data with well-defined per-transaction triggers, and the reporting-deadline countdown plus the resale-eligibility gate are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a high-transaction-volume retail trade most independent shops currently track in a paper ticket book with no systematic deadline or holding-period cross-check.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the police-reporting deadline tracker, the per-item holding-period gate, and the TILA disclosure checklist; land the first 5-8 shops through National Pawnbrokers Association (NPA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Pawnbrokers Association (NPA) member directory and state pawnbroker association meetings, where independent shop owners already navigate high-volume daily reporting deadlines and holding-period tracking by hand.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $50/mo per additional store location tracked. Average client (a shop with 1.5 locations on average across the client base) = $199 + $25 = $224/mo. 47-55 shops × $224/mo = **$10,528-12,320/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every transaction opens its own police-reporting countdown and holding-period clock, and a growing multi-location shop adds reporting volume continuously across every counter — a high-frequency, per-transaction compliance stream tied directly to daily volume.
**Biggest risk:** An item resold or melted before its statutory holding period elapsed, or a transaction reported past its statutory deadline, is exactly the finding a police compliance audit is built to catch — a violation that can cost the shop its license regardless of good-faith intent — the reporting-deadline tracker and the resale-eligibility gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → reporting-deadline tracker, holding-period gate, and TILA checklist deployed, first 5-8 shop retainers through NPA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 24-28 shops. Replaces-full-time-salary → 47-55 shops, pushing past $10.53-12.32K/mo.

### 5. Propane (LP Gas) Dealer State License, NFPA 58 Reconnection Testing & CETP Employee Training Compliance
**Problem:** Propane (LP gas) dealers hold a state LP gas board license for the company, and often individual delivery drivers and service technicians hold their own state credential, both renewed annually. NFPA 58 (the Liquefied Petroleum Gas Code), adopted by reference in most state fire codes, mandates a leak test and visual inspection of a customer's tank and gas system at every reconnection or after any interruption in service, sets specific safety and inspection protocols for bulk-plant and cylinder-filling operations, and requires every employee involved in propane handling or delivery to complete documented, task-specific training through a Certified Employee Training Program (CETP) before performing that specific function — filling cylinders, making bulk deliveries, reconnecting a system — unsupervised. A driver dispatched to reconnect or fill a tank without documented CETP training in that exact task is precisely the finding a state fire marshal or LP gas board safety audit targets, given propane's direct fire and explosion hazard. Separately, DOT/PHMSA regulations require propane cylinders (including standard 20-lb exchange cylinders) to be requalified on a fixed retest cycle — commonly every 12 years, or 5 years for certain cylinder types — and any cylinder past its requalification date is prohibited from being filled or exchanged; a company found filling out-of-date cylinders is a direct DOT/PHMSA citation. A small dealer running several trucks and drivers manages staggered per-employee CETP task certification, per-cylinder requalification-date tracking across an exchange inventory that turns over constantly, and per-customer reconnection leak-test documentation with no dedicated compliance function — and a driver performing a task without documented CETP training, or an out-of-date cylinder re-entering the exchange fleet, is exactly the gap a state fire marshal or PHMSA audit is built to catch.
**What to build:** A per-employee CETP task-certification tracker (cylinder filling, bulk delivery, system reconnection tracked as separate task categories) with a "cleared to perform" gate blocking a dispatch to a task the employee isn't documented as trained on; a per-cylinder DOT requalification-date tracker flagging any cylinder past its retest date before it can re-enter the exchange fleet; and a per-customer tank reconnection leak-test log satisfying NFPA 58's post-interruption inspection requirement.
**Skill fit:** NFPA 58's task-specific training requirements and DOT's cylinder requalification cycle are public, structured regulatory data with computable per-employee and per-cylinder deadlines, and the task-certification gate plus the cylinder requalification tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fuel-delivery trade most small dealers currently track on a paper training roster and a guess at cylinder age.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the CETP task-certification tracker with the dispatch-eligibility gate, the per-cylinder requalification tracker, and the reconnection leak-test log; land the first 5-8 dealers through National Propane Gas Association (NPGA) state chapter outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Propane Gas Association (NPGA) member directory and state propane association meetings, where small dealer owners already navigate task-specific CETP training documentation and cylinder requalification tracking across a constantly turning exchange fleet.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $20/mo per driver/technician tracked. Average client (6 drivers) = $199 + $120 = $319/mo. 33-40 dealers × $319/mo = **$10,527-12,760/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every dispatch requires a fresh task-certification check, every cylinder in the exchange fleet carries its own independent requalification clock, and every reconnection opens its own leak-test event — a continuous, high-frequency compliance stream tied directly to delivery and exchange volume.
**Biggest risk:** A driver dispatched to fill or reconnect a system without documented CETP training in that specific task, or an out-of-date cylinder filled and returned to the exchange fleet, is exactly the finding a state fire marshal or PHMSA audit is built to catch — the task-certification gate and the cylinder requalification tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → CETP tracker, cylinder requalification tracker, and leak-test log deployed, first 5-8 dealer retainers through NPGA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 dealers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 33-40 dealers, pushing past $10.53-12.76K/mo.

Idea #2 (fire alarm & sprinkler contractors) has the most operationally continuous compliance mechanism of the five — NFPA 25's weekly-to-annual component intervals never stop recurring across a growing building portfolio — but per-contractor economics depend more on technician headcount than transaction or case volume, making NFSA's outreach community more important than usual to reach density quickly.
