### 1. Landscape Architecture Firm State Board Licensure, CLARB LARE & Landscape-Architect-of-Record Compliance

**Problem:** Landscape architecture is a licensed profession in 43 states plus DC, governed by a state landscape-architect licensing board that is legally and administratively separate from the state architecture board or the state professional-engineering board. Individual licensees must pass the Landscape Architect Registration Examination (LARE), a multi-section exam administered by the Council of Landscape Architectural Registration Boards (CLARB), and maintain a CLARB Council Record to support license-by-comparable-standards in additional states as the firm's project footprint grows across state lines. Every license renews on its own state-specific cycle with a defined continuing-education requirement — commonly a set number of professional development hours through American Society of Landscape Architects (ASLA)-approved or CLARB-approved coursework — and a licensee who lapses mid-project puts every stamped site-design, grading, and planting-plan sheet under that person's seal at risk of being deemed issued by an unlicensed practitioner, which several state boards treat as an independently citable violation separate from any underlying design defect. States with corporate-practice-of-landscape-architecture statutes additionally require the firm itself (not just its individual licensees) to hold a certificate of authorization, and require a specific "landscape architect of record" to be designated and kept current for each active project — a designation that has to be reassigned if that individual leaves the firm or lets their license lapse mid-project. A firm with landscape architects licensed across five or six states, bidding public park, streetscape, and campus work that increasingly requires proof of active licensure and CE currency in the RFP response itself, tracks per-person license expiration, CE-hour accumulation, CLARB Council Record status, and per-project landscape-architect-of-record assignments on a principal's spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming every currently stamped project still has a currently licensed architect of record before the next state board renewal deadline or RFP submission.

**What to build:** A per-licensee, per-state license-expiration and CE-hour tracker with a renewal-deadline alert calendar; a CLARB Council Record and multi-state comparable-standards application status tracker; a per-project landscape-architect-of-record assignment log that flags any active project whose designated architect of record has an expiring or lapsed license; and an RFP-ready licensure/CE compliance export the firm can attach to a public-bid submission on demand.

**Skill fit:** State landscape-architect board rosters, CLARB exam-pass records, and CE-approval catalogs are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-licensee expiration triggers, and the architect-of-record assignment gate is the same deterministic "block the risky state before it happens" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a licensed-design-profession niche this repo has never addressed and that shares no licensing board, exam body, or scope of stamped work with the architecture-firm or PE/surveyor-firm ideas already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-licensee expiration/CE tracker, the CLARB Council Record tracker, and the per-project architect-of-record gate. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small landscape architecture firms.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Society of Landscape Architects (ASLA) state chapter directories and CLARB's own licensee lookup reach exactly the small multi-state firms currently tracking per-person license and CE currency across a growing footprint on a principal's spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-399/mo per firm depending on licensee count and number of licensed states. 32-42 firms × $290/mo avg = $9,280-12,180/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every individual licensee's license and CE cycle renews on its own independent clock for as long as they're employed, every new state the firm bids work in adds another board's renewal and CE-approval regime to track, and every new active project reopens the architect-of-record assignment question — a continuous, headcount-and-footprint-driven compliance stream tied directly to the firm's growth.

**Biggest risk:** A single-state, single-licensee firm has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need a spreadsheet still handles fine. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward firms with licensees across three or more states and active public-sector bid work, where board-by-board renewal and RFP-readiness genuinely outgrows a principal's spreadsheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 14 firms ($4.1K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 24 firms ($7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 32-42 firms plus a board-facing licensure-compliance export firms can attach directly to a public agency's RFP response.

### 2. Small Natural Gas Distribution Pipeline Operator PHMSA Operator Qualification, DIMP & Public Awareness Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Small natural gas distribution operators — municipally owned gas utilities, rural gas districts, and privately owned distribution systems serving a few thousand meters or fewer — operate under PHMSA's federal pipeline safety code at 49 CFR Part 192, enforced in most states through that state's PHMSA-certified intrastate pipeline safety program. Every operator must maintain a written Distribution Integrity Management Program (DIMP) under 49 CFR §§192.1001-1015, identifying the system's specific leak-history threats, running leak-survey and leak-detection at defined intervals tied to pipe material and system size, and evaluating and prioritizing risk on a documented cycle — a DIMP that isn't actually followed on schedule is exactly the finding a state pipeline-safety inspector's periodic audit is built to catch. Every individual who performs a "covered task" — operating a valve, making a tie-in, responding to an odor call — must be qualified under the Operator Qualification rule at 49 CFR §192.805 through a documented evaluation method, and must be periodically re-evaluated on an interval the operator itself sets and must then actually follow; a field technician dispatched on a covered task with a lapsed OQ qualification is a direct citation risk independent of whether anything goes wrong on the job. Operators must also run a public-awareness program consistent with API RP 1162 as incorporated by 49 CFR §192.616, delivering mailings to residents, excavators, and public officials on a fixed cadence with retained proof of delivery, and must file PHMSA's Annual Report (Form F 7100.1-1) and any incident reports (Form F 7100.2-1) on time. A small operator running a system with a handful of field technicians and a part-time compliance-coordinator role manages per-employee OQ qualification and re-evaluation dates, DIMP leak-survey scheduling, and public-awareness mailing cadence on a superintendent's spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific technician's OQ status is actually current before they're dispatched on a covered task.

**What to build:** A per-employee Operator Qualification tracker with a dispatch-eligibility gate blocking assignment of any covered task to a technician whose OQ evaluation has lapsed; a DIMP leak-survey and threat-reassessment scheduler tied to the system's documented intervals; and a public-awareness mailing and PHMSA annual/incident-report filing tracker with a proof-of-delivery archive.

**Skill fit:** PHMSA's OQ, DIMP, and public-awareness rules are public, structured federal regulatory data with concrete per-employee and per-mailing-cycle triggers, and the dispatch-eligibility gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a gas-distribution-operator niche entirely distinct from the tank-storage SPCC, UST, and propane-dealer ideas already in this repo, none of which involve a buried pressurized distribution main, a PHMSA-defined covered task, or an Operator Qualification program.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the OQ dispatch-eligibility gate, the DIMP leak-survey scheduler, and the public-awareness/annual-report tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small municipal and private gas distribution operators.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Public Gas Association (APGA), whose membership is built almost entirely of small municipally owned gas systems, reaches exactly the operators currently tracking per-technician OQ status and DIMP survey intervals on a superintendent's spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $399-799/mo per operator depending on system size and field-technician count. 16-22 operators × $550/mo avg = $8,800-12,100/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every field technician's OQ qualification re-evaluates on its own independent clock for as long as they're on staff, every DIMP leak-survey cycle recurs on its own interval for as long as the system operates, and every mile of main the operator adds both expands the survey workload and adds new covered-task assignments to track — a continuous, system-growth-driven compliance stream tied directly to the operator's footprint.

**Biggest risk:** A field technician dispatched on a covered task with a lapsed OQ qualification, followed by an incident, is a severe safety and regulatory event for a small operator. Mitigate by making the OQ dispatch-eligibility gate a hard block rather than an advisory flag, and by qualifying leads toward operators with enough field staff and system miles that the compliance calendar genuinely outgrows a superintendent's spreadsheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 8 operators ($4.4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 13 operators ($7.2K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 16-22 operators plus a PHMSA-audit-ready compliance export operators can hand a state pipeline-safety inspector during a routine review.

### 3. Contract Ethylene Oxide Medical Device Sterilization Facility EPA NESHAP Subpart O, FDA Registration & OSHA EtO Exposure Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Contract sterilization facilities that use ethylene oxide (EtO) gas to sterilize medical devices on behalf of device manufacturers operate under EPA's National Emission Standards for Ethylene Oxide Commercial Sterilization Facilities at 40 CFR Part 63 Subpart O — significantly tightened by EPA's 2024 final rule, which lowered allowable facility-wide EtO emission limits and added enhanced fenceline monitoring, process-vent controls, and more frequent performance testing. A facility that misses a required performance test or lets a continuous emissions monitoring system go uncalibrated past its due date is exactly the finding an EPA or delegated-state-agency Subpart O inspection is built to catch, with consequences that can include a Title V permit deviation report and civil penalty exposure. Because EtO is classified as a carcinogen under OSHA's dedicated exposure standard at 29 CFR §1910.1047, every facility must run a documented industrial-hygiene monitoring program tracking employee exposure against the permissible exposure limit and action level, and must enroll and maintain any employee who crosses the action level in a mandatory medical surveillance program on a fixed periodic examination schedule — a lapsed medical-surveillance exam for an exposed employee is an independently citable OSHA violation regardless of the facility's air-permit status. A facility sterilizing devices on behalf of multiple manufacturer clients must also separately maintain its own FDA facility registration and device listing as a contract sterilizer under 21 CFR Part 807, and must retain sterilization-cycle validation and biological-indicator records tied to each specific client lot to support that manufacturer's own FDA Quality System Regulation documentation. A mid-size contract sterilizer running multiple EtO chambers across shifts manages Subpart O monitoring/testing deadlines, OSHA exposure monitoring and medical-surveillance scheduling, and per-lot sterilization-validation recordkeeping on an EHS manager's spreadsheet and a filing cabinet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific chamber's next required performance test or a specific exposed employee's next medical-surveillance exam is actually on the calendar before the deadline passes.

**What to build:** An EPA Subpart O performance-testing, CEMS-calibration, and Title V deviation-reporting tracker; an OSHA EtO exposure-monitoring and medical-surveillance scheduler flagging any exposed employee approaching a due exam; and a per-lot sterilization-cycle validation and biological-indicator recordkeeping tracker tied to each client manufacturer's lot number for audit-ready retrieval.

**Skill fit:** Subpart O's testing/monitoring intervals and OSHA's EtO medical-surveillance schedule are public, structured federal regulatory data with concrete per-chamber and per-employee triggers, and the deadline-and-recordkeeping pattern is the same deterministic system this series builds repeatedly, applied to a contract-sterilization niche entirely distinct from the device-manufacturer FDA QSR idea, the sterile-compounding-pharmacy ideas, and the solvent-based dry-cleaning NESHAP ideas already in this repo, none of which involve EtO, Subpart O, or a contract sterilizer's per-lot validation obligation to a separate manufacturer client.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the Subpart O testing/monitoring tracker, the OSHA medical-surveillance scheduler, and the per-lot validation recordkeeping tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent contract sterilization facilities.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the AAMI (Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation) sterilization standards community, whose working groups draw directly from contract-sterilizer EHS and quality staff, plus the publicly posted EPA list of Subpart O major and area sources, reach exactly the facilities currently tracking performance-test and medical-surveillance deadlines on an EHS manager's spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $499-999/mo per facility depending on chamber count and client-lot volume. 15-20 facilities × $700/mo avg = $10,500-14,000/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every chamber's Subpart O performance-testing and CEMS-calibration cycle recurs on its own fixed interval for as long as the facility operates, every exposed employee's OSHA medical-surveillance exam renews on its own periodic clock, and every new client manufacturer's lot generates its own validation-record retention obligation — a continuous, throughput-and-headcount-driven compliance stream tied directly to the facility's production volume.

**Biggest risk:** The population of EtO contract sterilizers nationally is small (a few hundred facilities), capping the addressable market. Mitigate by pricing at the higher end given the Title V/OSHA enforcement stakes and by expanding into device manufacturers who operate their own captive EtO chambers and face the identical Subpart O and OSHA obligations.

**Growth path:** Side project → 8 facilities ($5.6K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 13 facilities ($9.1K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 15-20 facilities plus an EPA/OSHA-audit-ready compliance export facilities can hand an inspector during a routine Subpart O or exposure-monitoring review.

### 4. Grade A Bulk Milk Hauler/Sampler Company PMO Driver Certification, Wash-Ticket & Sample Chain-of-Custody Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Companies that operate bulk milk tank trucks picking up raw Grade A milk from dairy farms and delivering it to processing plants for pasteurization work under the FDA-endorsed Pasteurized Milk Ordinance (PMO) as administered through the National Conference on Interstate Milk Shipments (NCIMS). Every individual driver must hold a state-issued Bulk Milk Hauler/Sampler permit earned by completing an approved sampling-and-hauling training course and passing a state exam, with the permit subject to periodic recertification — a driver dispatched on a pickup route with a lapsed hauler/sampler permit is exactly the finding a state milk-sanitation rating officer's routine audit is built to catch, and a pattern of it can jeopardize the receiving plant's own Grade A rating and its listing on the Interstate Milk Shippers (IMS) list that lets its product move across state lines. Each tank truck must be interior-washed and sanitized at an approved wash station before its next load, with a wash ticket retained as the documentary proof a plant's receiving dock and a state inspector both expect to see on file; a truck that shows up without a current wash ticket can be turned away at the plant, stranding that day's route. Every farm pickup requires an official raw-milk sample drawn under a documented chain-of-custody and a temperature-at-pickup log confirming the milk was cooled to 45°F or below within the PMO's required window — a missing sample or an undocumented temperature reading breaks the chain of custody a processing plant relies on to trace a quality or antibiotic-residue problem back to the specific farm and load it came from. A hauling company running several trucks and drivers across a multi-farm pickup route manages per-driver permit/recertification status, per-truck wash-ticket currency, and per-load sample-and-temperature documentation on a dispatcher's clipboard and a filing cabinet, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific driver, truck, and load are all simultaneously in compliance before that morning's route pulls out.

**What to build:** A per-driver Bulk Milk Hauler/Sampler permit and recertification tracker with a dispatch-eligibility gate; a per-truck wash-ticket currency tracker flagging any vehicle due for its next wash-station visit; and a per-load sample chain-of-custody and pickup-temperature log tied to the specific farm, driver, and delivery-plant combination for that route.

**Skill fit:** PMO hauler/sampler training and recertification requirements, and state Grade A sanitation-rating rosters, are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-driver and per-load triggers, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the chain-of-custody log are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a milk-hauling niche entirely distinct from the Grade A processing-plant compliance idea and the general livestock-transporter idea already in this repo, neither of which certifies an individual driver as a sampler or documents a per-load milk chain-of-custody.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the driver dispatch-eligibility gate, the wash-ticket tracker, and the per-load sample/temperature chain-of-custody log. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent milk-hauling companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state department of agriculture Grade A hauler/sampler permit rosters (public directories used to qualify leads directly) and the International Dairy Foods Association's (IDFA) member-plant network, whose receiving docks depend on properly certified haulers, reach exactly the small hauling companies currently reconciling driver permits and wash tickets on a dispatcher's clipboard with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-499/mo per hauling company depending on truck/driver count and route volume. 26-34 companies × $350/mo avg = $9,100-11,900/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every load hauled generates its own sample-and-temperature documentation event regardless of how routine the run, every driver's hauler/sampler permit recertifies on its own independent clock, and every new truck or driver the company adds multiplies the number of simultaneously tracked permit-and-wash-ticket records — a continuous, route-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the hauler's fleet growth.

**Biggest risk:** A broken chain of custody that keeps a plant from tracing a contaminated load back to its source farm is a severe liability event for both the hauler and the receiving plant. Mitigate by making the per-load sample/temperature log a mandatory step rather than an optional add-on, and by qualifying leads toward hauling companies running multiple trucks across a multi-farm, multi-plant route, where the staggered permit-and-wash-ticket calendar genuinely outgrows a dispatcher's clipboard.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 companies ($4.2K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 20 companies ($7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 26-34 companies plus a plant-facing chain-of-custody export haulers can hand a receiving dock or state sanitation-rating officer on demand.

### 5. Reinsurance Intermediary Broker/Manager Multi-State Licensing & Intermediary Contract Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Firms that place or manage reinsurance transactions between primary (ceding) insurers and reinsurers hold a distinct license under a state's version of the NAIC Reinsurance Intermediary Model Act — a "reinsurance intermediary-broker" license for firms that place reinsurance on a ceding insurer's behalf, or a "reinsurance intermediary-manager" license for firms granted binding/underwriting authority on a reinsurer's behalf — separate from any standard property-casualty or life producer license, surplus lines license, or MGA appointment. Because a reinsurance intermediary routinely places business with cedents and reinsurers domiciled in other states, the firm and its individual producers typically need resident-plus-multi-state-nonresident reinsurance-intermediary licensure, each renewing on its own state-specific cycle, and a firm placing a treaty on a lapsed nonresident license in the reinsurer's home state is exactly the finding a state insurance department's market-conduct exam is built to catch. The Model Act also requires a written contract between the intermediary and every insurer or reinsurer it represents, specifying the scope of the intermediary's authority and — for intermediary-managers with binding authority — the fund-remittance and accounting-settlement timeline the intermediary must meet, with that contract required to be available for regulator review on request; a contract that's expired, unsigned, or missing required NAIC Model Act provisions is a direct examination finding independent of whether any transaction under it went wrong. Intermediary-managers with binding authority commonly face an additional biennial or annual requirement to submit financial statements or an independent audit report to each represented insurer's domiciliary regulator, on a deadline that runs separately from any state license-renewal date. A reinsurance intermediary firm placing treaty and facultative business across a dozen or more ceding companies and reinsurers manages multi-state license renewal, per-relationship intermediary-contract currency, and financial-reporting deadlines on a compliance officer's spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check confirming every active insurer/reinsurer relationship still has a current, Model-Act-compliant contract on file before the next market-conduct exam or contract-renewal date.

**What to build:** A multi-state reinsurance-intermediary license renewal tracker covering resident and nonresident licenses for both the firm and its individual producers; a per-insurer/reinsurer intermediary-contract currency tracker flagging any relationship whose written contract is expired or missing required Model Act provisions; and a financial-statement/audit-report filing tracker tied to each represented insurer's domiciliary-regulator deadline.

**Skill fit:** State reinsurance-intermediary license rosters and each state's adopted Model Act provisions are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-relationship and per-license triggers, and the contract-currency tracker is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a reinsurance-specific licensing niche entirely distinct from the retail-producer, surplus-lines-broker, MGA, and captive-manager ideas already in this repo, none of which hold a reinsurance intermediary-broker or intermediary-manager license or file a Model-Act intermediary contract.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the multi-state license tracker, the per-relationship contract-currency tracker, and the financial-reporting deadline tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent reinsurance intermediary firms.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Reinsurance Association of America (RAA) member directory and its annual conference reach exactly the small independent intermediary firms currently reconciling multi-state licenses and per-relationship contracts on a compliance officer's spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $399-799/mo per firm depending on licensed-state count and number of represented insurer/reinsurer relationships. 18-24 firms × $550/mo avg = $9,900-13,200/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every state license renews on its own independent cycle for as long as the firm places business there, every new ceding insurer or reinsurer relationship adds another Model-Act contract to track, and every intermediary-manager relationship with binding authority adds its own recurring financial-reporting deadline — a continuous, relationship-count-driven compliance stream tied directly to the firm's book of business.

**Biggest risk:** A firm placing business in only two or three states with a handful of long-standing relationships has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward firms licensed across many states with a large and growing count of represented insurers and reinsurers, where the license-and-contract calendar genuinely outgrows a compliance officer's spreadsheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 firms ($5.5K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 16 firms ($8.8K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 18-24 firms plus a regulator-facing contract-compliance export firms can produce during a state market-conduct exam.

## This Session's Pick: #3 (Contract EtO Sterilization Facility) for the highest-stakes, most currently-tightening regulatory driver; #4 (Grade A Bulk Milk Hauler/Sampler) for the most naturally recurring per-load compliance event

Contract EtO sterilization facility compliance stands out because EPA's 2024 Subpart O rule actively tightened emission limits and added monitoring burden industry-wide in the last two years, meaning every facility in the addressable market is dealing with a compliance calendar that just got harder rather than staying static — a rare case in this series where the regulatory driver is getting more demanding, not just staying constant, which strengthens both the urgency of the pitch and the willingness to pay a premium price. AAMI's concentrated sterilization-standards community gives direct, credible access to exactly the EHS and quality staff who own this problem inside a contract sterilizer.

Grade A bulk milk hauler/sampler compliance has the most naturally recurring compliance event in this session: every single farm pickup generates its own sample-and-temperature chain-of-custody record regardless of how routine the run, which means the product's usage frequency scales directly with the hauler's daily route volume rather than a slower filing or renewal cadence — and state department of agriculture hauler-permit rosters make the first cohort of leads unusually easy to qualify and reach directly by name.
