### 1. Independent Welding Inspection Company AWS CWI Certification & Per-Project Inspection Report Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Independent welding inspection companies that sell third-party inspection-for-hire to structural-steel fabricators, pipeline contractors, and general contractors depend entirely on each individual inspector's American Welding Society Certified Welding Inspector (CWI) credential — earned through a rigorous exam and renewed on a 9-year cycle either through a full re-exam or a documented endorsement-letter recertification path requiring evidence of continuous relevant work — and an inspector who signs and stamps a weld-acceptance report after their CWI has lapsed has issued a document the client's own quality-assurance program, and in many cases the local building department, won't accept, exactly the gap a client's own audit of the inspection company's credential file is built to catch. Every inspection also has to be logged against the specific code the client's contract calls for — AWS D1.1 for structural steel, API 1104 for pipeline welds, ASME Section IX for pressure-boundary welds — since a report written against the wrong code's acceptance criteria is worthless to a client relying on it to close out a project's own code-compliance documentation, and a busy inspection company running several inspectors across concurrent job sites has to know at a glance which inspector is qualified for which code and which project. An inspection company also has to track its own equipment calibration — ultrasonic thickness gauges, magnetic-particle yokes — on its own periodic recertification schedule, since a report generated from an out-of-calibration instrument is a defect a client's own receiving audit can trace straight back to the inspection company. A company running several CWIs across a rotating multi-site project load currently tracks per-inspector CWI renewal dates, per-project code assignments, and equipment calibration on an office manager's spreadsheet and a filing cabinet of paper certification cards, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific inspector's CWI, code qualification, and equipment calibration are all simultaneously current before that day's report goes out under the company's letterhead.

**What to build:** A per-inspector CWI renewal tracker with a recertification-path reminder (exam vs. endorsement-letter) and a "cleared to inspect" gate tied to each project's required code; a per-project code-assignment log matching inspector qualifications against contract requirements; and an equipment calibration tracker for UT gauges, MT yokes, and other field instruments with a due-date alert before the next inspection.

**Skill fit:** AWS's CWI renewal cycle and code-qualification structure are public, structured certifying-body data with concrete per-inspector triggers, and the "cleared to inspect" gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a third-party inspection-services niche entirely distinct from the Structural Steel Fabricator's own facility-level AISC/AWS D1.1 compliance already in this repo, which governs a fabricator's own welding rather than an independent inspector's credential.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the CWI renewal/code-qualification gate, the per-project assignment log, and the equipment calibration tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small independent welding inspection companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Welding Society's own local section meetings and its CWI-holder directory reach exactly the small inspection companies currently reconciling inspector credentials and equipment calibration on an office spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per company depending on inspector headcount and project volume. 22-28 companies × $429/mo avg = $9,438-12,012/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every inspector's CWI renews on its own independent 9-year clock requiring active tracking well before the deadline, every new project reopens the code-qualification-matching question, and every piece of field equipment recurs on its own calibration cycle for as long as the company operates — a continuous, headcount-and-project-driven compliance stream tied directly to the company's bidding activity.

**Biggest risk:** A one- or two-inspector shop working a single steady client has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward companies running several inspectors across multiple codes and concurrent job sites, where the credential-and-calibration calendar genuinely outgrows a spreadsheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 companies ($5.1K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 20 companies ($8.6K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 22-28 companies plus a client-facing credential-and-calibration export companies can attach to a bid package on demand.

### 2. Frozen Dessert & Ice Cream Manufacturing Plant State License, FDA Standard-of-Identity & Batch Overrun Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Small-batch ice cream and frozen dessert manufacturers selling to grocery, foodservice, and scoop-shop accounts hold their own state "frozen dessert" or "manufactured milk products" plant license — a distinct license category from a fluid Grade A milk permit in the large majority of states — and must formulate and document every batch against FDA's standard of identity for ice cream and related products under 21 CFR Part 135, which sets a minimum milkfat percentage, a minimum weight per gallon, and a maximum permitted overrun (the air incorporated during freezing); a batch that overruns past the standard-of-identity air limit to stretch yield, or that falls under the minimum milkfat threshold on a recipe substitution, is a mislabeling violation exactly a state dairy inspector's routine plant audit is built to catch, since the product's own label declares it "ice cream" against a legal definition the batch may no longer meet. Every batch of mix must also pass through its own documented pasteurization or HTST-equivalent heat-treatment cycle before freezing, logged with time-and-temperature charts separate from any fluid-milk pasteurization record, and a batch mix run without a complete heat-treatment log on file is exactly the gap a state audit or a customer's own supplier-verification request targets. A plant running several concurrent flavor batches through a single pasteurizer and freezer line manages state license renewal, per-batch milkfat/overrun calculations, and pasteurization-log documentation on a production manager's clipboard and a recipe-card box, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific batch's overrun and milkfat actually meet the standard of identity before that flavor ships under its label claim.

**What to build:** A state frozen-dessert plant license renewal tracker; a per-batch overrun-and-milkfat calculator flagging any batch outside FDA's 21 CFR Part 135 standard-of-identity range before it's approved to ship; and a pasteurization/heat-treatment log tied to each batch with a completeness gate blocking any batch from being logged as sellable until its full temperature record is on file.

**Skill fit:** FDA's 21 CFR Part 135 standard-of-identity thresholds and state frozen-dessert licensing rules are public, numerically precise regulatory data with a concrete per-batch calculation, and the overrun/milkfat calculator is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a frozen-manufacturing niche entirely distinct from the fluid Grade A milk plant and raw-milk dairy ideas already in this repo, neither of which manufactures a frozen, aerated, formulated product at all.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license renewal tracker, the per-batch overrun/milkfat calculator, and the pasteurization-log completeness gate. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent ice cream and frozen dessert manufacturers.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the International Dairy Foods Association's Ice Cream segment and the National Ice Cream Retailers Association's manufacturer-member directory reach exactly the small plants currently reconciling batch overrun and pasteurization logs on a clipboard with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-499/mo per plant depending on flavor-batch volume and account count. 24-30 plants × $359/mo avg = $8,616-10,770/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every batch produced generates its own overrun-and-milkfat calculation regardless of how routine the flavor, every pasteurization run opens its own heat-treatment-log event, and every new grocery or foodservice account the plant signs multiplies label-claim scrutiny on its existing SKUs — a continuous, batch-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the plant's production schedule.

**Biggest risk:** A very small plant running a single flavor for one farmers-market account has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward plants running multiple flavors across grocery and foodservice distribution, where the batch-and-label calendar genuinely outgrows a recipe-card box.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 plants ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 20 plants ($7.2K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 24-30 plants plus a state-audit-ready compliance export plants can hand a dairy inspector during a routine plant review.

### 3. Independent Biomedical Equipment Servicing Company CBET Certification, FDA Servicer/Remanufacturer Classification & Per-Device PM Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Independent biomedical equipment servicing companies — third-party service organizations (ISOs) that hospitals, surgery centers, and clinics contract with instead of the equipment manufacturer's own field-service arm — must correctly classify every service job against the FDA's 2018 servicing-vs-remanufacturing framework, which draws a hard line between routine servicing (returning a device to its original specifications, no FDA registration required) and "remanufacturing" (a change significant enough to alter the device's performance or intended use, which triggers full FDA facility registration and Quality System Regulation obligations); an ISO that performs what actually qualifies as remanufacturing while operating as an unregistered servicer is exactly the finding an FDA inspection or a hospital's own vendor-credentialing audit is built to catch. Every technician typically holds AAMI's Certified Biomedical Equipment Technician (CBET) credential, renewed on its own periodic continuing-education cycle distinct from any state license, and a technician dispatched to service a hospital's infusion pumps or patient monitors on a lapsed CBET is a credential gap the hospital's own vendor-qualification file will flag at the next accreditation survey. Every serviced device must also carry a documented preventive-maintenance record tied to the hospital client's Joint Commission equipment-management standard (EC.02.04.03) or an Alternative Equipment Maintenance program under ANSI/AAMI EQ56 — a PM interval the servicing company, not the hospital, is contractually responsible for tracking and completing on schedule, and a device that missed its scheduled PM with no documentation on file is exactly the gap a Joint Commission survey targets during a hospital's own accreditation review. A servicing company running several CBET technicians across multiple hospital contracts manages per-technician CBET currency, per-job servicing-vs-remanufacturing classification, and per-device PM-schedule completion on a service-ticket spreadsheet and a filing cabinet of manufacturer service manuals, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific technician, a specific job's FDA classification, and a specific device's PM schedule are all simultaneously current before that service ticket closes.

**What to build:** A per-technician CBET certification and continuing-education currency tracker with a "cleared to service" gate; a per-job servicing-vs-remanufacturing classification checklist flagging any job description that crosses into FDA remanufacturing territory before work begins; and a per-device PM-schedule tracker tied to each hospital client's equipment inventory with a due-date alert ahead of the next Joint Commission survey window.

**Skill fit:** AAMI's CBET renewal requirements and the FDA's published servicing/remanufacturing classification criteria are public, structured certifying-body and regulatory data with concrete per-technician and per-device triggers, and the PM-schedule tracker is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a third-party medical-equipment-servicing niche entirely distinct from the DMEPOS/DME supplier and medical waste hauler ideas already in this repo, none of which repairs or maintains a hospital's own existing capital equipment.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the CBET currency gate, the servicing/remanufacturing classification checklist, and the per-device PM-schedule tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent biomedical equipment servicing companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the International Association of Medical Equipment Remarketers and Servicers (IAMERS) member directory and AAMI's Technology Management Council reach exactly the small ISOs currently reconciling CBET currency and PM schedules on a service-ticket spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $399-799/mo per company depending on technician count and hospital-contract portfolio size. 18-24 companies × $549/mo avg = $9,882-13,176/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every technician's CBET renews on its own independent cycle for as long as they're staffed, every service job reopens the FDA classification question regardless of how routine the repair, and every device in a hospital client's inventory recurs on its own independent PM clock for as long as the contract runs — a continuous, technician-and-device-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the company's contract portfolio.

**Biggest risk:** A servicer that misclassifies a job as routine service when it actually crosses into remanufacturing exposes both itself and its hospital client to a serious FDA compliance finding. Mitigate by making the classification checklist a hard decision point rather than an advisory flag, and by qualifying leads toward companies running multiple hospital contracts where the PM-and-credential load genuinely outgrows a spreadsheet.

### 4. Used Cooking Oil Collection & Biodiesel-Feedstock Recycling Route Operator EPA Used-Oil & RIN Chain-of-Custody Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Independent route operators collecting spent cooking oil from restaurant grease traps and fryer-oil totes operate under EPA's used oil management standards at 40 CFR Part 279, which require proper storage, container labeling, and shipment documentation for every load moved as used oil being recycled rather than disposed of — a load picked up without the required shipping paper is exactly the finding a state environmental agency's hauler-permit audit is built to catch. Because used cooking oil has become a major feedstock for biodiesel and renewable diesel production, a collector selling to a biodiesel producer must maintain a documented pickup-to-sale chain of custody — which restaurant account, which load, which delivery date, matched to the buyer's receiving record — sufficient to support that producer's EPA Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) Renewable Identification Number (RIN) generation and, in California and similar states, Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) pathway certification; a collector whose documentation can't support the buyer's RIN claim gets paid the lower commodity-grease rate instead of the premium a verifiable renewable-feedstock chain of custody commands, a real and recurring revenue loss on every undocumented load. A route operator also has to track each restaurant account's typical accumulation rate against its own pickup schedule, since a missed pickup on a high-volume account is both a lost load and, for the restaurant, a grease-trap overflow risk that reflects on the collector's own service reliability. An operator running a multi-stop weekly or biweekly route across dozens of restaurant accounts currently tracks pickup volumes and chain-of-custody documentation on a paper route sheet and a driver's memory, and a load delivered to the biodiesel buyer without a complete pickup-to-sale record, discovered only when the buyer's own RIN-generation paperwork comes up short, is lost premium revenue on grease the collector already paid the fuel to haul.

**What to build:** A per-account pickup-schedule tracker computing each restaurant's next due pickup from its historical accumulation rate; a per-load chain-of-custody log capturing pickup account, date, and volume through to the buyer's delivery receipt, with a completeness flag before a load is submitted for RIN-supporting documentation; and an EPA 40 CFR 279 shipping-paper and container-labeling compliance checklist for every load moved.

**Skill fit:** EPA's used-oil management standards and RFS RIN-generation documentation requirements are public, structured federal regulatory data with a concrete per-load traceability requirement, and the chain-of-custody log is the same deterministic "record it, prove it, get paid for it" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a UCO-recycling niche entirely distinct from the rendering/deadstock collection idea already in this repo, which handles animal byproducts destined for the feed stream rather than spent cooking oil destined for fuel.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the pickup-schedule tracker, the per-load chain-of-custody log, and the shipping-paper compliance checklist. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent UCO collection route operators.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — Clean Fuels Alliance America's feedstock-supplier network and regional recycling-and-waste-hauler trade associations reach exactly the small route operators currently reconciling pickup schedules and chain-of-custody documentation on a paper route sheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-499/mo per operator depending on account count and route volume. 24-30 operators × $359/mo avg = $8,616-10,770/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every pickup generates its own chain-of-custody event regardless of how routine the account, every new restaurant account signed reopens the pickup-schedule question, and every load sold to a biodiesel buyer carries its own documentation-completeness stakes tied directly to the premium price the collector actually gets paid — a continuous, account-and-route-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the operator's growth.

**Biggest risk:** A small operator running a handful of low-volume accounts and selling into the undifferentiated commodity-grease market has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward operators running a growing multi-account route who sell directly to biodiesel producers and want the documented chain of custody the premium price depends on.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 operators ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 20 operators ($7.2K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 24-30 operators plus a buyer-facing chain-of-custody export operators can produce to support their biodiesel buyer's RIN-generation paperwork.

### 5. Residential/Commercial Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) Installer NFPA 855, AHJ Plan-Review & UL 9540A Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Independent electrical contractors installing residential and small-commercial battery energy storage systems operate under NFPA 855 (Standard for the Installation of Stationary Energy Storage Systems), which sets spacing, fire-rated separation, and thermal-runaway venting requirements a jurisdiction's fire marshal or building department reviews before a permit is issued — a system installed without documentation proving it meets NFPA 855's separation and ventilation requirements for its specific battery chemistry and energy capacity is exactly the finding an AHJ's plan-review rejection or a post-installation fire-department inspection is built to catch, and a rejected plan-review submission stalls a project the homeowner or business client was told would be operating by a specific date. Larger commercial installations must additionally pass a review of UL 9540A large-scale fire testing documentation for the specific battery model being installed — proof the manufacturer's own unit has been tested for thermal-runaway propagation under the conditions the installation's fire code demands — and an installer who submits a permit application without the correct UL 9540A test report on file for that exact model and configuration gets the application bounced back, burning weeks of schedule on a project. Every jurisdiction's AHJ also applies its own version of NFPA 855's requirements with local amendments, meaning an installer working across several neighboring cities or counties has to track which specific plan-review checklist and documentation package each jurisdiction actually requires rather than assuming one submission format works everywhere. An installer running several concurrent residential and commercial storage projects across multiple jurisdictions manages per-project NFPA 855 documentation, UL 9540A test-report matching, and jurisdiction-specific plan-review checklists on a project manager's spreadsheet and a folder of PDF spec sheets, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific project's fire-code documentation package is actually complete for its specific jurisdiction before that permit application goes in.

**What to build:** A per-project NFPA 855 documentation tracker computing the required separation/ventilation package from the battery chemistry and capacity being installed; a UL 9540A test-report matching tool tied to each specific battery model and configuration to confirm the correct test documentation is on file before permit submission; and a jurisdiction-specific plan-review checklist library covering the local AHJ amendments an installer's active service area actually requires.

**Skill fit:** NFPA 855's fire-code requirements and UL 9540A's test-report structure are public, structured code-body data with a concrete per-project documentation requirement, and the documentation-completeness gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a stationary-battery-storage niche entirely distinct from the solar PV installer's NABCEP/utility-PTO compliance already in this repo, which governs an entirely different code family with no fire-marshal plan-review or thermal-runaway testing component at all.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the NFPA 855 documentation tracker, the UL 9540A test-report matching tool, and the jurisdiction-specific plan-review checklist library. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent battery storage installation contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — regional electrical-contractor associations affiliated with the National Electrical Contractors Association (NECA) and the exhibitor/attendee lists from energy-storage industry trade shows reach exactly the small installers currently reconciling fire-code documentation and jurisdiction-specific plan-review requirements on a spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

## This Session's Pick: #3 (Independent Biomedical Equipment Servicing Company) for the sharpest regulatory-classification stakes; #5 (Residential/Commercial BESS Installer) for the most timely, fastest-growing buyer segment
