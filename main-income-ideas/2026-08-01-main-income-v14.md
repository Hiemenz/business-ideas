### 1. Mineral Rights Brokering
**Problem:** Landowners who own oil, gas, coal, or other subsurface mineral rights — often inherited, often in rural states — are routinely approached by energy companies and mineral rights acquisition funds with lowball offers. Most mineral rights owners don't know what their minerals are worth, don't know which buyers pay fair prices, and don't know how to run a competitive sales process. Simultaneously, smaller mineral rights acquisition companies and individual investors are actively seeking deals but struggle to find motivated sellers at scale, since mineral ownership records are buried in county courthouse deed records that are difficult to search systematically. A mineral rights broker bridges this gap: finding motivated sellers through proactive outreach, running a competitive bid process among vetted buyers, and earning a commission on the transaction — the same role a real estate broker plays for surface property, applied to the subsurface.
**What to build:** A mineral rights brokerage practice — a mineral ownership research tool (using state-specific county deed and Division of Interest records to identify landowners with mineral rights in active or productive oil and gas plays, which becomes the prospecting database for seller outreach), a mineral valuation model (comparing the subject minerals against comparable sales in the same county using publicly available deed records and production data), a multi-buyer bid solicitation workflow (submitting the offering package to a network of vetted mineral rights buyers — acquisition funds, E&P companies, and individual mineral investors — and managing the bid process through closing), and a closing coordination system (managing title opinion, deed preparation, and proceeds disbursement at closing).
**Skill fit:** Direct on the research and valuation tooling — identifying mineral owners from deed records, normalizing mineral descriptions (net mineral acres, royalty interest, working interest) across the patchwork of county records, and building a comparable-sales valuation model are data-engineering and research problems the builder handles faster than a traditional landman who does it manually. The buyer network development (outreach to mineral acquisition funds) is relationship work that compounds rapidly once the first successful transaction demonstrates credibility.
**MVP scope:** 3-4 weekends to build a county deed record research tool for one target county (counties within active shale plays — Permian Basin in Texas, Williston Basin in North Dakota, Appalachian Basin in Pennsylvania — have the highest transaction volume and public record availability), a comparable-sales valuation spreadsheet, and a bid package template; initial buyer network built through outreach to mineral acquisition fund contacts findable in LinkedIn and industry association directories.
**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — distribute by identifying prospective sellers proactively (mailing to identified mineral owners in active plays before they receive an unsolicited lowball offer, positioning the service as "before you sell, know what it's worth") and through estate planning and probate attorneys (who encounter inherited mineral rights in every estate they handle and have clients who need exactly this service).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Commission of 4-8% of gross transaction value on the seller's side; mineral rights transactions in active plays typically range $20,000-500,000+, generating $800-40,000 per closed transaction. Closing 3-6 transactions/mo across a mix of sizes once the buyer network and seller pipeline are established = **$10-30K/mo**, with individual large transactions (300+ net mineral acres in an active play) generating single commissions of $50,000-150,000 that punctuate the steady smaller-deal base.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The prospecting database (mineral owners in active plays) is a durable asset — once the county deed research is done for a target play, the owner list is reusable for repeated outreach campaigns as new sellers become motivated over time. Each successful transaction generates word-of-mouth referrals within the seller community (mineral owners know other mineral owners in the same family or region) and within the buyer network (a buyer who gets consistent deal flow from this broker becomes a reliable repeat counterparty who doesn't need to be re-sold on each transaction).
**Biggest risk:** Real estate broker licensing requirements: in some states (Texas is notably strict), brokering mineral rights transactions for compensation requires a real estate license if the minerals are classified as real property — which they are in most states. Research the specific state licensing requirement before operating and either obtain the real estate salesperson license (the lowest-bar credential, typically 60-180 hours of education + exam) or partner with a licensed broker for compliance. The license is an early prerequisite, not a long-term barrier.
**Growth path:** Side project → real estate license obtained (if required in target state), first 3-5 seller contacts through deed research outreach, buyer network established with 4-6 active acquisition fund contacts, first transaction closed. Replaces-part-time-income → 2-3 closings/mo, seller pipeline built through estate attorney referrals and direct mail campaigns, buyer network generating competitive bids on each deal (~$5-12K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 4-7 closings/mo across a mix of play types and transaction sizes, deed research tool covering 5-10 high-volume counties, referral pipeline from estate attorneys and repeat sellers compounding, pushing past $12-30K/mo.

### 2. Franchise Candidate Matchmaking Service
**Problem:** Every year, tens of thousands of people seriously consider buying a franchise — a defined, repeatable business model with brand support — but most have no framework for evaluating which franchise fits their skills, capital, risk tolerance, and geographic market. The franchise industry itself has thousands of concepts across every possible category, each with a Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) that runs hundreds of pages and is nearly impossible for a non-attorney non-franchise-expert to parse meaningfully. Franchise consultants (also called franchise brokers) address this gap: they consult with prospective buyers, identify suitable franchise matches from a portfolio of franchise brands they represent, and guide the buyer through the discovery and decision process — earning a placement fee from the franchisor (not the buyer) when a match results in a signed franchise agreement. The buyer pays nothing; the franchisor pays because the consultant sourced a qualified, well-matched candidate they wouldn't have found otherwise.
**What to build:** A franchise matchmaking practice — a candidate intake and profiling tool (structured questionnaire mapping the candidate's financial capacity, industry experience, preferred work type, lifestyle goals, and geographic target market), a franchise portfolio database (tracking the key metrics for each franchise brand represented: initial investment range, royalty structure, territory availability, typical owner background, FDD Item 19 earnings data), a candidate-to-franchise matching engine (ranking the franchise portfolio against the candidate profile on the key fit criteria), and a franchise discovery process management tool (tracking each candidate through the multi-step franchise discovery process: FDD review, validation calls with existing franchisees, financial qualification, territory check, and signing day).
**Skill fit:** Direct on the profiling, matching, and process-tracking tooling — building a structured data model that maps candidate attributes to franchise fit criteria and tracks each candidate through a multi-step discovery funnel is a data engineering and workflow problem. Joining an established franchise broker network (FranChoice, FranServe, or Fransmart provide networks of franchise brands, training, and back-office support for independent consultants) removes the need to build the franchise portfolio from scratch and provides immediate access to hundreds of franchise opportunities with established placement fee agreements.
**MVP scope:** 2-4 weeks to join a franchise broker network (most have a straightforward application, onboarding training, and a franchise portfolio you can represent immediately); 2-3 weekends to build the candidate intake and matching workflow on top of the network's portfolio; initial candidate outreach begins through existing professional contacts while the tooling is built.
**Time to first $:** 45-60 days — distribute through small business development centers (SBDCs, which counsel aspiring entrepreneurs who are specifically evaluating business ownership options), entrepreneurship programs at community colleges and universities, military veteran transition programs (a large demographic for franchise candidates, since franchises suit a structured operational background), and estate/tax attorneys who have clients who recently received inheritance capital and are exploring business ownership.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** Franchise placement fees average $20,000-30,000 per closed franchise agreement, paid by the franchisor; closing 1-2 franchise agreements/mo once the candidate pipeline is running = **$20-60K/mo**, making this one of the highest per-transaction ceiling ideas in the series — a single productive month exceeds the annual target. The pipeline operates with 60-120 day sales cycles (longer than the 90-day target for first dollar, but first dollar arrives within 60 days through the network's training and fast-track programs), offset by the very high per-close value.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Each candidate who ultimately doesn't buy a franchise still generates referrals — they know other people considering business ownership, and the consultation process builds genuine trust that converts to word-of-mouth introductions. The SBDC and veteran program distribution channels generate a self-renewing annual cohort of new candidates without any ongoing outreach effort once the relationship is established with the program coordinator.
**Biggest risk:** Franchise sales cycles average 90-120 days from first consultation to signed agreement, meaning the cash flow is lumpy and delayed — candidates who seem ready on week 1 routinely take 4-5 months to sign or decide not to proceed. Maintain a pipeline of 15-25 active candidates at different stages at all times so that closings happen regularly even though each individual cycle is long, and don't count on franchise income as the primary income source until the pipeline is consistently producing 2+ closings per month.
**Growth path:** Side project → joined franchise broker network, first 5-10 candidate consultations through SBDC and veteran program referrals, matching tool live for the network's franchise portfolio. Replaces-part-time-income → 15-20 active candidates in pipeline, first 2-3 franchise agreements signed (~$40-80K in first closings, though lumpy). Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-30 active candidates, 1-2 closings/mo consistently, referral pipeline from signed franchisees and SBDC counselors generating inbound candidates without cold outreach, pushing well past $15-30K/mo at steady state.

### 3. Boutique HOA Management for Small Self-Managed Communities
**Problem:** Small homeowners associations — communities of 10-75 units, typically townhome developments, small condo buildings, or tight-knit planned neighborhoods — are either self-managed by volunteer board members who are overwhelmed and unqualified, or managed by a large professional management company that treats them as low-priority accounts given their small size relative to the company's 500-unit community clients. The result is the same in both cases: delinquent assessments go unpursued, vendor contracts are never competitively bid, reserve funds are underfunded, board meetings are chaotic, and the community's CC&Rs are inconsistently enforced. A boutique HOA management service that genuinely focuses on small communities — not as a loss leader for bigger clients, but as the intentional target market — can charge meaningfully more per unit than large companies while delivering dramatically better service.
**What to build:** A boutique HOA management service — an assessment billing and delinquency tracking system (sending monthly or quarterly assessment invoices, tracking payment, and initiating the delinquency process per the CC&Rs when owners fall behind), a vendor management and bid coordination workflow (maintaining a preferred vendor list by service category, soliciting competitive bids for maintenance work, and tracking vendor performance), a reserve fund tracking and projection model (current reserve balance vs. funded percentage vs. projected capital expenditure schedule — the core of the annual reserve study), a board meeting preparation and minutes system (agenda template, prior action item tracking, post-meeting minutes distribution), and a violation and architectural review workflow (logging reported violations, tracking correspondence, and managing the hearing process) — delivered as a flat monthly management fee per unit.
**Skill fit:** Direct on the billing, tracking, and reporting tooling — assessment billing reconciliation, delinquency aging, vendor bid comparison, and reserve fund projection modeling are structured data and financial modeling problems the builder builds better and faster than a traditional HOA manager working from generic property management software that doesn't fit small communities well. No professional license is required to manage an HOA in most states (unlike property management for rental units, which requires a real estate license in most states — HOA management is a separate category exempt from that requirement in most jurisdictions, though a few states have specific HOA manager certification programs that are certifications rather than licenses).
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build an assessment billing and delinquency tracker, a board meeting agenda and minutes template, and a vendor bid comparison tool; the first 1-2 HOA clients are onboarded manually while the systems are refined against real community needs.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through HOA attorneys (who represent community associations in collection, CC&R enforcement, and governance matters and know exactly which communities are poorly managed), real estate attorneys who handle purchases in common-interest developments (they encounter HOA management problems at every closing when they review HOA financial documents for the buyer), and direct outreach to self-managed HOA board presidents (identifiable through county recorder deed filings that show the HOA as an entity — the board president's name and address are often in the association documents).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $30-60/unit/mo management fee (small communities justify higher per-unit pricing than large ones because the per-community overhead is similar regardless of size); managing 8-12 HOA communities averaging 35 units each = 280-420 units under management × $40/unit/mo average = **$11,200-16,800/mo**, achieved with 8-12 client relationships rather than 280 individual clients to manage independently.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** HOA management contracts typically run 1-3 years with 60-90 day termination notice provisions, creating highly durable recurring revenue — a community that is being well-managed doesn't switch providers, and switching costs (transitioning financial records, vendor relationships, and board communications to a new manager) are high enough that retention is the norm rather than the exception. Each new community added compounds the monthly revenue without the prior communities churning out, building a base that is genuinely stable over time.
**Biggest risk:** The first complaint from an unhappy homeowner who doesn't understand the difference between the HOA's authority (enforcing CC&Rs, managing common areas) and the management company's role (administering the HOA's decisions) can create friction that consumes disproportionate time; establish clear written communication protocols with each board about what decisions the management company makes vs. what requires a board vote, so homeowner complaints are directed to the board rather than the management company for any substantive decisions.
**Growth path:** Side project → first 1-2 HOA community clients through HOA attorney referrals, assessment billing and meeting management tools live and delivering visible improvement over the community's prior chaos. Replaces-part-time-income → 4-5 community clients (140-175 units), management workflows systematized across communities, attorney referral relationships generating 1-2 new community inquiries per quarter (~$5-8K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 8-12 community clients (280-420 units), per-unit tooling reducing per-community management time, reserve fund modeling and vendor bid management differentiating from large generic companies, pushing past $11-17K/mo.

### 4. Real Property Title Abstract & Search Service
**Problem:** Every real estate closing requires a title search — a review of the county public records going back 40-60 years to establish a clean chain of title and identify any liens, judgments, easements, or encumbrances that would affect the buyer's ownership. Title companies that issue title insurance policies rely on independent title abstractors to actually perform this search work, since the abstractor's deep knowledge of local county records (deed books, mortgage records, tax lien filings, court judgments, probate records) is more efficient than the title company's generalist staff. In rural counties, small markets, and geographic areas outside major metro title company concentrations, abstracting is genuinely in short supply — title companies are actively seeking reliable independent abstractors who can turn around accurate searches quickly.
**What to build:** A title abstracting service — a county record research workflow (systematically searching each relevant record type: deeds, mortgages, releases, judgments, mechanics' liens, tax certificates, and probate records back to the root of title in the target county), an abstract report template (presenting each instrument found in chronological order with the relevant details — grantor/grantee, recording information, encumbrance type, and release status — in the format each title company client expects), a judgment and lien search workflow (checking federal and state court records, UCC filings, and county tax records for the current and prior owners), and a turnaround-time management system (tracking pending orders and their committed delivery dates, since title companies have closing deadlines that make turnaround time as important as accuracy).
**Skill fit:** Direct — title abstracting is a structured-data research and extraction problem: navigate a public records system → extract relevant instruments → classify by type → organize chronologically → present in a standardized format. The builder's data research skills apply immediately and more systematically than a traditional abstractor's manual process. Geographic information systems (GIS) experience helps with legal description interpretation; basic real estate document literacy (reading deeds, mortgages, and easement language) is learnable within the first handful of searches.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build a search workflow checklist and abstract report template for one target county; the first 5-10 searches are done manually to learn the county's specific record organization and quirks before optimizing the research process; no equipment or physical presence is required for counties with online record access (the majority of US counties with significant real estate activity have digitized their records).
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through title companies directly (title companies are findable in every county, actively use independent abstractors, and make purchase decisions at the operations manager level rather than through a long enterprise sales process); title underwriters (First American, Fidelity, Old Republic, Stewart) also maintain abstractor networks and refer work through their local agent relationships; real estate attorneys who handle their own title work are a secondary channel.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $75-200 per title search depending on county and scope (a two-owner residential search is $75-100; a multi-chain commercial search requiring 60-year depth is $150-200+); completing 80-120 searches/mo once 3-5 title company clients are placing regular orders = **$7-18K/mo**, with the throughput increasing as the county's record organization becomes deeply familiar and each subsequent search takes less time than the last.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Title search volume in a county tracks real estate transaction volume, which is driven by interest rates and inventory rather than the abstractor's effort — a market with strong transaction volume generates consistent order flow from established title company clients without any ongoing sales effort. The knowledge of a specific county's record organization (which deed books have been re-indexed, which years had floods that damaged records, which grantor names have indexing errors) is genuinely irreplicable from outside and creates a moat that protects market share in that county from distant abstractors who lack the local knowledge.
**Growth path:** Side project → first title company client relationship established, first 10-15 searches completed in one target county, abstract report format accepted by the client without revision requests. Replaces-part-time-income → 2-3 title company clients placing regular orders, 40-60 searches/mo, turnaround-time reliability building referral trust (~$4-8K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 4-6 title company clients, 80-120 searches/mo in one county plus adjacent county coverage added, pushing past $8-18K/mo with the county-specific expertise making this abstractor the default choice for any title company working in the target area.

### 5. Employee Compliance Training Program Management
**Problem:** Businesses across a wide range of industries face mandatory training requirements that most owners don't actively manage: OSHA 10/30 certification for construction workers, sexual harassment prevention training (required by law in California, New York, Illinois, and others), food handler and food manager certification (required in most states for restaurant and food service employees), anti-money laundering (AML) training for financial services staff, HIPAA privacy training for healthcare workers, and drivers' hours-of-service training for transportation companies. Most small businesses (20-200 employees) handle this sporadically — running a training when someone complains or when a regulator asks — rather than systematically, leaving them exposed to fines, audit findings, and liability. A managed training program service tracks which employees need which training, ensures completion before deadlines, maintains the records, and delivers the training through existing certified providers rather than building content from scratch.
**What to build:** A compliance training program management service — an employee roster and training requirement mapping tool (each employee role → applicable training requirements → frequency → certification validity period), a training completion tracking system (logging completions with certificate documentation, expiration dates, and upcoming renewal alerts), a training scheduling and enrollment workflow (connecting employees to the right existing certified training providers — online platforms like 360training, ClickSafety, StateFoodSafety, or in-person providers for OSHA card training), a compliance status dashboard for the business owner (showing which employees are current, which are due within 30 days, and which are overdue), and a regulator-ready records package (a formatted compliance summary the business can produce immediately when an OSHA inspector, health department, or licensing board asks for training records) — charged as a monthly retainer per business based on employee count.
**Skill fit:** Direct on the tracking and workflow tooling — employee roster management, certification expiration tracking, training provider integration, and compliance status reporting are exactly the structured data and workflow problems the builder builds well. The compliance requirement research (which training is required for which role under which regulation in which state) is a one-time learning investment per business type that becomes reusable across all clients in that vertical.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build an employee training tracker and certification expiration alert system for one specific industry vertical (construction with OSHA requirements and food service with food handler certification are the highest-volume starting points, each with clear, well-documented training requirements); the first 3-5 clients are onboarded manually while the intake workflow is validated.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — distribute through commercial insurance agents (who cover businesses with explicit training compliance requirements and know exactly which clients have lapses that create liability), HR consulting firms (who advise small businesses on employment law compliance and encounter training gaps constantly), and industry-specific trade associations (construction associations, restaurant associations, and transportation associations all have members with real training compliance problems).
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $150-500/mo per business client based on employee count and training complexity (a 15-employee restaurant needing food handler + sexual harassment training is $150-200/mo; a 60-employee construction company needing OSHA + driver safety training is $350-500/mo); 30-50 active business clients = **$6-18K/mo**, with the retainer base growing as each client's employee count grows and as clients refer others in their industry association network.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Training compliance is recurring by design — certifications expire, new employees need onboarding, and regulations add new requirements over time. A business that has outsourced this problem doesn't bring it back in-house when the retainer renews; the switching cost (rebuilding the training records and expiration calendar from scratch) makes retention the default outcome. Each new employee a client hires increases the scope of work and justifies a retainer increase, so client revenue grows without re-signing them.
**Biggest risk:** Regulatory requirements for specific training types vary significantly by state and sometimes by municipality — a food handler training that satisfies California requirements may not satisfy Texas requirements, and building a multi-state practice without maintaining accurate state-specific requirement data creates compliance gaps that reflect on the service. Start in one state with a well-documented requirement structure (California and New York have the most extensive and publicly documented mandatory training frameworks) before expanding to additional states, and build a state-specific requirement reference database as the core operational asset.
**Growth path:** Side project → first 3-5 business clients in one vertical (restaurants or construction) through commercial insurance agent referrals, training tracker live for the most common training types in that vertical. Replaces-part-time-income → 12-18 clients across 2-3 industries, compliance status reporting demonstrating audit-readiness value, insurance agent referral partners generating consistent new client introductions (~$3-6K/mo). Replaces-full-time-salary → 30-50 clients across construction, food service, healthcare, and transportation verticals, state-specific requirement database covering 5-8 states, pushing past $8-15K/mo.

## This Session's Pick: Start With #4, Track #2 as the Highest-Per-Close Parallel Bet

**Clearest ramp to first dollar:** Idea #4 (title abstracting) has the most direct path to revenue in this batch — title companies are findable, actively use independent abstractors, and make vendor decisions at the operations level without a lengthy approval process. The first search can be completed and invoiced within 2-3 weeks of establishing the first client relationship, and the county-specific knowledge moat builds with every search completed, making early-mover advantage in a target county genuinely durable. The skill fit (structured public records research) is the most direct match to the builder's data research background of any idea in this session.

**Highest-per-close parallel bet:** Idea #2 (franchise matchmaking) has the highest single-transaction ceiling of any idea across all 14 sessions — $20,000-30,000 per signed franchise agreement, with 2 closings in a month generating more than most other ideas produce in a quarter. The 90-120 day sales cycle means it takes longer to build the pipeline than most ideas in this series, but joining a franchise broker network immediately provides a portfolio to represent, training, and often inbound candidate referrals from the network's own marketing. Building the franchise candidate pipeline in parallel with title abstracting (the two distribution channels — SBDCs and title companies — don't compete for the same outreach time) creates a portfolio where the abstracting income is steady and immediate while the franchise income is lumpy but dramatically higher-ceiling.
