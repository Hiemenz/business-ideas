### 1. Structural Demolition Contractor Pre-Demolition Survey, Utility-Disconnect & Silica Exposure Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Structural demolition contractors — the companies operating the excavators, wrecking balls, and high-reach shears that bring down buildings — operate under OSHA's Demolition standard (29 CFR 1926 Subpart T), which requires a documented engineering survey of the structure by a competent person before any demolition work begins, assessing the condition of the framing, floors, and walls and the possibility of unplanned collapse during the operation (29 CFR 1926.850(a)); the same section requires that all electric, gas, water, steam, sewer, and other utility service lines serving the structure be shut off, capped, or otherwise controlled at a point outside the building before wrecking starts, with confirmation typically required from the utility company itself. Separately, many states and municipalities license "demolition contractor" as its own contractor classification distinct from general building contracting — New York City's Department of Buildings, for example, requires a Class 1 or Class 2 Licensed Demolition Contractor credential with its own continuing-education and renewal cycle — and OSHA's respirable crystalline silica in construction standard (29 CFR 1926.1153) requires any contractor performing tasks that generate silica dust, including concrete and masonry demolition, to maintain a written exposure control plan mapping each task to its required engineering control or respiratory protection under the standard's Table 1, reviewed and updated at least annually. Before a demolition contractor can even mobilize, they also need confirmation that the facility owner or an asbestos abatement subcontractor has filed the required NESHAP 10-working-day demolition notification (40 CFR 61 Subpart M) — a filing the demolition contractor doesn't own but is exposed by if it's missing or the crew starts before the notification window closes. A small demolition contractor running three to eight concurrent jobs tracks the engineering survey sign-off, utility-disconnect confirmations, the silica exposure control plan's task matrix, its own state/local license renewal, and the NESHAP notification confirmation for each job — usually across a job-folder system a project manager assembles by hand, and a crew that mobilizes on a site without a documented engineering survey on file, or before all utilities are confirmed disconnected, is exactly the gap an OSHA post-incident investigation or a state licensing board complaint is built to catch, with unplanned-collapse and utility-strike incidents being among the most severe possible findings in the trade.

**What to build:** A per-job engineering-survey document tracker recording the competent person's name, qualifications, survey date, and structural findings, with a hard gate blocking the job from being marked "cleared to demolish" until the survey is on file; a per-job utility-disconnect checklist covering each utility type with confirmation date and utility-company reference number; a state/local demolition-contractor license and continuing-education renewal tracker; a silica exposure control plan tracker mapping each job's demolition tasks against OSHA's Table 1 controls with an annual plan-review reminder; and a NESHAP-notification confirmation gate that blocks a job's start date until the owner's or abatement subcontractor's 10-day notification filing is confirmed on file.

**Skill fit:** OSHA's 1926 Subpart T and 1926.1153 standards are published, publicly available federal regulations with concrete numeric thresholds, and the "no dispatch until the survey/checklist/plan is on file" gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to the entity that physically operates the wrecking equipment rather than the abatement subcontractor whose notification obligation is entirely separate.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the engineering-survey tracker with its dispatch gate, the utility-disconnect checklist, and the silica exposure control plan tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent demolition contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Demolition Association (NDA) runs an active member directory and regional chapter events reaching small independent demolition contractors directly, supplemented by state demolition-contractor license registries that give a second cold-outreach list.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-349/mo per contractor depending on concurrent job volume and crew count. 45-60 contractors × $269/mo avg = $12,105-16,140/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new job triggers a fresh engineering survey, utility-disconnect checklist, and silica task-matrix cycle, and the contractor's own license renews independently — a continuous, job-volume-driven compliance stream that scales directly with how many demolition contracts the company wins.

**Biggest risk:** Contractors may believe their general liability insurer or the general contractor's own permit process already covers this, since the paperwork often gets buried inside a larger project's documentation. Mitigate by leading with the unplanned-collapse and utility-strike incident scenario, which resonates immediately with any demolition contractor who has had a near-miss on a job site.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 contractors ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 35 contractors ($9.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 contractors plus a multi-crew dispatch tier for contractors running several jobs simultaneously.

### 2. Roofing Contractor State License, OSHA Fall-Protection & Manufacturer-Certified-Installer Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Roofing contractors operate under a state-specific roofing-contractor license classification in many states, distinct from a general building-contractor license — Florida's Chapter 489 Certified/Registered Roofing Contractor credential, Louisiana's State Licensing Board for Contractors roofing classification, and California's C-39 Roofing classification under the CSLB among them — renewed on a set cycle with a continuing-education requirement, and operating without that specific classification (working under a general contractor license alone, in a state that requires the roofing-specific one) is a licensing-board finding that can void the underlying contract with the homeowner. OSHA's fall-protection provisions specific to roofing work (29 CFR 1926.501(b)(10)-(13)) require a guardrail system, safety net, or personal fall-arrest system for any employee working on a low-slope or steep roof six feet or more above a lower level, with a written fall-protection plan required whenever conventional protection is judged infeasible on a low-slope roof — violations of this exact standard rank among OSHA's most frequently cited nationally nearly every year, with per-violation penalties exceeding $16,000 and substantially more for a repeat or willful citation. Layered on top, manufacturer certified-installer programs (GAF Master Elite, CertainTeed SELECT ShingleMaster, Owens Corning Preferred Contractor, Malarkey Certified Installer) require the contractor to maintain current liability insurance, hit a minimum annual install volume, and complete periodic recertification training to stay eligible to offer the manufacturer's extended or enhanced warranty — a warranty tier that's frequently the deciding factor in a homeowner's contractor choice, and a lapsed insurance certificate or a missed recertification session silently cuts the contractor off from selling every job that depends on that warranty. A small roofing company running several crews tracks its own state license renewal, per-job fall-protection method and written-plan documentation, and multiple manufacturer certified-installer program requirements — usually across a job-folder system and a filing cabinet of insurance certificates, and a crew working a roof without a documented fall-protection plan, or a contractor selling a manufacturer-backed warranty job after its certified-installer status quietly lapsed, is exactly the gap an OSHA inspection or a homeowner's warranty-denial complaint is built to catch.

**What to build:** A state roofing-contractor license and continuing-education renewal tracker; a per-job fall-protection compliance checklist recording the protection method used, roof pitch, and a written fall-protection plan on file whenever guardrails or nets are infeasible; a multi-manufacturer certified-installer program tracker covering each program's insurance-certificate currency, install-volume threshold, and recertification-training due date; and a general liability/workers'-comp insurance-certificate renewal tracker feeding both the state license and every manufacturer program simultaneously.

**Skill fit:** State contractor-license renewal cycles, OSHA's roofing-specific fall-protection thresholds, and manufacturer certified-installer program requirements are all published, structured compliance data, and the "confirm the record is on file before the job proceeds" gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a trade this repo has so far only touched through a storm-damage insurance-supplement paperwork tool with no license, OSHA, or manufacturer-credential component.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the state license tracker, the per-job fall-protection checklist, and the multi-manufacturer certified-installer tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent roofing contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Roofing Contractors Association (NRCA) runs an active member directory and state-affiliate chapters reaching small independent roofing contractors directly, supplemented by manufacturer certified-installer program directories (GAF, CertainTeed, Owens Corning all publish searchable contractor locators) that give a second cold-outreach list of contractors already holding exactly the credentials this tool tracks.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $149-299/mo per contractor depending on crew count and number of manufacturer certifications held. 55-70 contractors × $219/mo avg = $12,045-15,330/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every job triggers its own fall-protection documentation event, every manufacturer program's insurance-certificate and recertification cycle renews independently, and the contractor's own state license renews on its own schedule — a continuous, job-volume-driven compliance stream that scales directly with how many roofs the company works.

**Biggest risk:** Contractors often assume their general liability insurer or their state general-contractor license already covers this, since fall-protection paperwork and manufacturer-program renewals get buried in day-to-day job management. Mitigate by leading with OSHA's own most-frequently-cited-standard data for roofing fall protection, which resonates immediately with any contractor who has had an inspector show up on a job site.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 contractors ($3.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 35 contractors ($7.7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 55-70 contractors plus a multi-manufacturer-certification concierge tier for contractors adding a second or third certified-installer program.

### 3. Rope-Access & High-Rise Building-Envelope Maintenance Contractor IRATA/SPRAT Certification & Facade-Inspection-Cycle Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Rope-access companies performing high-rise window cleaning, facade repair, and building-envelope inspection work operate under OSHA's rope descent systems standard (29 CFR 1910.27), which requires the building owner's roof anchorage points to be inspected and certified by a qualified person at least annually before any rope-descent work can begin — a certification the rope-access contractor must have on file before rigging off a roof, since working from uncertified anchorages is itself an OSHA violation exposing the contractor directly. Individual technicians are certified through IRATA (Industrial Rope Access Trade Association) or SPRAT (Society of Professional Rope Access Technicians) at Levels 1 through 3, each level requiring logged rope-access hours to advance and, for IRATA certification specifically, a revalidation exam and updated logbook every three years to stay current. In cities with periodic facade-inspection ordinances — New York City's Facade Inspection & Safety Program (FISP, formerly Local Law 11) and Chicago's facade ordinance among them — building owners must have their exterior walls inspected on a recurring statutory cycle by a Qualified Exterior Wall Inspector, and the rope-access contractor performing the physical hands-on drop-testing and probing work needs to track each building's inspection-cycle due date, since a filing that lapses past its statutory window in NYC escalates into an accumulating public-safety violation the building owner will trace directly back to whichever contractor was supposed to be tracking it. A small rope-access company running 5-15 technicians across dozens of client buildings tracks each technician's IRATA/SPRAT level and revalidation date, each building's annual OSHA anchorage certification, each building's facade-inspection-cycle due date, and per-job rigging-plan permits filed with the local Department of Buildings — usually on a spreadsheet and a shoebox of laminated certification cards, and a technician working past their revalidation date, or a building's facade-inspection filing that lapses past its cycle, is exactly the finding a Department of Buildings audit or a workplace-safety inspection is built to catch.

**What to build:** A per-technician IRATA/SPRAT certification-level and revalidation tracker with logged rope-access hours accumulated against the advancement or revalidation threshold; a per-building OSHA anchorage-certification renewal tracker (annual, per 1910.27); a per-building municipal facade-inspection-cycle tracker computing each property's next filing due date and flagging any building approaching or past its statutory window; and a per-job rigging-plan and Department of Buildings permit tracker.

**Skill fit:** OSHA's rope descent systems standard, IRATA/SPRAT's published certification and revalidation requirements, and municipal facade-inspection-cycle ordinances are all structured, public regulatory and accrediting-body data, and computing each building's next-due inspection cycle from its last filing date is the same deterministic "compute the deadline, flag the lapse" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a rope-access trade this repo has never addressed.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the technician certification/revalidation tracker, the building anchorage-certification tracker, and the facade-inspection-cycle tracker for New York City and Chicago's ordinances first. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent rope-access companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — IRATA's and SPRAT's certified-company directories give a direct, highly qualified cold-outreach list of companies already managing exactly this certification and inspection-cycle burden by hand.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249-449/mo per company depending on technician count and building portfolio size. 35-45 companies × $339/mo avg = $11,865-15,255/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every technician's certification runs its own multi-year revalidation clock, every serviced building's OSHA anchorage certification renews annually, and every building under a municipal facade-inspection ordinance runs its own recurring statutory cycle — a continuous, technician-and-building-portfolio-driven compliance stream that grows as the company adds buildings and hires technicians.

**Biggest risk:** A small company working a handful of buildings in a city without a facade-inspection ordinance has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for companies operating in NYC, Chicago, and other facade-ordinance cities specifically, where the inspection-cycle tracking genuinely outgrows a spreadsheet, and by positioning the technician-certification tracker alone as a lower-tier entry offer for companies elsewhere.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 companies ($3.4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 26 companies ($8.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 35-45 companies plus a multi-city facade-ordinance expansion tier as companies take on buildings in additional jurisdictions.

### 4. Limousine, Black Car & For-Hire Vehicle (Livery) Company State PUC/Municipal TLC License & Chauffeur Permit Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Limousine, black car, and livery sedan/SUV companies operate under a state Public Utilities Commission certificate of public convenience and necessity, or in cities like New York a municipal Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC) base license, distinct from both the FMCSA passenger-carrier operating authority and CSA safety rating that govern large motorcoaches, and from the Medicaid managed-care enrollment and monthly OIG exclusion-list screening obligation that govern non-emergency medical transportation. Every driver must hold an individual chauffeur or livery-driver permit renewed on the state or municipal regulator's own cycle, typically requiring a background check and, in many jurisdictions, periodic drug testing, and every vehicle must pass an annual state or PUC-administered inspection and carry livery plates renewed on their own cycle, with the regulator requiring proof of commercial insurance filed directly with the commission at coverage minimums well above a standard personal auto policy (often $1.5 million combined single limit or more). A small livery company running a fleet of 10-30 vehicles and drivers tracks each driver's chauffeur-permit and background-check renewal, each vehicle's annual inspection and livery-plate renewal, and the company's own PUC certificate or TLC base-license renewal and insurance-filing currency — usually on a dispatcher's spreadsheet updated between rides, and a driver dispatched past their permit's renewal date, or a vehicle operating on a lapsed inspection, is exactly the finding a PUC or TLC compliance sweep is built to catch, with penalties ranging from per-vehicle fines to suspension of the company's operating certificate.

**What to build:** A company-level PUC certificate or TLC base-license renewal tracker; a per-driver chauffeur-permit and background-check/drug-test renewal tracker with a "cleared to dispatch" gate; a per-vehicle annual inspection and livery-plate renewal tracker; and an insurance-certificate filing tracker matched to the regulator's specific minimum coverage requirement.

**Skill fit:** State PUC certificate requirements and municipal TLC licensing rules are public, structured regulatory data, and the per-driver "cleared to dispatch" eligibility check is the same deterministic "block the assignment until the record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an intrastate for-hire-vehicle category this repo has never addressed despite covering both the FMCSA-regulated charter bus and the Medicaid-regulated NEMT niches.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the base-license/certificate tracker, the per-driver eligibility gate, and the per-vehicle inspection tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent livery and black-car companies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Limousine Association (NLA) runs an active member directory and regional chapter events reaching small independent livery operators directly, supplemented by state PUC-published lists of certificated for-hire carriers.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $179-329/mo per company depending on fleet size. 45-60 companies × $249/mo avg = $11,205-14,940/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every driver's chauffeur permit and every vehicle's inspection and plate renew on their own independent cycles for the life of the company's fleet, and the company's own certificate/base-license renews on its own schedule — a continuous, fleet-and-driver-driven compliance stream that scales directly with how many vehicles and drivers the company adds.

**Biggest risk:** Companies often believe their dispatch software's basic driver roster already covers this, since permit and inspection tracking feels like a low-priority administrative task amid daily dispatch pressure. Mitigate by leading with the operating-certificate-suspension scenario, which owners recognize as an existential threat since a suspended certificate stops every vehicle in the fleet simultaneously.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 companies ($3.7K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 34 companies ($8.5K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 companies plus a multi-jurisdiction tier for companies operating fleets across state or municipal TLC boundaries.

### 5. Environmental Emergency Spill Response Contractor HAZWOPER, USCG OSRO Classification & PREP Exercise Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Environmental emergency spill response contractors — companies dispatched to contain and clean up chemical and oil releases at industrial sites, transportation incidents, and waterways — operate under OSHA's HAZWOPER emergency-response training requirement (29 CFR 1910.120(q)), which requires every responder to hold initial training matched to their responder level (First Responder Awareness, First Responder Operations, Hazardous Materials Technician, or Hazardous Materials Specialist, with Technician and Specialist levels typically requiring 24-40 hours of initial training) plus a mandatory annual 8-hour refresher to stay current — a responder dispatched past their refresher due date is legally barred from working the site. Companies that respond to oil spills additionally pursue U.S. Coast Guard Oil Spill Removal Organization (OSRO) classification under the Oil Pollution Act of 1990 (33 CFR Part 154, Appendix C), a credential many client contracts require, which demands documented response-equipment capability reviewed periodically and participation in scheduled PREP (National Preparedness for Response Exercise Program) drills — announced and unannounced tabletop and equipment-deployment exercises on a required cadence — with a lapsed exercise schedule risking suspension of the classification itself. Several states, including Texas and California, additionally require an environmental emergency-response contractor to hold its own state registration renewed on a set cycle. A small spill-response company running a roster of HAZWOPER-certified responders across several standing client contracts tracks each responder's certification level and annual refresher due date, the company's OSRO classification renewal and PREP exercise schedule, and its state contractor registration renewal — usually across a training binder and a compliance officer's personal calendar, and a responder dispatched past their refresher due date, or a missed PREP exercise discovered during a USCG classification audit, is exactly the finding that can suspend the company's OSRO classification and cost it every client contract that requires a classified responder on call.

**What to build:** A per-responder HAZWOPER certification-level and annual-refresher tracker with a "cleared to respond" dispatch gate; a USCG OSRO classification renewal and PREP exercise-schedule tracker recording drill type, due date, and completion documentation; a state emergency-response-contractor registration renewal tracker; and an equipment/PPE readiness inventory log tied to the company's OSRO response-capability documentation.

**Skill fit:** OSHA's HAZWOPER training requirements and the Coast Guard's published OSRO classification and PREP exercise cadence are structured, public regulatory data, and the per-responder "cleared to respond" dispatch gate is the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an emergency-response readiness category distinct from every hazardous-waste-generator, recycling, or medical-waste idea already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-responder certification/refresher tracker with its dispatch gate, the OSRO classification and PREP exercise tracker, and the state registration tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent spill-response contractors.

**Time to first $:** 30-60 days — the Spill Control Association of America (SCAA) runs an active member directory and regional meetings reaching small independent spill-response contractors directly, supplemented by state environmental emergency-response contractor registries that give a second cold-outreach list.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per company depending on responder headcount and whether the company holds OSRO classification. 30-40 companies × $429/mo avg = $12,870-17,160/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every responder's annual HAZWOPER refresher recurs on its own clock, PREP exercises recur on a required schedule for the life of an OSRO classification, and state registration renews on its own cycle — a continuous, headcount-and-classification-driven compliance stream tied directly to the company's legal authority to keep responding to spill calls.

**Biggest risk:** Companies without OSRO classification and a smaller responder roster have a comparatively lower-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for OSRO-classified companies and multi-responder teams specifically, where a classification suspension is an existential threat to every contract, and by positioning the HAZWOPER refresher tracker alone as a lower-tier entry offer for smaller companies.

**Growth path:** Side project → 10 companies ($4.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 22 companies ($9.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 30-40 companies plus an OSRO-classification concierge tier for companies pursuing or maintaining Coast Guard classification.

## This Session's Pick: #5 (Environmental Emergency Spill Response) for the highest income ceiling and starkest single-point-of-failure stakes; #3 (Rope-Access/High-Rise Facade Maintenance) for the most differentiated, least-crowded niche

Environmental emergency spill response stands out because a suspended USCG OSRO classification is a single point of failure that can end every client contract a company holds simultaneously — the same "certification loss unravels the whole business" dynamic that made this series' PEO and title-agency picks compelling in earlier sessions — and the Spill Control Association of America's tight member community gives direct access to operators who already feel this risk acutely every time a PREP exercise deadline approaches.

Rope-access/high-rise facade maintenance is the most differentiated pick because it is a genuinely narrow trade with almost no existing compliance-SaaS competition, IRATA and SPRAT's certified-company directories deliver a small but highly organized and reachable buyer community, and the combination of a multi-year technician revalidation cycle with an annual building anchorage certification and a municipal facade-inspection cycle gives the product three independent, non-optional recurring compliance events layered on top of each other.
