### 1. Human Crematory Cremation-Authorization & Identification Chain-of-Custody Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Crematories operate under state cemetery/funeral licensing boards that require a fully executed cremation authorization form signed by the legally authorized next of kin before any case can be scheduled, a local registrar-issued disposition or cremation permit filed and on hand before the retort can run, and — because most states enforce a strict one-body-at-a-time cremation rule — an unbroken identification-tag chain linking a specific decedent from intake, through the retort cycle, to the return of cremated remains to the family or funeral home of record. A crematory that runs a case without a completed authorization form, a filed disposition permit, or a documented identification-tag match at every handoff point is exactly the finding a state cemetery/funeral board inspection or a family complaint investigation is built to catch, and given the irreversible nature of cremation, an identification error is both a business-ending liability event and a mandatory-reportable incident in most states. Every retort operator must also hold a current certified operator credential — commonly the Cremation Association of North America's (CANA) certification — and a crematory that runs a technician past their certification's renewal window compounds the exposure on every case that technician handles. A small independent crematory or a crematory operating as a satellite facility for several referring funeral homes, processing dozens of cases a week, manages authorization-form completeness, permit currency, and identification-tag chain-of-custody with a paper case folder and a logbook, with no systematic cross-check between a specific decedent's paperwork and the actual case running through the retort that day.
**What to build:** A per-case authorization-and-permit completeness tracker with a "cleared to cremate" gate blocking retort scheduling until the signed authorization form and the filed disposition permit are both on file; an identification-tag chain-of-custody log recording every handoff point (intake, retort load, retort unload, remains processing, return) against the same case ID; and a retort-operator CANA certification currency tracker gating which technician can be assigned to a scheduled case.
**Skill fit:** State cremation-authorization requirements and CANA's certified-operator program are public, structured regulatory and industry-credential data with a well-defined per-case record model, and the cremate-eligibility gate plus the identification chain-of-custody log are the same deterministic "block the action until the required document exists / verify the chain at every handoff" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a death-care trade most small crematories currently track through a paper case folder with no systematic cross-check between a decedent's authorization paperwork and the case physically running through the retort.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the authorization/permit tracker with the cremate-eligibility gate, the identification-tag chain-of-custody log, and the CANA certification tracker; land the first 5-8 crematories through Cremation Association of North America (CANA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — CANA's member directory and state cemetery/funeral association chapters, where small independent and satellite crematories already juggle per-case authorization paperwork and identification chain-of-custody with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base (up to 40 cremations/month) + $4/mo per additional case. Average client (70 cases/month, 30 over base) = $299 + 30×$4 = $419/mo. 24-29 crematories × $419/mo = **$10,056-12,151/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every single case reopens its own authorization-and-permit check and its own identification chain-of-custody log from intake to return, with no case ever inheriting compliance from the last one — a continuous, per-case compliance stream tied directly to the crematory's daily volume rather than an annual license renewal.
**Biggest risk:** A case run without a completed authorization form, or an identification-tag mismatch at any handoff point, is exactly the irreversible failure that ends a crematory's license and its referring funeral-home relationships overnight — the cremate-eligibility gate and the chain-of-custody log are the core protections, built to make the compliant path the only path the tool allows.
**Growth path:** Side project → authorization/permit tracker, chain-of-custody log, and operator certification tracker deployed, first 5-8 crematory retainers through CANA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 14-17 crematories. Replaces-full-time-salary → 24-29 crematories, pushing past $10.06-12.15K/mo.

### 2. Elevator & Escalator Maintenance Contractor ASME A17.1 Categorized Inspection & QEI Certification Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Elevator and escalator maintenance contractors operate under ASME A17.1 as adopted into state and municipal elevator-safety codes, which require every unit under a service contract to pass a categorized periodic inspection — commonly a Category 1 annual inspection and a Category 5 five-year full load test — witnessed and certified by a state-recognized Qualified Elevator Inspector (QEI), with a current inspection certificate that must be physically posted in the unit's cab at all times. A unit running past its Category 1 or Category 5 inspection window, or a cab displaying an expired certificate, is exactly the finding a state elevator-safety-division audit or a building-owner's own liability review is built to catch, and an expired certificate is often grounds for a state-ordered shutdown of the unit until it's re-inspected. Every field technician performing maintenance must also hold an individually renewed elevator mechanic license (commonly tracked against the National Association of Elevator Contractors' Certified Elevator Technician program), and a contractor dispatching a technician on a lapsed license compounds the exposure on every unit that technician services. A small independent elevator maintenance contractor running service contracts on 40-100 units across a metro area's office buildings, apartment complexes, and parking structures manages per-unit categorized inspection cycles, per-technician license currency, and cab-certificate posting compliance with an office spreadsheet and no systematic alert distinguishing which specific unit's Category 5 five-year window is approaching from which one just cleared its annual.
**What to build:** A per-unit categorized-inspection tracker (Category 1 annual, Category 5 five-year) with due-date countdowns and a QEI-witnessed sign-off log; a cab-certificate posting confirmation log tied to each completed inspection; and a per-technician elevator mechanic license renewal tracker with a "cleared to service" gate blocking assignment of any technician whose license has lapsed.
**Skill fit:** ASME A17.1's categorized-inspection-interval structure and state elevator mechanic licensing cycles are public, structured code and regulatory data with a well-defined per-unit and per-technician record model, and the inspection-due-date countdown plus the service-eligibility gate are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the code-mandated interval / block the action until the credential clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a vertical-transportation trade most small contractors currently track on a spreadsheet with no systematic distinction between a specific unit's annual-versus-five-year inspection window.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the categorized-inspection tracker with due-date countdowns, the cab-certificate posting log, and the technician license gate; land the first 5-8 contractors through National Association of Elevator Contractors (NAEC) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — NAEC's member directory and state elevator contractor associations, where small independent maintenance contractors already juggle per-unit categorized-inspection deadlines and per-technician license renewals with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base (up to 25 units under contract) + $8/mo per additional unit. Average client (60 units, 35 over base) = $299 + 35×$8 = $579/mo. 16-20 contractors × $579/mo = **$9,264-11,580/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every unit under contract carries its own independent Category 1 and Category 5 inspection clock, and every technician's mechanic license renews on its own cycle — a continuous, per-unit and per-technician compliance stream tied directly to the contractor's service-portfolio size rather than a once-a-year filing.
**Biggest risk:** A unit running past its inspection window with an expired cab certificate, or a technician dispatched on a lapsed license, is exactly the finding that triggers a state-ordered shutdown and exposes the contractor to liability — the inspection countdown and the service-eligibility gate are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → categorized-inspection tracker, cab-certificate log, and technician license gate deployed, first 5-8 contractor retainers through NAEC outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 contractors. Replaces-full-time-salary → 16-20 contractors, pushing past $9.26-11.58K/mo.

### 3. Fire Sprinkler & Suppression System Contractor NFPA 25 Inspection Cascade & NICET Certification Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Fire sprinkler and suppression system contractors maintain water-based fire protection systems under NFPA 25, which mandates a fixed cascade of inspection and test intervals — quarterly checks on components like waterflow alarms, annual full-system inspections, and five-year internal obstruction investigations and gauge tests — with every interval calculated independently per system and per component. A contractor who lets a quarterly waterflow-alarm test or an annual main-drain test lapse past its NFPA 25 window is exactly the finding a local fire marshal's spot audit or an insurance carrier's loss-control inspection is built to catch, and a lapsed test on a system that later fails during an actual fire is the kind of liability exposure that can end a small fire-protection contracting business outright. Every technician performing inspections and testing must also carry current NICET (National Institute for Certification in Engineering Technologies) certification in fire protection, and a contractor's individual state fire-protection contractor license must stay current per jurisdiction, with a per-installation local fire marshal permit required before a new system can be placed in service. A small fire sprinkler contractor running service contracts on 50-150 systems across commercial and multifamily buildings manages the quarterly/annual/five-year test cascade, per-technician NICET currency, and per-installation permit tracking with a technician's paper inspection tags and a shared spreadsheet, with no systematic cross-check between a specific system's actual last-test date and what NFPA 25's cascade says is due next.
**What to build:** A per-system NFPA 25 inspection-and-test cascade tracker computing each component's next-due date independently (quarterly, annual, five-year) with escalating alerts as a deadline approaches; a per-technician NICET certification currency tracker with a "cleared to inspect" gate; and a per-installation local fire marshal permit tracker confirming each new system has its required permit on file before being placed in service.
**Skill fit:** NFPA 25's tabular inspection-and-test-interval structure and NICET's certification categories are public, highly structured standards that map directly onto a deadline-cascade rules engine — a strong fit for a developer who can encode a standard's interval table without fire-protection-engineering credentials, the same "compute the deadline from the code-mandated interval" pattern this series builds repeatedly.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-system NFPA 25 cascade tracker, the per-technician NICET certification gate, and the per-installation permit tracker; land the first 5-8 contractors through American Fire Sprinkler Association (AFSA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Fire Sprinkler Association's member directory and state fire-protection contractor associations, where small independent contractors already juggle per-system quarterly/annual/five-year test deadlines with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $279/mo base (up to 30 systems under contract) + $6/mo per additional system. Average client (70 systems, 40 over base) = $279 + 40×$6 = $519/mo. 18-23 contractors × $519/mo = **$9,342-11,937/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every system under contract carries its own independent quarterly, annual, and five-year test cascade running simultaneously, and every technician's NICET certification renews on its own cycle — a continuous, per-system and per-component compliance stream tied directly to the contractor's service-portfolio size.
**Biggest risk:** A lapsed quarterly or annual test on a system that later fails during a real fire is exactly the liability exposure that can end a small fire-protection contractor's business — the NFPA 25 cascade tracker is the core protection, built to surface the next-due test before a fire marshal or an insurance auditor finds it first.
**Growth path:** Side project → NFPA 25 cascade tracker, NICET certification gate, and installation permit tracker deployed, first 5-8 contractor retainers through AFSA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 11-14 contractors. Replaces-full-time-salary → 18-23 contractors, pushing past $9.34-11.94K/mo.

### 4. Certified Backflow Prevention Device Tester Annual Test & Water-Utility Reporting Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Certified backflow prevention testers service the backflow preventers installed on irrigation systems, commercial buildings, and fire suppression connections that local water utilities require under their cross-connection control programs — and in most jurisdictions, every installed device must be tested annually by a certified tester (commonly holding an ASSE 5110 or AWWA-affiliated credential), with the test result submitted directly to the water utility by a deadline tied to that specific device's install date or last-test anniversary, not a single fixed calendar date across a tester's whole portfolio. A device tested late, or a passing test result that's never actually submitted to the utility, is exactly the finding a water utility's cross-connection control audit is built to catch, and a customer whose device falls out of compliance can have their water service shut off by the utility — a consequence that falls squarely on the tester's reputation even though the utility, not the tester, pulls the trigger. A small independent backflow testing operation servicing several hundred devices across a metro area's commercial, irrigation, and fire-line accounts manages each device's rolling annual test-and-submission deadline with a paper test-tag carbon copy and a spreadsheet, with no systematic way to see which specific devices are approaching their individual annual window out of several hundred all on different anniversaries.
**What to build:** A per-device test-due-date tracker computing each device's individual annual deadline from its install or last-test date, with alerts as a device approaches its window; a water-utility submission-confirmation log tracking whether each completed test result has actually been submitted to the correct jurisdiction's utility; and a tester certification currency tracker for the technician's own ASSE/AWWA credential.
**Skill fit:** Cross-connection-control annual-testing requirements and certified-tester credentialing are public, structured municipal regulatory data with a well-defined per-device record model, and the rolling-deadline tracker plus the submission-confirmation log are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from a per-record anniversary date / confirm the required filing actually happened" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a plumbing-adjacent trade most small testers currently track through a carbon-copy test tag with no systematic view of several hundred devices' individually staggered annual deadlines.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the per-device rolling-deadline tracker, the water-utility submission-confirmation log, and the tester certification tracker; land the first 5-8 testers through American Backflow Prevention Association (ABPA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Backflow Prevention Association's member directory and state cross-connection-control certification program alumni networks, where small independent testers already juggle several hundred individually staggered annual test deadlines with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base (up to 100 devices tracked) + $1.50/mo per additional device. Average client (300 devices, 200 over base) = $199 + 200×$1.50 = $499/mo. 19-24 testers × $499/mo = **$9,481-11,976/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every device on a tester's book carries its own independent rolling annual deadline that never aligns with any other device's anniversary, and every new commercial or irrigation account added expands that staggered deadline set permanently — a continuous, per-device compliance stream that only grows as the tester's portfolio grows.
**Biggest risk:** A device tested late, or a passing result never actually submitted to the water utility, is exactly the gap that gets a customer's water service shut off and costs the tester the account — the rolling-deadline tracker and the submission-confirmation log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → rolling-deadline tracker, submission-confirmation log, and certification tracker deployed, first 5-8 tester retainers through ABPA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 11-14 testers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 19-24 testers, pushing past $9.48-11.98K/mo.

### 5. State-Licensed Money Transmitter & Check Casher BSA/AML, CTR & SAR Filing Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Small money transmitters and check cashers — storefront remittance agents, currency exchanges, and check-cashing outlets — operate under state money transmission licensing (such as New York's Department of Financial Services regime and California's Money Transmission Act) layered on top of federal Money Services Business registration with FinCEN under 31 CFR Part 1022, which requires a documented, board-approved Bank Secrecy Act/anti-money-laundering compliance program, mandatory Currency Transaction Reports (CTR) filed for any cash transaction exceeding $10,000, and Suspicious Activity Reports (SAR) filed within a strict federal 30-day window whenever a transaction pattern meets the regulatory trigger — deadlines that don't pause for a small operator's limited back-office staff. A location that misses a CTR filing deadline, or that fails to file a SAR within the 30-day window on a flagged pattern, is exactly the finding a FinCEN examination or a state DFS/DFPI compliance audit is built to catch, and civil money penalties for BSA violations routinely run into the tens of thousands of dollars per violation even for a small single-location operator — a penalty scale that can end a small money-transmission business outright. Every location's money transmitter license and every compliance officer's required BSA training must also stay current on their own renewal cycles. A small money transmitter or check-cashing operator running 2-5 licensed locations processes daily cash transactions with a teller-terminal log and a manual review of which transactions crossed the CTR threshold, with no systematic countdown from the moment a suspicious pattern is flagged to the federal 30-day SAR filing deadline.
**What to build:** A per-transaction CTR threshold-monitoring log flagging any cash transaction crossing $10,000 with a filing-deadline countdown; a SAR case tracker starting a 30-day countdown the moment a transaction pattern is flagged for review, with escalating alerts as the deadline approaches; and a per-location money transmitter license and compliance-officer BSA training renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** FinCEN's CTR/SAR filing-trigger rules and state money transmitter licensing cycles are public, structured federal and state regulatory text with a well-defined per-transaction and per-location record model, and the threshold-monitoring log plus the SAR countdown are the same deterministic "flag the trigger event and compute the deadline from it" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a regulated-financial-services trade most small operators currently track through a teller-terminal log with no systematic countdown from a flagged pattern to the federal filing deadline.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the CTR threshold-monitoring log, the SAR case tracker with the 30-day countdown, and the per-location license/training tracker; land the first 5-8 operators through Money Transmitter Regulators Association (MTRA) and National Money Transmitters Association affiliate outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Money Transmitters Association's member directory and state money-services-business associations, where small independent operators already juggle per-transaction CTR thresholds and the federal 30-day SAR filing window with no dedicated compliance software.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base per licensed location + $199/mo per additional location under the same operator. Average client (3 locations) = $349 + 2×$199 = $747/mo. 13-17 operators × $747/mo = **$9,711-12,699/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every cash transaction crossing the CTR threshold reopens its own filing-deadline check, and any flagged suspicious pattern starts its own independent 30-day SAR countdown at any time — a continuous, per-transaction compliance stream tied directly to the operator's daily cash volume rather than an annual license renewal.
**Biggest risk:** A missed CTR filing or a SAR filed past the federal 30-day window is exactly the finding that draws steep BSA civil money penalties and can trigger license revocation — the threshold-monitoring log and the SAR countdown are the core protections, built to catch the filing deadline before a FinCEN examiner does.
**Growth path:** Side project → CTR monitoring log, SAR countdown tracker, and license/training tracker deployed, first 5-8 operator retainers through MTRA and NMTA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 8-10 operators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 13-17 operators, pushing past $9.71-12.70K/mo.
