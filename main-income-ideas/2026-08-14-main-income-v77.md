### 1. Ignition Interlock Device (IID) Installer/Service Center State Certification & Violation-Reporting Compliance

**Problem:** Ignition interlock device installers and service centers — the shops that install and maintain court- or DMV-mandated breath-alcohol interlock devices for restricted-license drivers — must hold a current state DUI/DWI program or DMV installer certification, which in most states requires each technician to complete manufacturer-specific training and periodic recertification, and requires the shop itself to remain in good standing with the state program administrator that licenses which device models and installers may legally serve that state's court-ordered population; a technician performing installs or calibrations past a lapsed certification is exactly the finding a state program compliance audit targets and can result in the shop's authorization being suspended, cutting off its entire client referral pipeline from courts and probation offices overnight. Every installed device must be brought back for calibration on a fixed cycle set by the state (commonly every 30-60 days), and a driver who misses a scheduled calibration appointment, records a failed breath test, or triggers a tamper/circumvention alert generates a violation event that the installer is required to report to the state monitoring authority or the sentencing court within a strict window — commonly 24-72 hours — since courts rely on these reports to decide whether a restricted-license driver stays in compliance or has their driving privilege revoked; a late or missed violation report is one of the most common findings a state program review of an installer's compliance file targets, and repeated lapses put the shop's own certification at risk. A small installer running one or two service-center locations and managing anywhere from 100-300 actively monitored devices across a rolling calibration schedule handles per-technician certification currency, per-device calibration due dates, and time-sensitive violation-event reporting with no dedicated compliance function.

**What to build:** A per-technician state-certification and manufacturer-training currency tracker with an "authorized to install/calibrate" gate; a per-device calibration due-date tracker computing each unit's next appointment from the state-mandated cycle and flagging overdue units before a client's restricted license is put at risk; and a violation-event report generator that packages a failed test, missed appointment, or tamper alert into the state's required notification format and tracks the clock against the mandated reporting deadline.

**Skill fit:** State DUI/DWI program installer-certification cycles and device calibration intervals are structured, public regulatory data with a well-defined per-technician and per-device record requirement, and the calibration-due tracker plus the violation-report deadline clock are the same deterministic "compute the next-due date from the cycle / block or alert once a deadline is at risk" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a court-facing monitoring trade most small installers currently track on a manufacturer's basic portal with no systematic cross-check against the state's own reporting-deadline rules.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the technician-certification tracker, the per-device calibration scheduler, and the violation-event report generator with its deadline clock; land the first 5-8 service centers through direct outreach to independently owned franchise installers of the major device manufacturers (Smart Start, Intoxalock, LifeSafer) listed on state DUI-program installer directories.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — state DMV/DUI program public installer directories (which list every certified service center by name) combined with direct outreach to independently owned service centers, who already juggle rolling 30-60 day calibration cycles and 24-72 hour violation-reporting deadlines against a manufacturer's basic scheduling portal with no purpose-built compliance layer.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base (up to 100 devices tracked) + $1/mo per additional device. Average client (150 devices) = $249 + $50 = $299/mo. 32-40 service centers × $299/mo = **$9,568-11,960/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every actively monitored device carries its own independent 30-60 day calibration clock, every failed test or tamper alert opens a fresh, time-sensitive violation-report window, and every technician's certification recurs on its own renewal cycle — a continuous, per-device and per-event compliance stream that scales directly with how many restricted-license drivers a service center currently monitors, not a one-time install fee.

**Biggest risk:** A technician calibrating a device past a lapsed certification, a unit brought in late for its mandated calibration, or a violation event reported past the state's notification deadline is exactly the finding a state program compliance review is built to catch — the certification gate, the calibration scheduler, and the violation-report deadline clock are the core protections, and losing state authorization ends the shop's entire court-referral business.

**Growth path:** Side project → certification tracker, calibration scheduler, and violation-report generator deployed, first 5-8 service-center retainers through installer-directory outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 centers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 32-40 centers, pushing past $9.57-11.96K/mo.

### 2. Playground Safety Inspection Company CPSI Certification, CPSC/ASTM Equipment Standard & Surfacing Compliance

**Problem:** Independent playground safety inspection companies — hired by school districts, municipal park departments, HOAs, and daycare centers to conduct periodic playground equipment audits — depend on every inspector holding a current Certified Playground Safety Inspector (CPSI) credential issued by the National Recreation and Park Association (NRPA), which requires recertification via exam or renewal course on a fixed 3-year cycle; an inspection performed by a lapsed CPSI is not a valid safety audit in the eyes of most municipal and school-district contracts and insurance carriers, and it is exactly the gap a client's insurance renewal audit or a post-injury liability claim is built to expose. Every inspected site must be evaluated against the CPSC Public Playground Safety Handbook and ASTM F1487 equipment standards (entrapment, pinch-point, and fall-height hazards) on a periodic schedule most district and municipal contracts define annually, and protective surfacing must separately pass impact-attenuation testing under ASTM F1292, which many jurisdictions require retested after any surface material change or on a set interval — a site inspected on schedule for equipment hazards but overdue on its surfacing impact-attenuation retest is exactly the finding a district's insurance carrier or a post-fall injury investigation targets, since inadequate surfacing under a fall zone is one of the most litigated playground-injury fact patterns. Inspectors must also cross-reference each client site's actual installed equipment against the CPSC's active recall database, since a manufacturer recall on a specific swing hanger or slide component is a hazard an inspection report is expected to catch. A small inspection company covering a portfolio of school-district, municipal, and daycare-center sites manages per-site inspection-cycle scheduling, per-inspector CPSI certification currency, surfacing retest scheduling, and recall cross-referencing with no dedicated compliance function.

**What to build:** A per-site inspection-cycle tracker computing each site's next-due audit date from its contract terms; a per-inspector CPSI certification currency tracker with a "cleared to inspect" gate blocking a report from being issued once an inspector's 3-year credential lapses; a surfacing impact-attenuation retest scheduler flagging sites due for ASTM F1292 retesting; and a recall cross-reference alert matching each site's logged equipment inventory against the CPSC's active recall list.

**Skill fit:** NRPA's CPSI renewal cycle and the CPSC recall database are public, structured data sources with a well-defined per-inspector and per-equipment-item record requirement, and the inspection-due tracker plus the recall cross-reference are the same deterministic "compute the next-due date from the cycle / match the record against the published list" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a public-safety inspection trade most small companies currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between an inspector's credential status and a site's surfacing-retest currency.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the inspection-cycle tracker, the CPSI certification gate, the surfacing retest scheduler, and the recall cross-reference alert; land the first 5-8 companies through National Recreation and Park Association (NRPA) CPSI member directory outreach.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — NRPA's CPSI credential-holder directory and the National Program for Playground Safety (NPPS) member network, where small inspection companies already juggle multi-site district contracts, staggered CPSI renewal dates across several inspectors, and surfacing-retest scheduling with no purpose-built compliance tool.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $10/mo per site under contract. Average client (15 sites) = $199 + $150 = $349/mo. 26-32 companies × $349/mo = **$9,074-11,168/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every contracted site carries its own recurring inspection-due clock, every inspector's CPSI credential renews on its own independent 3-year cycle, and every surfacing installation opens its own impact-attenuation retest schedule — a continuous, per-site and per-inspector compliance stream that grows with every new district or municipal contract won.

**Biggest risk:** An inspection performed by a lapsed CPSI, a site overdue on its surfacing impact-attenuation retest, or a client site running equipment under an active CPSC recall the report failed to flag is exactly the finding an insurance-carrier audit or a post-injury liability claim is built to catch — the certification gate, the retest scheduler, and the recall cross-reference are the core protections.

**Growth path:** Side project → inspection-cycle tracker, CPSI gate, surfacing-retest scheduler, and recall alert deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through NRPA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 26-32 companies, pushing past $9.07-11.17K/mo.

### 3. Fats, Oil & Grease (FOG) Hauling & Grease Trap Pumping Company Multi-Municipality Pretreatment Permit & Manifest Compliance

**Problem:** Fats, oil & grease (FOG) hauling companies that pump grease traps and interceptors for commercial kitchens operate under each municipality's local sewer authority pretreatment program, administered under the Clean Water Act's federal pretreatment regulations (40 CFR Part 403) — a hauler working across a multi-city or multi-county metro area typically must hold a separate hauler permit issued by each individual sewer authority it services, since pretreatment programs are administered locally rather than under a single statewide license, and operating in a jurisdiction without that specific municipality's current hauler permit on file is exactly the finding a sewer authority's routine hauler-compliance audit targets, with penalties that can include permit revocation across that municipality. Every pump-out must be documented on a manifest recording the volume collected and confirming disposal at an approved FOG receiving/processing facility — disposing of collected grease into a storm drain or an unauthorized sanitary sewer connection is a Clean Water Act illegal-discharge violation that many municipalities actively investigate through Illicit Discharge Detection and Elimination (IDDE) programs, and a hauler unable to produce a complete manifest trail for a specific pump-out is exactly the gap that investigation is built to expose. Each restaurant client's grease trap or interceptor also has its own required pump-out interval — driven by trap size and the establishment's grease volume, and set by that municipality's pretreatment ordinance, commonly ranging from monthly to quarterly — and a hauler managing 80-150 restaurant accounts across several jurisdictions' differing permit and pump-interval rules can't reliably track which client is coming due, or which municipality's permit is up for its own separate renewal, without a dedicated system.

**What to build:** A per-municipality hauler-permit renewal tracker managing each jurisdiction's separate permit expiration date; a per-client grease-trap pump-out interval scheduler computing each restaurant account's next-due date from trap size and that municipality's ordinance-driven interval, generating an optimized dispatch route; and a manifest/disposal-documentation generator producing the volume-and-destination manifest each municipality's pretreatment program requires per pump-out.

**Skill fit:** Local sewer-authority pretreatment ordinances and hauler-permit renewal cycles are public, structured municipal regulatory data with a well-defined per-jurisdiction and per-client record requirement, and the permit-renewal tracker plus the manifest generator are the same deterministic "compute the deadline from the jurisdiction's cycle / generate the required documentation from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a wastewater-adjacent hauling trade most small FOG companies currently track on a paper route sheet with no systematic cross-check between a client's pump-out interval and the specific municipality's permit currency.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the multi-municipality permit tracker, the per-client pump-out scheduler with dispatch routing, and the manifest generator; land the first 5-8 haulers through National Association of Wastewater Transporters (NAWT) FOG Hauler Certification Program member outreach.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Association of Wastewater Transporters (NAWT) FOG Hauler Certification Program directory and state Water Environment Association pretreatment/FOG-coordinator meetings, where small hauling companies already juggle separate permits across every municipality they serve with no purpose-built compliance tool.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199/mo base + $3/mo per restaurant client account. Average client (120 accounts) = $199 + $360 = $559/mo. 16-20 haulers × $559/mo = **$8,944-11,180/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every serviced municipality carries its own independent permit-renewal clock, every restaurant account's grease trap generates a recurring pump-out due date tied to that jurisdiction's ordinance, and every pump-out opens a fresh manifest-documentation requirement — a continuous, per-jurisdiction and per-client compliance stream that grows with every new city a hauler expands into.

**Biggest risk:** A hauler operating in a municipality without that jurisdiction's current permit on file, or unable to produce a complete manifest trail after an IDDE illegal-discharge investigation, is exactly the finding a sewer-authority compliance audit is built to catch — the multi-municipality permit tracker and the manifest generator are the core protections.

**Growth path:** Side project → permit tracker, pump-out scheduler, and manifest generator deployed, first 5-8 hauler retainers through NAWT outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 haulers. Replaces-full-time-salary → 16-20 haulers, pushing past $8.94-11.18K/mo.

### 4. Timber Harvesting & Logging Company State Forest Practices Act Notification, Riparian Buffer & Reforestation Compliance

**Problem:** Commercial timber harvesting and logging companies operating on private forested tracts must file a state Forest Practices Act notification or harvest plan (such as Washington DNR's Forest Practices Application, Oregon ODF's Notification of Operation, or California's CAL FIRE-approved Timber Harvesting Plan) before any cutting begins, specifying harvest-unit boundaries and confirming the operation will meet the state's riparian buffer requirements — the no-cut or limited-cut zone width along streams, which varies by the stream's fish-bearing classification and the harvest method used — and dispatching a crew to a site before that state-specific plan is filed and any mandatory waiting period has elapsed is exactly the finding a state forest-practices field inspector targets on a site visit, an issue that can suspend the company's harvest-operation registration entirely. Every active harvest unit must be operated under the state's required best-management-practices for erosion control and water-quality protection (tied to Clean Water Act Section 319 nonpoint-source rules), and in many states individual fallers or equipment operators working state or certified timberland must hold a current logger-certification credential (through American Loggers Council-affiliated state programs, such as the Northeast Master Logger Program or Associated California Loggers) renewed through periodic training — a crew member operating without a current logger certification on a certified tract is a finding that can jeopardize both the operator's own registration and the landowner's forest certification. Most states also require a post-harvest compliance report — documenting slash disposal and, in states like California, that the site meets a reforestation stocking standard — filed within a set number of years after harvest completion, and a company running several concurrent harvest units across a season manages per-unit plan filings, riparian buffer compliance, per-crew-member certification currency, and post-harvest reforestation deadlines with no dedicated compliance function.

**What to build:** A per-job harvest-plan/notification filing tracker with a hard block preventing a crew from being dispatched to a site until that state's plan is filed and any mandatory waiting period has elapsed; a riparian buffer and BMP compliance checklist per harvest unit mapping the required buffer width to each stream segment's classification; a per-crew-member logger-certification currency tracker; and a post-harvest compliance report generator tracking each unit's slash-disposal and reforestation-stocking filing deadline.

**Skill fit:** State Forest Practices Act notification requirements, riparian buffer classification rules, and logger-certification renewal cycles are public, structured state regulatory data with a well-defined per-unit and per-crew-member record requirement, and the plan-filing gate plus the post-harvest deadline tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / compute the filing deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a forestry trade most small logging companies currently track on a paper plan-filing folder with no systematic cross-check between a harvest unit's plan status and its post-harvest reporting deadline.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the harvest-plan filing tracker with the dispatch gate, the riparian buffer/BMP checklist, the crew-certification tracker, and the post-harvest report generator; land the first 5-8 companies through American Loggers Council-affiliated state logging association outreach.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Loggers Council-affiliated state logging associations (such as Associated California Loggers and the Washington Contract Loggers Association) and regional logger-certification program member meetings, where small logging company owners already navigate state-specific harvest-plan filings and staggered crew-certification renewal with no dedicated compliance staff.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base (up to 6 concurrent harvest units) + $25/mo per additional active unit. Average client (8 units) = $299 + $50 = $349/mo. 26-32 companies × $349/mo = **$9,074-11,168/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new harvest unit opens its own plan-filing and riparian-buffer compliance cycle, every crew member's logger certification renews on its own recurring schedule, and every completed harvest opens a fresh post-harvest reforestation-reporting deadline — a continuous, per-unit and per-crew compliance stream tied directly to how much acreage the company is actively cutting.

**Biggest risk:** A crew dispatched before a state harvest plan is filed, a harvest unit cut without the required riparian buffer, or a post-harvest reforestation report filed late is exactly the finding a state forest-practices field inspection is built to catch — the dispatch gate, the buffer checklist, and the post-harvest deadline tracker are the core protections.

**Growth path:** Side project → plan-filing tracker with dispatch gate, buffer checklist, and post-harvest report generator deployed, first 5-8 company retainers through state logging-association outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 16-20 companies. Replaces-full-time-salary → 26-32 companies, pushing past $9.07-11.17K/mo.

### 5. Sand, Gravel & Aggregate Quarry Operator MSHA Part 56 Training, Citation-Abatement & State Reclamation Compliance

**Problem:** Sand, gravel, and aggregate quarry operators are subject to MSHA's Part 56 surface metal/nonmetal mine safety standards and MSHA's own routine inspection and citation program, separate from and in addition to general OSHA jurisdiction — every employee working at or around the pit must complete Part 46 new-miner training before starting work and an annual refresher thereafter, and a worker sent to the active pit face without current, on-file Part 46 training is exactly the finding an MSHA inspector targets first on any site visit, a citation that can shut down the affected work area on the spot. Every MSHA citation carries its own abatement deadline, and a citation left unaddressed past that deadline escalates to a withdrawal order that halts operation of the cited equipment or area entirely until the hazard is corrected and verified — a quarry operator tracking citations by memory or a shared inbox is exactly the gap an escalating citation exploits, since the consequence compounds the longer it goes unaddressed. Separately, most quarries operate under a state mining/reclamation permit requiring an approved reclamation plan and a bond sized to the acreage actively disturbed, with an annual report reconciling disturbed acreage against the permitted limit and a scheduled bond-release inspection once reclamation milestones are met — a quarry operating beyond its permitted disturbed-acreage limit, or missing its annual reclamation report, is exactly the finding a state mining inspector or a bond-renewal review targets, and can block the operator from expanding into its next permitted phase. A small quarry running one or two active pits and a crushing/screening plant manages per-employee MSHA training currency, per-citation abatement-deadline tracking, and reclamation-acreage/bond reporting with no dedicated compliance function.

**What to build:** A per-employee MSHA Part 46/48 training-currency tracker with a "cleared to work at the active pit face" gate blocking assignment once a worker's new-miner or annual-refresher training lapses; a citation and abatement-deadline tracker flagging every open MSHA citation against its abatement due date before it escalates to a withdrawal order; and a reclamation-acreage and bond-compliance tracker reconciling actively disturbed acreage against the state permit's limit and the annual reclamation-report filing deadline.

**Skill fit:** MSHA's Part 46/48 training-cycle rules and public citation database, and state mining reclamation-permit reporting requirements, are structured federal and state regulatory data with a well-defined per-employee and per-citation record requirement, and the work-eligibility gate plus the abatement-deadline tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears / compute the deadline from the trigger event" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a surface-mining trade most small quarry operators currently track on a paper training binder and a mental sense of which citations are still open.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the training-currency tracker with the work-eligibility gate, the citation/abatement-deadline tracker, and the reclamation-acreage/bond compliance tracker; land the first 5-8 quarries through National Stone, Sand & Gravel Association (NSSGA) state-affiliate member outreach.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — National Stone, Sand & Gravel Association (NSSGA) state-affiliate member directories and regional aggregate-producer association meetings, where small quarry operators already juggle MSHA citation abatement deadlines and state reclamation-bond reporting with no dedicated compliance staff.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base + $8/mo per employee tracked. Average client (25 employees) = $349 + $200 = $549/mo. 16-20 quarries × $549/mo = **$8,784-10,980/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every employee's MSHA training recurs on its own independent annual cycle, every citation opens its own abatement-deadline clock the moment it's issued, and every reporting year generates a fresh reclamation-acreage reconciliation against the permit's limit — a continuous, per-employee and per-citation compliance stream tied directly to the quarry's active workforce and inspection history.

**Biggest risk:** An employee working the pit face on lapsed Part 46 training, a citation left open past its abatement deadline until it escalates to a withdrawal order, or disturbed acreage exceeding the state permit's limit is exactly the finding an MSHA inspection or a state reclamation-bond review is built to catch — the work-eligibility gate, the abatement-deadline tracker, and the reclamation-acreage tracker are the core protections.

**Growth path:** Side project → training-currency gate, citation-abatement tracker, and reclamation-acreage tracker deployed, first 5-8 quarry retainers through NSSGA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 10-13 quarries. Replaces-full-time-salary → 16-20 quarries, pushing past $8.78-10.98K/mo.
