### 1. FCC-Licensed Low Power FM / Class A Television Broadcast Station Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Roughly 4,000+ Low Power FM (LPFM) stations and several hundred Class A television stations operate under FCC licenses (47 CFR Part 73) held mostly by nonprofits, churches, community groups, and small family broadcasters with no compliance staff — often a volunteer or part-time station manager. The FCC requires an online Public Inspection File kept current (ownership reports, political file, EEO materials), a Quarterly Issues/Programs List filed every three months describing how the station served community needs, an annual EEO Public File Report for stations with five or more full-time employees, biennial ownership reports (Form 323/323-E), weekly and monthly Emergency Alert System test logging, antenna structure registration (ASR) renewal and lighting-inspection logs, and license renewal itself on an 8-year cycle (Form 2100, Schedule 303-S or 302-DTV) filed within a specific pre-expiration window. Missing the Quarterly Issues/Programs List or letting the public file lapse is one of the most common bases for FCC complaints and fines against small stations, and a station that misses its renewal window can go dark. None of this requires a lawyer to track — it requires a calendar, a document checklist, and reminders — but almost no small station has built one.
**What to build:** A per-station compliance dashboard with: 8-year license renewal countdown with the correct pre-filing window flagged; quarterly issues/programs list builder (structured entry, PDF export, auto-dated for filing); EEO Public File Report annual deadline (keyed to station's anniversary date); public inspection file document checklist with upload/versioning; weekly/monthly EAS test log; ASR tower registration renewal and required FAA lighting-inspection log; biennial ownership report reminder.
**Skill fit:** The FCC publishes station license, ASR, and ownership data in structured databases (CDBS, LMS, ASR registry) that a developer can pull to pre-populate a station's compliance calendar automatically from its call sign — a genuine data-engineering edge over a manual spreadsheet competitor.
**MVP scope:** Build the renewal countdown, quarterly issues/programs builder, and public file checklist for one station type (LPFM first, since it's the largest population and most volunteer-run). Get first 5-8 clients from LPFM station managers directly.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — REC Networks, the leading LPFM advocacy and technical-assistance organization, runs an active online community and newsletter reaching thousands of LPFM licensees; a free compliance-calendar tool posted there with a paid tier converts station managers who already trust REC's guidance.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $150-300/mo per station. 50 stations × $200/mo avg = $10,000/mo. There are enough LPFM + small Class A stations nationally (4,000+ LPFM alone) that 50-80 paying stations is a realistic niche penetration, not a stretch.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** The 8-year renewal cycle and quarterly filings are non-optional federal obligations tied to the station's right to broadcast at all — a station that churns off the tool still has the same filings due next quarter, but a station that stays subscribed never has to think about the deadline again, which is a strong retention driver once trust is established.
**Biggest risk:** Volunteer-run LPFM stations are often cash-poor and price-sensitive. Mitigate by targeting Class A TV stations and larger LPFM licensees (church networks, university stations) first at a higher price point, and treat small single-frequency LPFMs as a lower-tier add-on rather than the core revenue base.
**Growth path:** Side project → 15 stations ($3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 40 stations ($8K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 60-80 stations plus a compliance-consulting upsell for renewal filings.

### 2. Architecture Firm Multi-State Licensure, NCARB Certification & Corporate Practice Registration Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** A small architecture firm with 3-15 licensed architects working across state lines (common once a firm does even a handful of out-of-state projects) has to track individual state license renewals (cycles and CE-hour requirements vary by state — some require specific HSW, health-safety-welfare, hours, not generic CE), each architect's NCARB Certificate maintenance (used for reciprocal licensure and increasingly required by clients/insurers), and — separately from individual licenses — the firm's own Certificate of Authorization or Certificate of Practice, which most states require before the *firm itself* (not just its individual architects) can stamp drawings or contract for services in that state. A firm expanding into a new state has to register the firm entity, not just wait for an architect to get personally licensed there, and missing this is a real liability exposure if a project gets challenged. Firms currently track this in spreadsheets maintained by an office manager or the principal architect personally, and renewal lapses are discovered when a state board rejects a permit submission.
**What to build:** Per-architect license portfolio across all states they're registered in, with renewal dates and state-specific CE/HSW-hour requirements tracked against actual completed CE (uploadable certificates); NCARB Certificate status and record-maintenance reminders; firm-level Certificate of Authorization/Practice tracker per state the firm operates in, with renewal deadlines and required officer-of-record designations; a "can we legally stamp this project in State X" eligibility check before a firm takes on out-of-state work.
**Skill fit:** State architecture board license-lookup data (most boards have public license-verification databases) can be scraped/synced to auto-verify renewal status and flag discrepancies — a real data problem, not just a form-filling one.
**MVP scope:** Build the individual license tracker with CE-hour requirements for the 10 most common practice states, plus the firm Certificate of Authorization tracker. Get first 5-8 firms from a state AIA (American Institute of Architects) chapter's small-firm roundtable.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — AIA has active small-firm/emerging-professional committees at nearly every state chapter, and firm principals attend these specifically to solve back-office problems like this one.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $150-250/mo per firm depending on architect count and number of states. 60 firms × $180/mo avg = $10,800/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** License renewal cycles (1-3 years depending on state) and NCARB record maintenance are recurring, non-optional events for every practicing architect at the firm, and the firm's own Certificate of Authorization renewal is a hard gate on the firm's ability to legally operate in a state — both create durable multi-year usage.
**Biggest risk:** Firms that only practice in their home state have no multi-state pain point and won't pay. Mitigate by qualifying leads for at least 2-state practice before pitching, and by bundling in single-state CE-hour tracking as a lower-tier offer to capture firms before they expand.
**Growth path:** Side project → 15 firms ($2.7K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 45 firms ($8.1K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 60-70 firms plus a per-state expansion consulting add-on.

### 3. USCG-Inspected Small Passenger Vessel (Subchapter T) Ferry, Tour Boat & Water Taxi Operator Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Small passenger vessel operators — harbor tour boats, dinner cruise operators, water taxis, small ferries, whale-watching boats — carrying more than 6 passengers for hire operate under 46 CFR Subchapter T and must hold a Coast Guard Certificate of Inspection (COI), renewed on a roughly 5-year cycle with periodic (often annual) exams in between. Unlike the uninspected 6-pack charter boats already common in this space, Subchapter T operators must maintain and log monthly fire and abandon-ship drills, keep lifesaving and firefighting equipment inspection records current (fire extinguisher hydrostatic test dates, life jacket condition checks, EPIRB battery/registration status), track each master's and mate's Merchant Mariner Credential (MMC) renewal and required physical/drug-test currency, and participate in a USCG- or DOT-compliant random drug-testing consortium program with quarterly testing-rate documentation. A small operator running 2-4 vessels typically has no dedicated compliance officer — the owner-operator tracks it on paper checklists in the wheelhouse, and a lapsed drill log or an out-of-date COI discovered during a Coast Guard spot-check can tie up a vessel during peak season, which is existential for a seasonal tourism business.
**What to build:** Per-vessel COI renewal countdown with periodic exam scheduling; monthly drill log (fire/abandon-ship) with digital sign-off by crew; lifesaving/firefighting equipment inspection checklist with hydrostatic-test and expiration tracking (fire extinguishers, life jackets, EPIRBs, flares); per-crew-member MMC expiration and medical-certificate tracker; drug-testing consortium participation log with quarterly rate reporting reminders.
**Skill fit:** This is fundamentally a scheduling/checklist/reminder engineering problem layered on well-defined federal inspection intervals — exactly the kind of structured, rules-driven tracker a software background is suited to build quickly and correctly.
**MVP scope:** Build the COI countdown, drill log, and equipment inspection checklist for a single vessel first. Get first 5-8 operators from the Passenger Vessel Association.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Passenger Vessel Association (PVA) is the dedicated national trade association for exactly this operator type, runs an annual conference and active member forums, and its members are small owner-operator businesses that feel this pain directly.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $150-350/mo per operator (scaling with fleet size). 45 operators × $220/mo avg = $9,900/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Monthly drills, periodic COI exams, and quarterly drug-testing reporting are recurring events baked into the federal inspection regime for as long as the vessel operates — this isn't a one-time filing but an ongoing operational cadence that keeps the tool in daily/weekly use.
**Biggest risk:** Seasonal tourism operators may want to pause subscriptions in the off-season. Mitigate with an annual pricing option at a discount to monthly, and position winter months as maintenance/COI-exam-prep season where the tool is still actively used for equipment inspection planning.
**Growth path:** Side project → 12 operators ($2.6K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 35 operators ($7.7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-55 operators plus a fleet-expansion tier for multi-vessel operators.

### 4. Freestanding Urgent Care Clinic State Licensure, CLIA Waiver & CMS Enrollment Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** The freestanding urgent care industry (over 11,000 clinics nationally, many independently owned single- or multi-site operations) sits at the intersection of several regulatory tracks that a small clinic's office manager has to juggle without dedicated compliance staff: state ambulatory care/clinic facility licensure (renewal cycles and inspection requirements vary by state), a CLIA certificate for the point-of-care lab testing urgent care depends on (strep, flu, UA, glucose — most run under a CLIA Certificate of Waiver, but any complexity creep into moderate-complexity testing changes the compliance burden entirely), CMS enrollment and revalidation (Form 855B, on a roughly 5-year PECOS revalidation cycle) to bill Medicare, DEA registration renewal for any physician/NP/PA who prescribes controlled substances on-site, state x-ray machine registration and operator certification if the clinic does onsite imaging, and OSHA bloodborne pathogen program documentation. Each employed provider's own state medical/NP/PA license and DEA number also has to be tracked separately so the clinic never has someone seeing patients or prescribing on a lapsed credential. A single-site clinic that misses its CLIA renewal or a provider's DEA lapse can be blocked from testing or prescribing mid-shift, which is a direct revenue and liability hit in a walk-in-volume business model.
**What to build:** State facility license and CLIA certificate renewal tracker with inspection-readiness checklists; CMS PECOS revalidation countdown; per-provider credential tracker (state license, DEA registration, board certification) with expiration alerts tied to shift scheduling so an expiring credential is flagged before it lapses on an active provider; x-ray registration and operator-certification tracker for clinics with onsite imaging; OSHA bloodborne pathogen training log.
**Skill fit:** State medical board and CMS PECOS data can be synced to auto-verify provider license and enrollment status rather than relying on manual entry — a genuine data-integration edge for a developer over a generic compliance-checklist competitor.
**MVP scope:** Build the CLIA/facility license tracker and per-provider credential tracker first, since those are universal to every urgent care regardless of imaging capability. Get first 5-8 clinics from a state urgent care association.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Urgent Care Association (UCA) and several active state-level urgent care associations run regular member meetings specifically for independent and small-group operators (as distinct from hospital-owned chains), making them a direct channel to the target buyer.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $200-400/mo per clinic. 40 clinics × $275/mo avg = $11,000/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** CMS revalidation, CLIA renewal, and rolling provider-credential expirations are recurring, non-optional events throughout the life of the clinic, and a multi-provider clinic has several credentials in motion at any given time, which keeps the tool in continuous active use rather than a once-a-year checklist.
**Biggest risk:** Larger regional urgent care chains have in-house credentialing staff and won't be the target buyer. Mitigate by explicitly targeting independent 1-5 location operators, where the tool replaces a manual spreadsheet rather than competing with an internal compliance department.
**Growth path:** Side project → 12 clinics ($3.3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 30 clinics ($8.25K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 40-50 clinics plus a provider-credentialing-service upsell.

### 5. Insurance Premium Finance Company State Licensing & Notice-of-Cancellation Compliance Tracker
**Problem:** Insurance premium finance companies — small lenders that front commercial and personal insurance premiums so policyholders can pay in installments rather than in full up front — are licensed under state insurance codes (not banking/consumer-lending codes) in most states that regulate the activity, and the licensing and compliance burden multiplies fast for any premium finance company operating in more than a handful of states. Each state sets its own premium finance company license renewal cycle and surety bond requirement, its own cap on the finance charge/interest rate the company can charge, and — critically — its own statutory notice-of-cancellation timeline: when a policyholder misses a payment, the premium finance company has the contractual power to instruct the insurer to cancel the underlying policy, but only after sending a state-specific minimum-notice-period cancellation warning (commonly 10-15 days, with wording and delivery-method requirements that vary by state) and, in many states, a specific waiting period before the actual cancellation request goes to the carrier. Getting this notice timeline wrong exposes the company to bad-faith cancellation claims and regulatory fines, and a company financing premiums in 15-20 states is tracking 15-20 different rule sets by hand, usually in a compliance binder maintained by one person.
**What to build:** Multi-state license and surety bond renewal tracker; a state-by-state notice-of-cancellation rule engine that calculates the correct notice date and required wording/delivery method for a given policyholder's state when a payment is missed; interest-rate/finance-charge cap reference table flagged against the company's actual pricing by state; annual report filing calendar per state.
**Skill fit:** This is a rules-engine and reference-data problem — encoding 40+ states' premium finance statutes into a lookup table that outputs the correct cancellation notice date and language is exactly the kind of structured data work a developer can build once and maintain incrementally, and it's a genuine moat since assembling that rule set correctly the first time is the hard part.
**MVP scope:** Build the license/bond renewal tracker and the notice-of-cancellation rule engine for the 15 most commonly licensed states first. Get first 5-8 clients from a premium finance trade association or insurance agency network that owns a premium finance arm.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Automotive Finance Association and, more directly, groups like the National Association of Premium Finance Companies (or the premium finance sections within regional insurance associations) give direct access to compliance officers at small premium finance companies who currently manage this in spreadsheets.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $200-450/mo per company depending on number of states licensed. 30 companies × $300/mo avg = $9,000/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every missed payment across every policy the company finances triggers a fresh notice-of-cancellation calculation, and every state license carries its own renewal — this generates continuous, high-frequency usage rather than a once-a-year filing, which is a strong daily-active-use driver for retention.
**Biggest risk:** Larger premium finance companies (bank-affiliated, e.g., AFCO/Flatiron-scale) already have in-house legal/compliance systems and aren't the buyer. Mitigate by explicitly targeting independent regional premium finance companies and captive premium finance arms of mid-size insurance agencies, which are underserved by enterprise compliance software.
**Growth path:** Side project → 10 companies ($3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 25 companies ($7.5K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 30-40 companies plus a per-state expansion advisory upsell.

## This Session's Pick: #4 (Urgent Care) for the strongest combination of market size and pricing power; #1 (Broadcast) for the cleanest, most defensible data moat

Urgent care wins on raw numbers — over 11,000 clinics nationally, a well-organized trade association (UCA) with active independent-operator chapters, and a pricing ceiling ($200-400/mo) that reflects real healthcare-compliance stakes (CMS revalidation, CLIA, controlled-substance prescribing). It also has the most natural expansion path: once a clinic trusts the tool for credential tracking, a provider-credentialing service upsell is a short reach.

Broadcast station compliance is the most interesting from a builder's-edge perspective: the FCC's public license, ownership, and tower-registration databases are structured and scrapeable, meaning a developer can auto-populate a station's entire compliance calendar from its call sign alone — a feature a non-technical competitor building the same tool by hand simply can't match. It's a smaller addressable market than urgent care, but REC Networks gives a uniquely direct, trust-already-established channel to thousands of LPFM licensees who have nowhere else to go for this.
