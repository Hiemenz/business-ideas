### 1. Licensed Plumbing Contractor Multi-Jurisdiction License, Local Competency-Card & Permit/Inspection Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Independent plumbing contractors working across county or city lines hold a state plumbing license (issued by a dedicated board such as the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners or North Carolina's Board of Examiners of Plumbing, Heating and Fire Sprinkler Contractors) that must be renewed on a fixed cycle with continuing-education hours, but the state license is frequently only half the picture: many counties and cities layer their own local competency card or certificate on top of the state license (as in most Florida counties under F.S. 489), and a contractor who lets either the state license or a specific county's local card lapse can't legally pull a permit in that jurisdiction even though every other credential is current. Every single job also needs its own local building permit and must pass rough-in and final inspection against whichever plumbing code edition — Uniform Plumbing Code or International Plumbing Code, each revised on its own three-year cycle — that specific jurisdiction has adopted, so a contractor running crews across three adjoining counties can be inspected against three different code editions in the same week, and showing up with fixtures or venting sized to the wrong edition's requirements is a failed inspection and a rescheduled crew. A contractor running several concurrent jobs across a metro area currently tracks state license status, per-county competency cards, and which code edition governs each job site on a shop-office wall calendar and a filing cabinet of permit stubs, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific job's jurisdiction, code edition, and contractor credentials are all simultaneously in order before a crew is dispatched.

**What to build:** A dual-tier license tracker covering the state plumbing license plus every county/city competency card the contractor holds, with a renewal and CE-hour countdown for each; a per-jurisdiction code-edition reference flagging which UPC/IPC edition and local amendments currently apply at any job site the contractor works; and a per-job permit-and-inspection tracker logging rough-in and final inspection status with a correction-notice log.

**Skill fit:** State plumbing-board renewal cycles, county competency-card rosters, and jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction code-adoption dates are public, structured regulatory data, and the per-jurisdiction code-edition cross-reference is the same deterministic "know which rule applies before you show up" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a licensed-trade niche entirely distinct from the generic Multi-State Specialty Contractor License Portfolio tool already in this repo, which tracks license and bond renewal across many trades but has no per-job permit, inspection, or code-edition logic at all.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the dual-tier license/CE tracker, the jurisdiction code-edition reference, and the per-job permit/inspection tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent plumbing contractors working multi-county metro service areas.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — PHCC (Plumbing-Heating-Cooling Contractors Association) national and state chapter meetings and IAPMO continuing-education course rosters reach exactly the small contractors currently reconciling multi-jurisdiction license and code-edition status on a wall calendar with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $129-249/mo per contractor depending on number of jurisdictions and crew size. 45-60 contractors × $190/mo avg = $8,550-11,400/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every jurisdiction a contractor expands into reopens the local-competency-card question, every code-cycle adoption date resets the applicable-edition question for every job in that jurisdiction simultaneously, and every job pulled generates its own permit-and-inspection event regardless of how routine the work — a continuous, jurisdiction-and-job-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the contractor's own service-area growth.

**Biggest risk:** A contractor working a single stable jurisdiction with a long-current license has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward contractors expanding across county lines or running multiple crews, where the multi-jurisdiction card-and-code-edition load genuinely outgrows a wall calendar.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 contractors ($2.9K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 30 contractors ($5.7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 contractors plus a jurisdiction-ready permit-and-inspection export a contractor can hand a building inspector on the spot.

### 3. Private Probation / Misdemeanor Community-Corrections Company State Supervision-Provider, Ability-to-Pay Screening & Court-Reporting Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Private companies contracted by municipal and county courts to supervise misdemeanor probationers — authorized under statutes such as Georgia's O.C.G.A. §42-8-100 et seq., with similar frameworks in Tennessee, Mississippi, Alabama, Missouri, and Utah — must register or certify with the state agency overseeing community supervision, background-check and train every individual probation officer before that officer supervises a single case, and file a monthly caseload-and-violation status report back to each contracting court, and a missed monthly report or an officer placed on a caseload before their background check clears is exactly the finding a court's own contract-compliance audit is built to catch, with contract termination a real consequence. The mechanism that carries the most legal exposure sits underneath the fee structure itself: the statutes and the federal consent decrees that followed cases like *Rodriguez v. Providence Community Corrections* require the company to run a documented ability-to-pay (indigency) screening before charging supervision fees and to cap what it collects under a published fee schedule, and a probationer charged a fee without a documented screening on file — or billed above the statutory cap — is exactly the pattern that has triggered class-action litigation and state-level bans against private-probation companies in multiple jurisdictions. A company running a caseload across several contracted courts currently tracks officer clearance status, per-client ability-to-pay documentation, and each court's monthly reporting deadline on a caseworker spreadsheet and a stack of paper intake forms, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific client's screening is on file and a specific court's report is filed before that court's deadline passes.

**What to build:** A per-officer background-check and training-hour tracker with a "cleared to supervise" gate blocking any caseload assignment until clearance is confirmed; a per-client ability-to-pay/indigency screening log tied to the state's statutory fee schedule, flagging any account approaching or exceeding the fee cap before it's billed; and a per-contracted-court monthly reporting-deadline tracker generating the required caseload/violation status export.

**Skill fit:** State community-corrections statutes, published fee-cap schedules, and each contracted court's reporting cadence are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-officer and per-client triggers, and the ability-to-pay screening gate is the same deterministic "confirm the safeguard is documented before relying on it" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a criminal-justice-supervision niche entirely distinct from the Bail Bond Agency and Immigration Bond Agent ideas already in this repo, neither of which supervises a post-conviction caseload or runs an indigency-screening obligation of any kind.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the officer-clearance gate, the ability-to-pay screening log with fee-cap flagging, and the per-court reporting-deadline tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small private-probation companies contracted with municipal and county courts.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Probation and Parole Association's (APPA) member directory and state-level community-corrections association meetings reach exactly the small company operators currently reconciling officer clearance and fee-cap documentation on a spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per company depending on caseload size and number of contracted courts. 20-26 companies × $420/mo avg = $8,400-10,920/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new probationer intake reopens its own ability-to-pay screening question, every contracted court's monthly reporting deadline recurs on its own independent clock, and every new officer hired to handle caseload growth reopens the clearance question — a continuous, caseload-and-court-count-driven compliance stream tied directly to the company's own contract growth.

**Biggest risk:** A company holding a single small-court contract with a stable, low caseload has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need, and the underlying industry faces real reform and litigation pressure in several states. Mitigate by qualifying leads toward companies holding multiple court contracts with larger caseloads, where the screening-and-reporting load genuinely outgrows a spreadsheet, and by positioning the documented ability-to-pay trail as the company's own defense against the litigation risk the industry faces.

### 4. USDA-Certified Organic Farm & Processor National Organic Program Organic-System-Plan, Input-Approval & Audit-Trail Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Certified organic farms and processors operate under 7 CFR Part 205 (the National Organic Program), maintaining an Organic System Plan that a USDA-accredited certifying agent — CCOF, Oregon Tilth, QAI, or a similar ACA — reviews and renews during a mandatory annual on-site inspection, and a lapsed annual update or an inspection that finds the operation's actual practices have drifted from its filed plan is exactly the finding that can suspend certification mid-season. The mechanism with the sharpest financial edge is the input-approval requirement: every new fertilizer, pest-control product, or feed additive must be cross-checked against the National List of Allowed and Prohibited Substances (7 CFR §205.601-606) — commonly verified through an OMRI (Organic Materials Review Institute) listing — before it's ever applied, because a single prohibited-substance application isn't a warning, it's decertification of that specific field for a multi-year period during which nothing harvested from it can be sold as organic at the price premium that justifies the whole operation. Underneath both of those sits a five-year audit-trail requirement tying every unit sold as organic back to a specific certified field, harvest date, and input record, and the USDA's 2024 Strengthening Organic Enforcement rule layered new supply-chain traceability and import-certificate obligations on top, increasing the documentation an inspector expects to see on demand. An operation running several fields or a small processing line currently tracks its Organic System Plan status, input approvals, and lot-level traceability on a farm-office binder and a filing cabinet of input labels, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific input is actually OMRI-listed before a crew applies it, or that a specific sale's traceability record is complete before the annual inspection.

**What to build:** An Organic System Plan renewal tracker counting down to the certifying agent's annual review deadline; a per-input OMRI-list cross-check gate blocking any new fertilizer, pesticide, or feed additive from being logged as applied until its approved status is confirmed; and a five-year lot-traceability log tying every organic sale back to its specific field, harvest date, and input record for the annual inspection and any NOP audit.

**Skill fit:** The National List of Allowed and Prohibited Substances, OMRI's published product listings, and each certifying agent's annual-review cycle are public, structured regulatory and accrediting-body data with a concrete per-input approval trigger, and the input-approval gate is the same deterministic "block the action until the record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a certified-organic niche entirely distinct from the Hemp Product Processor idea already in this repo, which runs through the Farm Bill's THC-potency testing regime rather than 7 CFR Part 205's certifying-agent inspection and input-approval structure.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the Organic System Plan renewal tracker, the OMRI input-approval gate, and the five-year lot-traceability log. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small certified-organic farms and processors ahead of their next annual inspection.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the Organic Trade Association's (OTA) member directory and regional organic-farmer associations such as Marbleseed (formerly MOSES) reach exactly the small certified operations currently reconciling input approvals and lot traceability on a farm-office binder with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $99-199/mo per operation depending on field count and whether processing is included. 70-90 operations × $135/mo avg = $9,450-12,150/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new input a farm wants to use reopens the OMRI-approval question, every harvest and sale generates its own traceability event regardless of how routine the crop, and every annual inspection reopens the full Organic System Plan review — a continuous, input-and-sales-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the operation's own growth.

**Biggest risk:** A very small single-field operation with a stable, narrow input list has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by pricing a lower single-field tier to capture the broader base and by qualifying leads toward operations running multiple fields, seasonal input changes, or a processing line, where the approval-and-traceability load genuinely outgrows a binder.

**Growth path:** Side project → 20 operations ($2.7K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 40 operations ($5.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 70-90 operations plus an inspection-ready traceability export an operation can hand its certifying agent during the annual on-site review.

### 5. PACA-Licensed Produce Dealer, Broker & Commission Merchant USDA License, Trust-Preservation & Prompt-Payment Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Independent produce dealers, brokers, and commission merchants buying or selling more than 2,000 pounds of fresh or frozen fruits and vegetables in interstate commerce on a given day must hold a license under the Perishable Agricultural Commodities Act (7 U.S.C. §499a et seq.), administered by USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service and renewed annually with a fee scaled to trading volume, and a lapsed license discovered mid-transaction voids the statutory trust protection that makes the whole industry's credit-based trading possible. The Act's sharpest edge is the trust-preservation-and-prompt-payment mechanism: a buyer preserves a seller's PACA trust benefits only by including specific trust-preservation language on every invoice within the statutory timeframe, and a licensee who fails to pay a seller within the prompt-payment window — commonly 10 days unless a longer term is agreed in writing — has committed a PACA violation regardless of intent, and a documented pattern of late payment is exactly what triggers a reparation complaint, license suspension, or revocation. Layered underneath, every officer, owner, or key employee "responsibly connected" to the license must be listed with USDA, and someone barred from the industry under an active employment ban following a prior license revocation can't be hired or promoted into a responsibly-connected role anywhere else — a violation invisible to a dealer's own hiring manager without a name-by-name cross-check against USDA's published ban list before the offer goes out. A dealer running a full book of grower and buyer relationships currently tracks license renewal status, invoice trust-language compliance, and payment-window deadlines on an accounts-payable spreadsheet and a stack of grower contracts, with no systematic cross-check confirming a specific invoice's trust language is correct and a specific payment is inside the statutory window before that account ages into a violation.

**What to build:** A PACA license renewal and fee-tier tracker; a per-invoice trust-preservation language compliance check paired with a prompt-payment countdown flagging any outstanding payable approaching the 10-day (or negotiated) violation threshold; and a "responsibly connected individual" roster cross-checked against USDA's published PACA employment-ban list before any new hire or officer appointment is finalized.

**Skill fit:** USDA AMS's PACA license renewal schedule, the statutory trust-language and prompt-payment rules, and the published employment-ban list are public, structured regulatory data with concrete per-invoice and per-hire triggers, and the payment-window countdown is the same deterministic "flag before the deadline becomes a violation" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a produce-trading niche entirely distinct from the Grain Elevator & Grain Warehouse Operator idea already in this repo, whose compliance core is a state warehouse-bonding statute for a storable commodity rather than USDA AMS's trust-and-prompt-payment licensing regime for perishables.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license renewal tracker, the per-invoice trust-language/prompt-payment countdown, and the responsibly-connected employment-ban cross-check. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to small independent produce dealers and brokers.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the International Fresh Produce Association's (IFPA) member directory and Blue Book Services' produce-industry credit network reach exactly the small dealers and brokers currently reconciling trust-language and payment-window compliance on an accounts-payable spreadsheet with no dedicated compliance system.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $149-299/mo per dealer depending on trading volume and license tier. 45-60 dealers × $210/mo avg = $9,450-12,600/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every invoice issued generates its own trust-language-and-payment-window event regardless of how routine the shipment, every new grower or buyer relationship reopens the responsibly-connected-hiring question as the dealer's staff grows, and every annual license renewal reopens the fee-tier calculation as trading volume changes — a continuous, invoice-and-relationship-volume-driven compliance stream tied directly to the dealer's own trading growth.

**Biggest risk:** A very small dealer trading a narrow, stable set of relationships with reliably prompt payment has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by pricing a lower entry tier to capture the broader base and by qualifying leads toward dealers running a larger, more volatile book of grower and buyer accounts, where the trust-language-and-payment-window tracking genuinely outgrows a spreadsheet.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 dealers ($3.2K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 30 dealers ($6.3K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 dealers plus a reparation-complaint-ready trust-and-payment export a dealer can produce the moment a seller disputes a payment.
