# Main Income Ideas - 2026-08-17 (Session 93)

## Who this is for
A 29-year-old with software engineering and data analytics skills who wants to replace a full-time salary — targeting $8-15K+/mo — within 12-24 months, building solo during evenings and weekends with no outside funding. Every idea below is a monthly-retainer compliance SaaS aimed at small operators in a regulated industry who currently track licenses, filings, and inspection readiness on spreadsheets, paper folders, or nothing at all.

## Ground rules for every idea in this series

- **Realistic path to $8-15K+/mo** — every idea states the math plainly.
- **Time to first dollar < 90 days.**
- **Named distribution channel** — a real professional association, trade board, or industry publication where the target buyer already gathers.
- **No repeats.** This session covers five regulated industries that have not appeared in any prior session: (1) **precious metals & scrap metal dealers** (state secondhand-dealer/scrap-metal recordkeeping and law-enforcement reporting — distinct from pawnbrokers, who deal in consumer loans against collateral, and distinct from rent-to-own dealers covered in v91, who lease rather than buy-and-resell); (2) **money services businesses (check cashers & small money transmitters)** (FinCEN MSB registration and state money-transmitter licensing — distinct from payroll service bureaus covered in v91, which handle employer tax deposits, not third-party funds transmission, and distinct from home warranty providers covered in v91, which are insurance-adjacent but not BSA/AML-regulated); (3) **mold assessment & remediation contractors** (state mold-specific licensing and clearance-testing protocol documentation — distinct from lead/RRP or asbestos abatement, neither of which has appeared in this repo, and distinct from the pest-control or HVAC verticals covered in earlier sessions); (4) **Federal Firearms Licensee (FFL) retail dealers** (ATF Form 4473, bound-book Acquisition & Disposition recordkeeping, and periodic ATF compliance inspections — a federal licensing regime that has not appeared anywhere in this repo, distinct from the shooting-range or hunting-guide verticals if those appear elsewhere); (5) **private investigation agencies** (state PI licensing boards, armed-investigator firearms endorsements, and case-file retention rules — distinct from the process-server, bail-bondsman, or security-guard verticals, none of which has been covered, and distinct from the legal/compliance-adjacent professions like fiduciary guardians covered in v88, who operate under probate courts rather than state PI licensing boards).

### 1. Scrap Metal & Secondhand Precious Metals Dealer Recordkeeping & Law-Enforcement Reporting Compliance
**Problem:** Scrap metal recyclers and secondhand precious-metals/jewelry buyers operate under a patchwork of state metal-recycling-entity statutes (e.g., Texas Occupations Code Chapter 1956, California Business & Professions Code §21625 et seq. for secondhand dealers, Georgia's O.C.G.A. §43-43 for precious metal/gem dealers) plus city and county ordinances layered on top. The near-universal core obligations are: capturing seller photo ID and a legible description of every item purchased, holding purchased items untouched for a statutory period (commonly 10-15 days, sometimes 30) before they can be melted, scrapped, or resold, and submitting daily or near-real-time transaction reports to local law enforcement through systems like LeadsOnline or RAPID (Regional Automated Property Information Database) so stolen-property investigators can flag matches. A dealer who scraps an item before the hold period expires, or who fails to report a transaction within the jurisdiction's window (often 24-48 hours), faces license suspension, per-transaction civil fines that can run into the thousands of dollars, and in the worst case criminal exposure for receiving stolen property because the recordkeeping failure removes the dealer's "good faith" defense. Small yard owners and independent buyers running one or two locations rarely have compliance staff — the counter clerk is usually also the owner, and hold-period tracking across hundreds of small-dollar transactions a week is exactly the kind of bulk-date-arithmetic problem that gets neglected during a busy week.
**What to build:** A per-location transaction ledger where each purchase logs seller ID, item description/weight, purchase price, and timestamp; an automated hold-period countdown that blocks (or flags) any attempt to log an item as scrapped/resold before the statutory date; a daily law-enforcement report generator formatted for the jurisdiction's required submission method; a license and permit renewal calendar (dealer license, scale certification, local business license); and a dashboard the owner can show an inspector on the spot proving every open-hold item is still accounted for.
**Skill fit:** State scrap-metal and secondhand-dealer statutes are public and stable; hold-period and reporting-window rules can be encoded as lookup tables keyed by state/city, which is a straightforward data-modeling problem. The reporting-format piece (matching what local PDs expect from LeadsOnline-style systems) is a structured-data-export problem, not a novel integration.
**MVP scope:** Build the transaction ledger, hold-period countdown, and daily report export for 3-4 target states over 2-3 weekends; onboard the first 5-8 independent scrap yards and jewelry/coin buyers by calling local shops directly and offering a free first month in exchange for a walkthrough call.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the Institute of Scrap Recycling Industries (ISRI) state chapter meetings and the National Pawnbrokers Association's adjacent secondhand-dealer members, plus direct cold outreach to independently owned (non-chain) scrap yards found via state dealer-license registries.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $350/mo × 30 locations = $10,500/mo. Larger multi-scale-yard operators pay more for multi-location rollups: 35 locations blended at $380/mo average = $13,300/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every single purchase transaction creates a new hold-period deadline and a new law-enforcement report obligation — this isn't an annual renewal, it's a daily operational dependency, which drives daily logins and makes the tool sticky enough that owners budget for it like a utility.
**Biggest risk:** Hold-period and reporting-window rules vary by city, not just state, so a generic state-level ruleset could give an owner in a stricter municipality false confidence. Mitigated by letting each account attach a city-specific override to the state default and flagging any location where no override has been confirmed.
**Growth path:** Side project → 10 paying locations ($3.5K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 25 locations ($8.75K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 30-35 locations plus a multi-location enterprise tier for regional recycling chains.

### 2. Money Services Business (Check Casher & Small Money Transmitter) FinCEN & State DFI Compliance
**Problem:** Independent check-cashing storefronts and small money-transmission operators (bill-pay kiosks, informal remittance shops) are Money Services Businesses under FinCEN's definition and must register as an MSB with FinCEN (31 CFR 1022.380), maintain a written BSA/AML compliance program with a designated compliance officer (31 CFR 1022.210), file Currency Transaction Reports for cash transactions over $10,000 (31 CFR 1010.311) and Suspicious Activity Reports for flagged activity (31 CFR 1022.320), and re-register with FinCEN every two years. On top of the federal layer, most states require a money transmitter license obtained through the Nationwide Multistate Licensing System (NMLS), which comes with its own surety bond, minimum net worth, and permissible-investments requirements, plus annual license renewal and often an independent AML program audit. A check-casher operating in multiple states must track a separate license renewal, bond amount, and audit cycle per state, and a missed CTR/SAR filing or lapsed state license triggers FinCEN civil penalties (which start in the tens of thousands of dollars) or state cease-and-desist orders that can shut a storefront down overnight. Owner-operators running one or two locations typically have no dedicated compliance officer — the BSA officer role is bolted onto whoever runs the counter, and the CTR-threshold math across a day's transactions is easy to get wrong by hand.
**What to build:** A per-location BSA/AML program tracker with the required annual independent review scheduled automatically; a CTR-threshold aggregation tool that flags any customer whose same-day cash transactions cross $10,000 (including structuring-adjacent patterns just under the threshold, for the owner's own risk awareness); a SAR case log with filing-deadline countdowns; a multi-state license and bond renewal calendar pulling from NMLS state-specific requirements; and a compliance-officer training/attestation log that satisfies exam documentation requirements.
**Skill fit:** FinCEN's MSB rules and each state's NMLS checklist are published, structured regulatory text — encoding renewal cadences and threshold math is a data-modeling exercise, and the CTR aggregation logic is a straightforward same-day transaction-summing feature well within a data-analytics skill set.
**MVP scope:** Build the CTR aggregator, SAR case log, and single-state license renewal calendar over 2-3 weekends; sign the first 5-8 independent check cashers or remittance operators found through state money-transmitter licensee registries (public records) and direct outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the Financial Service Centers of America (FiSCA), the national trade association for check cashers and money transmitters, plus state-level licensee lists.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $500/mo × 25 locations = $12,500/mo, reflecting the higher price point BSA/AML compliance tools command versus simpler license trackers. A multi-state operator tier at $700/mo × 20 = $14,000/mo is realistic once the product covers 5+ states.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** BSA/AML compliance is a continuous, transaction-triggered obligation (every cash transaction is a potential CTR event) rather than a once-a-year renewal, and a lapsed program is one of the few compliance failures that can end a business overnight via FinCEN enforcement — that urgency keeps retention high.
**Biggest risk:** Getting the CTR/SAR logic wrong could give an operator false confidence on an actual regulatory filing obligation. Mitigated by explicitly positioning the tool as a tracking and documentation aid that surfaces filing deadlines and required program elements, not a substitute for the compliance officer's own filing judgment, stated clearly in onboarding and in-app copy.
**Growth path:** Side project → 8 paying locations ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 18 locations ($9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 25+ locations plus a multi-state enterprise tier.

### 3. Mold Assessment & Remediation Contractor State Licensing & Clearance-Protocol Compliance
**Problem:** States with mold-specific licensing regimes — Florida's Mold-Related Services law (Chapter 468, Part XVI, Florida Statutes), New York's Mold Assessment and Remediation licensing under Labor Law Article 32, and similar frameworks elsewhere — require separate mold assessor and mold remediator licenses, and in states like Florida and New York the same company generally cannot both assess and remediate the same job (a conflict-of-interest firewall meant to stop remediators from inventing problems to fix). Each license carries its own continuing-education hours, liability insurance minimums, and renewal cycle, and every job requires a documented chain: a pre-remediation mold assessment report identifying the scope of contamination, a written remediation protocol specifying containment and removal methods, and a post-remediation clearance test performed by an assessor (not the remediator) before the space can be certified safe to reoccupy. A contractor who skips or mis-sequences any step — remediates without a written protocol, or lets the same license-holder both assess and clear their own remediation work — risks license discipline from the state board and exposes themselves to liability if a homeowner or buyer later disputes that the mold was actually cleared. Small remediation crews running multiple jobs a week rarely have a system tracking which step each job is on, which license each technician currently holds current CE hours for, and which clearance reports are still outstanding.
**What to build:** A per-job pipeline tracker enforcing the assessment-protocol-remediation-clearance sequence (with a hard block if a clearance test is attempted before remediation is logged complete); a license and CE-hours tracker per technician with renewal-deadline alerts; a document vault for assessment reports, written protocols, and clearance certificates tied to each job address; and a client-facing certificate generator so homeowners and real estate agents get a clean paper trail proving the job was done in the required order.
**Skill fit:** State mold-licensing statutes and CE requirements are published and stable; the job-sequencing state machine (assessment → protocol → remediation → clearance) is a workflow-engine problem well suited to someone with software engineering background, and the license/CE tracking is the same data-management pattern used across every other idea in this series.
**MVP scope:** Build the job-sequence tracker and license/CE calendar for 1-2 target states over 2-3 weekends; sign the first 5-8 independent mold assessment/remediation contractors through direct outreach and a free pilot month.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the Indoor Air Quality Association (IAQA) and the American Council for Accredited Certification (ACAC), both of which run mold-professional credentialing and have active local chapters.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $300/mo × 40 contractors = $12,000/mo. Because a real-estate-transaction clearance certificate is often time-sensitive (closings wait on it), contractors treat this as a business-continuity tool, not a nice-to-have, supporting retention at this client count.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every remediation job — not just annual license renewal — generates a fresh multi-step compliance sequence with a real estate closing or health outcome riding on it, so usage is per-job and continuous rather than seasonal.
**Biggest risk:** A contractor could try to use the tool to shortcut the assessment/remediation separation requirement rather than follow it. Mitigated by hard-coding the same-license-holder conflict check as a blocking validation rather than a soft warning, so the product only works if used correctly.
**Growth path:** Side project → 10 contractors ($3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 25 contractors ($7.5K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 40+ contractors plus a real-estate-agent-facing clearance-lookup add-on.

### 4. Federal Firearms Licensee (FFL) Retail Dealer ATF Recordkeeping & Compliance-Inspection Readiness
**Problem:** The roughly 78,000 active Federal Firearms Licensees in the US — most of them small independent gun shops and home-based dealers — must maintain a Bound Book (Acquisition & Disposition record) logging every firearm that enters and leaves inventory, complete a Form 4473 for every retail sale with the NICS background-check result recorded, and retain Form 4473s for 20 years (permanently transferring records to ATF if the license is ever discontinued). ATF conducts periodic compliance inspections — routine cyclic inspections roughly every 3-5 years plus for-cause inspections triggered by trace requests or complaints — and inspectors reconcile physical inventory against the Bound Book line by line; a Bound Book with unexplained gaps, missing 4473s, or acquisition/disposition entries that don't reconcile with on-hand inventory is the single most common basis for a warning letter, license revocation referral, or in serious cases criminal referral for record-keeping violations. Small dealers renew their FFL every 3 years via ATF Form 7 and must also track state-specific overlays (waiting periods, additional background-check steps, assault-weapon rosters in states like California) that stack on top of the federal baseline. A one- or two-person gun shop doing the Bound Book by hand or in a spreadsheet is exactly the setup where a reconciliation gap surfaces during an inspection, not before.
**What to build:** A digital Bound Book that logs acquisitions and dispositions with the same fields ATF requires, auto-flags any serial number with an unresolved acquisition-disposition mismatch, and generates an inspection-ready reconciliation report on demand; a Form 4473 completeness checker (all required fields present, NICS result recorded) tied to each sale; a 20-year retention vault with search by serial number, purchaser, or date for trace-request response; and an FFL/SOT renewal and state-overlay compliance calendar.
**Skill fit:** ATF's recordkeeping requirements (27 CFR Part 478) are precisely specified public regulation, making the Bound Book reconciliation logic a well-defined data-integrity problem — exactly the kind of gap-detection and audit-trail work a data-analytics background is suited for.
**MVP scope:** Build the digital Bound Book with reconciliation flagging and the 4473 completeness checker over 2-3 weekends; sign the first 5-8 independent FFL dealers by offering a free import of their existing paper or spreadsheet Bound Book as the onboarding hook.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the National Shooting Sports Foundation (NSSF), the firearms industry's trade association, which runs compliance-focused webinars and has a large small-dealer membership base, plus local gun shop associations.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $250/mo × 45 dealers = $11,250/mo. Because Bound Book errors carry license-revocation risk, dealers are motivated to pay for reconciliation confidence even on thin retail margins, supporting a large client count at a moderate price point.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every single firearm transaction — not an annual event — creates a new Bound Book entry that must reconcile forever, and the 20-year retention requirement means dealers can't just export and walk away; the tool becomes permanent infrastructure for the life of the license.
**Biggest risk:** Handling Form 4473 data means handling sensitive personally identifiable information tied to firearm purchases, which requires genuinely careful data security (encryption at rest, strict access controls) — a breach here is reputationally and legally severe. Mitigated by treating this as a hard non-negotiable in the MVP build, not a v2 feature: encrypt PII fields, log all access, and never expose Form 4473 data through this tool's own APIs.
**Growth path:** Side project → 12 dealers ($3K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 28 dealers ($7K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45+ dealers plus a multi-store/SOT (Class III dealer) enterprise tier.

### 5. Private Investigation Agency State Licensing & Case-File Retention Compliance
**Problem:** Private investigators operate under state-by-state licensing boards with wildly inconsistent requirements — California requires PI licensure through the Bureau of Security and Investigative Services (BSIS) with fingerprinting and continuing-education hours, Texas licenses PIs through the Department of Public Safety's Private Security program, Florida through its Division of Licensing, while a handful of states have no state-level PI license at all — and an agency operating across state lines must track a separate license, CE cycle, and renewal date in every state where it takes cases. Investigators who carry firearms on the job need a separate armed-guard/firearms endorsement layered on top of the base PI license, with its own qualification and renewal cadence. Nearly every state also imposes case-file retention requirements (commonly several years, sometimes tied to the statute of limitations on the underlying matter) covering surveillance logs, client contracts, and evidence chain-of-custody documentation — a retention failure discovered mid-litigation, when opposing counsel subpoenas the investigator's file, can torpedo both the case and the agency's credibility with referring attorneys. A solo or small-team PI agency juggling multi-state cases and a mix of armed and unarmed investigators has no natural place to track which license is current, which investigator is cleared to carry, and which case files are inside or outside their retention window.
**What to build:** A per-investigator license and firearms-endorsement tracker with state-specific CE requirements and renewal alerts; a case-file retention calendar that calculates the required hold period per state and case type and flags files approaching safe-to-destroy versus must-retain status; a multi-state licensing dashboard for agencies operating across state lines; and a client-facing case-status export formatted for the referring attorney's file.
**Skill fit:** State PI licensing requirements and retention statutes are public regulatory text that map cleanly to a lookup-table data model, and the retention-window calculation (often tied to a triggering event date plus a statutory duration) is straightforward date-arithmetic — the same pattern used across every idea in this series.
**MVP scope:** Build the license/CE tracker and case-file retention calendar for 3-4 target states over 2-3 weekends; sign the first 5-8 independent PI agencies through direct outreach and a free pilot tied to importing their existing case roster.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — outreach through the National Association of Legal Investigators (NALI) and state-level associations like the California Association of Licensed Investigators (CALI), both of which serve exactly this small-agency membership base.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $350/mo × 30 agencies = $10,500/mo. Multi-state agencies pay a premium tier for cross-state license tracking: 35 agencies blended at $400/mo = $14,000/mo.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every open case creates its own retention clock and every investigator's CE and firearms endorsement runs on its own renewal cycle, so the tool has continuous, per-case and per-person usage rather than a single annual touchpoint.
**Biggest risk:** Retention-period rules can vary by case type within a single state (e.g., domestic/matrimonial cases sometimes carry different retention rules than insurance-fraud investigations), so a flat per-state default risks under- or over-retaining. Mitigated by letting agencies tag each case with a type at intake that maps to the correct retention rule, defaulting to the longest applicable period when uncertain.

FFL Dealer Compliance is the volume play: with roughly 78,000 active FFLs nationwide and a well-organized trade association (NSSF) actively running compliance education, the addressable market and distribution channel are both unusually large and unusually well-defined for a solo-builder, even at a comparatively modest $250/mo price point.
