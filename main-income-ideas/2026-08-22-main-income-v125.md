### 1. Long-Term Care (Institutional) Pharmacy Consultant Pharmacist Review, State Institutional Permit & E-Kit Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Long-term care pharmacies that dispense to nursing homes and skilled nursing facilities operate under CMS's Skilled Nursing Facility Requirements of Participation at 42 CFR 483.45, which requires a licensed consultant pharmacist to review every resident's complete drug regimen at least once every 30 days, document any identified irregularity — a potentially unnecessary drug, a duplicate therapy, an unmonitored high-risk medication — in a written report sent to the resident's attending physician and the facility's director of nursing, and confirm each flagged irregularity was actually addressed before the next monthly review cycle begins. A pharmacy serving a portfolio of nursing home clients across a metro area must additionally hold a state board of pharmacy institutional-pharmacy permit (a distinct license class from retail pharmacy), maintain DEA registration for the Schedule II-V medications it dispenses, and, at most contracted facilities, stock and periodically inspect an emergency medication kit ("E-kit" or "eBox") whose contents must match a board-approved emergency drug list and be restocked, resealed, and logged every time it's opened — a kit found expired, incomplete, or unsealed without a restocking record is exactly the finding a state board of pharmacy inspection or a CMS-surveyor chart pull is built to catch. Every facility also runs its own 28-day (or shorter) unit-dose cycle-fill schedule tied to its resident census, and a pharmacy running a dozen or more facility contracts manages staggered monthly consultant-pharmacist review deadlines per facility, per-facility E-kit inspection and restocking logs, and cycle-fill scheduling on a mix of the pharmacy's dispensing software (built for fill accuracy, not clinical-review-deadline tracking) and a paper consultant-pharmacist binder, and a facility whose monthly drug-regimen review lapses past its 30-day window is exactly the gap a CMS survey or a state board audit targets.

**What to build:** A per-facility, per-resident consultant-pharmacist review tracker computing each facility's next-due 30-day review deadline and flagging any facility approaching or past its window; an irregularity-report log tracking each flagged issue from identification through physician notification to confirmed resolution before the next cycle; a per-facility E-kit inspection and restocking tracker flagging any kit approaching expiration or left unrestocked after a logged use; and a cycle-fill scheduling calendar synced to each facility's specific fill cadence.

**Skill fit:** CMS's 42 CFR 483.45 monthly-review requirement and state E-kit restocking rules are published, structured regulatory data with a concrete 30-day recurring deadline, and the per-facility review-deadline computation plus the irregularity-resolution-before-next-cycle gate are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an institutional-pharmacy niche this repo has never addressed.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the consultant-pharmacist review tracker, the irregularity-report log, and the E-kit inspection tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent long-term care pharmacies.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Society of Consultant Pharmacists (ASCP) runs an active member directory and annual meeting reaching exactly the small independent LTC pharmacies and solo consultant pharmacists currently tracking monthly review deadlines across a dozen or more facility contracts on a paper binder.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per pharmacy depending on facility-contract count and resident census. 30-40 pharmacies × $429/mo avg = $12,870-17,160/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every contracted facility's 30-day consultant-pharmacist review clock resets continuously for the life of the contract, every E-kit requires ongoing inspection regardless of use, and every new facility contract the pharmacy signs adds an independent review-and-restocking obligation — a continuous, facility-portfolio-driven compliance stream tied directly to the pharmacy's core service relationship with every nursing home it serves.

**Biggest risk:** A very small LTC pharmacy serving only one or two facilities has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need a spreadsheet can still handle. Mitigate by qualifying leads for pharmacies serving eight or more facility contracts, where the staggered monthly-review calendar genuinely outgrows manual tracking.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 pharmacies ($5.1K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 26 pharmacies ($11.2K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 30-40 pharmacies plus a CMS-survey-readiness export pharmacies can hand a contracted facility during its own state survey.

### 2. Home Infusion Pharmacy & Home Infusion Therapy Provider ACHC/URAC Accreditation, USP <797> & DMEPOS Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Home infusion pharmacies that prepare patient-specific IV admixtures and deliver them to private residences for antibiotic, chemotherapy, TPN, or biologic infusion therapy must hold accreditation from the Accreditation Commission for Health Care (ACHC) or URAC as a home infusion therapy provider — a periodic on-site survey, commonly on a 3-year cycle, that most Medicare Advantage and commercial payers require as a condition of reimbursement, reviewing both the pharmacy's USP <795>/<797>/<800> sterile and hazardous-drug compounding practices and, distinctly, a documented home-nursing-visit and patient-education record for every infusion delivered, since home infusion is fundamentally a coordinated site-of-care service rather than a wholesale-dispensing relationship. Because most ambulatory infusion pumps used in the home are billed to Medicare Part B as durable medical equipment, a home infusion provider's DME arm must separately enroll with the National Supplier Clearinghouse (NSC) and pass its own DMEPOS accreditation survey — a second, parallel accreditation track layered on top of the pharmacy's own USP-chapter compliance, and a provider whose ACHC/URAC survey lapses, or whose DMEPOS accreditation goes stale, loses its ability to bill the payers that fund nearly all of its revenue simultaneously. A small home infusion pharmacy running several infusion nurses across a growing patient census manages ACHC/URAC survey-readiness documentation, per-patient home-visit and education-record completeness, DMEPOS accreditation currency, and per-technician USP <797>/<800> garbing and media-fill competency on a mix of the pharmacy's dispensing system and a compliance binder assembled ahead of each survey cycle, and a patient visit missing its documented education record, or a DMEPOS accreditation allowed to lapse, is exactly the finding an ACHC/URAC or NSC audit is built to catch.

**What to build:** An ACHC/URAC accreditation renewal and survey-readiness tracker; a per-patient home-nursing-visit and education-record completeness log tied to each infusion episode; a DMEPOS NSC enrollment and accreditation renewal tracker; and a per-technician USP <797>/<800> garbing, media-fill, and competency-currency tracker gating who may prepare a sterile admixture.

**Skill fit:** ACHC/URAC's home infusion accreditation standards, USP's sterile-compounding chapters, and NSC's DMEPOS enrollment requirements are published, structured accrediting-body and federal data, and the per-patient visit-documentation completeness check plus the dual-accreditation renewal tracker are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a home-based infusion niche distinct from every compounding-pharmacy or LTC-pharmacy idea already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the accreditation renewal tracker, the per-patient visit-documentation log, and the DMEPOS tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent home infusion pharmacies.

**Time to first $:** 30-60 days — the National Home Infusion Association (NHIA) runs an active member directory and annual conference reaching exactly the small independent home infusion providers navigating dual ACHC/URAC and DMEPOS accreditation without a dedicated compliance department.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349-699/mo per provider depending on patient census and DME billing scope. 20-28 providers × $499/mo avg = $9,980-13,972/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every new infusion patient generates a fresh home-visit-and-education-record obligation, every technician's USP competency renews on its own cycle, and the ACHC/URAC and DMEPOS accreditation cycles both recur independently for the life of the business — a continuous, patient-census-driven compliance stream tied directly to the provider's ability to keep billing the payers that fund it.

**Biggest risk:** A very small provider running only a handful of infusion patients has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for providers with a growing multi-nurse patient census, where the per-patient documentation volume and dual-accreditation calendar genuinely outgrow manual tracking.

**Growth path:** Side project → 8-10 providers ($4-5K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 16-18 providers ($8-9K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 20-28 providers plus a payer-audit-readiness export upsell for providers billing multiple Medicare Advantage plans.

### 3. Athletic Trainer Group & Outreach Sports Medicine Practice State Licensure, BOC Certification & Return-to-Play Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Athletic trainer groups that contract with high schools, colleges, and semi-pro teams to provide sideline coverage and injury management operate under the state athletic training practice act most states use to license individual ATs, paired with the national Board of Certification for Athletic Trainers (BOC) credential every practicing AT must maintain through a fixed continuing-education cycle — and, critically, a written physician-delegated standing order or collaborative practice protocol that must be current and specific to each contracted school or team before an AT may legally evaluate an injury or clear an athlete to return to activity, renewed annually alongside the relationship with that team's supervising physician. Every state's youth-sports concussion statute (the "Lystedt Law" family now enacted in all 50 states) further requires a documented graduated return-to-play protocol — a staged sequence of activity clearances — with sign-off from a licensed healthcare provider before a concussed athlete may resume play, and an athlete cleared without a complete, staged RTP record on file is exactly the finding a school district's insurance carrier or a post-injury liability claim is built to expose, since a mishandled concussion return is one of the most litigated fact patterns in youth and collegiate sports medicine. An outreach group running a roster of ATs across a dozen or more school and team contracts manages staggered per-AT BOC certification and state license renewal, per-contract standing-order currency, and per-athlete return-to-play documentation with no dedicated compliance function, and an AT dispatched to cover a game under a lapsed license or an expired standing order is exactly the gap a state athletic training board complaint is built to catch.

**What to build:** A per-AT BOC certification and state license renewal tracker with a "cleared to cover" dispatch gate; a per-contract standing-order/collaborative-practice-protocol currency tracker tied to each school or team relationship; a per-athlete graduated return-to-play documentation log requiring each staged clearance be recorded before an athlete is marked cleared; and a contract-renewal calendar tracking each school or team relationship's own annual renewal cycle.

**Skill fit:** State athletic training practice acts, BOC's certification-renewal structure, and the graduated return-to-play protocol required by state concussion statutes are public, structured licensing and regulatory data, and the per-athlete staged-clearance gate plus the dispatch-eligibility check are the same deterministic pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a sports-medicine outreach trade this repo has never addressed.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the AT certification/dispatch-gate tracker, the standing-order currency tracker, and the return-to-play documentation log. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent outreach athletic training groups.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the National Athletic Trainers' Association (NATA) and state AT association member directories reach exactly the small outreach groups juggling staggered BOC renewals and per-contract standing orders across a growing roster of school and team clients.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $199-399/mo per group depending on AT headcount and contract count. 45-60 groups × $269/mo avg = $12,105-16,140/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every contracted school or team's standing order renews on its own annual cycle, every AT's BOC certification recurs independently, and every athlete injury generates a fresh return-to-play documentation record — a continuous, contract-and-roster-driven compliance stream tied directly to the group's ability to keep covering games legally.

**Biggest risk:** A solo AT with a single school contract has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for outreach groups running several ATs across multiple concurrent school and team contracts, where the standing-order and return-to-play documentation volume genuinely outgrows a paper binder.

**Growth path:** Side project → 15 groups ($4K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 35 groups ($9.4K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 45-60 groups plus a school-district liability-readiness export groups can hand an athletic director's insurance renewal review.

### 4. Naturopathic Doctor Practice State Licensure Eligibility, NPLEX & Prescriptive/DEA Authority Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Naturopathic doctors face a compliance surface unlike almost any other licensed health profession: only roughly 25 states plus D.C. and several territories license NDs at all, meaning the very legality of practicing as an ND turns on which state a practice is physically located in, and among licensing states the scope of practice varies dramatically — several (Washington, Oregon, Vermont, and Arizona among them) grant a broad prescriptive formulary and independent DEA registration for controlled substances, while most licensing states grant little to no prescriptive authority and several license NDs with no controlled-substance authority whatsoever. Every ND must hold a passing score on the Naturopathic Physicians Licensing Examinations (NPLEX) and maintain state license renewal on that state's own continuing-education cycle, and a practice expanding into a second state, or hiring an associate ND, must re-verify that state's specific licensure status and prescriptive-authority tier before any prescribing activity begins there — a practice that assumes one state's scope of practice carries over to another is exactly the gap a state naturopathic board complaint or an unauthorized-practice-of-medicine investigation is built to catch. In states granting DEA-eligible prescriptive authority, the ND must additionally maintain a current federal DEA registration and, in several of those states, a separate state controlled-substance registration renewed on its own cycle. A small multi-practitioner naturopathic practice operating in one state, or expanding into a neighboring one, manages per-ND license and NPLEX-credential verification, state-specific scope-of-practice and prescriptive-tier documentation, and DEA/state CSR renewal with no dedicated compliance function, relying instead on each practitioner's own memory of what their state does and doesn't allow.

**What to build:** A per-ND, per-state licensure-eligibility tracker confirming current license status and NPLEX-credential currency before a new practitioner is scheduled; a state prescriptive-authority reference matched to each practice location, flagging which drug classes (if any) an ND may prescribe in that specific state; a DEA and state controlled-substance registration renewal tracker for NDs practicing in DEA-eligible states; and a CE-hour tracker per practitioner tied to each state's specific renewal requirement.

**Skill fit:** State naturopathic licensing statutes and their widely varying scope-of-practice and prescriptive-authority tiers are public, structured regulatory data well suited to a state-by-state lookup table, and the per-state eligibility and prescriptive-tier gate is the same deterministic "verify the rule before the action proceeds" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a licensed-medicine category this repo has never addressed.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the licensure-eligibility tracker, the state prescriptive-authority reference, and the DEA/CSR renewal tracker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent naturopathic practices.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Association of Naturopathic Physicians (AANP) runs an active member directory and annual conference reaching exactly the small independent NDs and multi-practitioner clinics navigating state-by-state licensure and prescriptive-authority variance without a dedicated compliance function.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $149-299/mo per practice depending on practitioner headcount and multi-state footprint. 55-70 practices × $219/mo avg = $12,045-15,330/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every practitioner's state license and CE cycle renews independently, and every new state a practice expands into (or every associate ND hired from a different state) triggers a fresh eligibility-and-scope verification — a continuous, practitioner-and-footprint-driven compliance stream that grows as the practice grows.

**Biggest risk:** A solo-practitioner ND in a single well-understood state has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need. Mitigate by qualifying leads for multi-practitioner practices or those operating across more than one state, where the prescriptive-authority variance genuinely creates a compliance risk a single practitioner's memory can't reliably cover.

**Growth path:** Side project → 18 practices ($3.9K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 40 practices ($8.8K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 55-70 practices plus a multi-state expansion-readiness report for practices considering a new location.

### 5. Telehealth Physician Group IMLC & Multi-State DEA/Controlled-Substance Registration Compliance Tracker

**Problem:** Telehealth physician groups whose doctors treat patients across many states rely on the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC) to streamline license issuance across its roughly 40 member states, but the compact only expedites the initial licensing paperwork — every state license it produces still renews independently on that state's own cycle with its own CME requirements, and a physician's IMLC-issued license in one state lapsing unnoticed while the physician continues treating patients there is exactly the finding a state medical board investigation or a malpractice-insurance audit is built to catch. Layered on top of state medical licensure, most states require a physician to hold that state's own controlled-substance registration (CSR) — separate from and in addition to a single federal DEA registration — before prescribing to a patient physically located there at the time of the telehealth visit, and the federal Ryan Haight Online Pharmacy Consumer Protection Act further restricts prescribing a Schedule II-V drug via telemedicine without a qualifying in-person examination, subject to narrow, periodically-extended DEA telemedicine flexibilities a group must track state by state and prescription by prescription. A telehealth group running a roster of physicians licensed across a dozen or more states manages staggered per-state license and CME renewal, per-state CSR currency layered against each physician's federal DEA registration, and Ryan Haight-eligibility determination for every controlled-substance prescription with no systematic cross-check — and a physician who prescribes a controlled substance into a state where their CSR has lapsed, or without confirming Ryan Haight eligibility, exposes the group to a state board complaint and a DEA registration action simultaneously.

**What to build:** A per-physician, per-state medical license and CME renewal tracker computing each state's specific due date from the IMLC issuance date or direct state renewal cycle; a per-physician, per-state controlled-substance registration tracker layered against the physician's federal DEA registration with a "cleared to prescribe" gate; a Ryan Haight eligibility checker flagging any controlled-substance prescription attempted without a qualifying in-person exam or an applicable telemedicine flexibility on file; and a cross-state licensure-and-CSR dashboard surfacing every physician's active practice-eligible states in one view.

**Skill fit:** IMLC's compact structure, state CSR requirements, and the DEA's telemedicine flexibility determinations are public, structured regulatory and compact-body data, and the per-state, per-physician eligibility gate is the same deterministic "verify the license and registration before the prescription is written" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a physician-specific telehealth compliance surface distinct from the PSYPACT-based behavioral-health telehealth ideas and the payer-facing credentialing service already in this repo.

**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the multi-state license/CME tracker, the per-state CSR tracker with the prescribing gate, and the Ryan Haight eligibility checker. First 5-8 clients through direct outreach to independent telehealth physician groups.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — the American Telemedicine Association (ATA) member directory and state telehealth physician group networks reach exactly the small independent telehealth practices juggling staggered IMLC state renewals and per-state CSR tracking across a growing multi-state physician roster.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299-599/mo per group depending on physician headcount and state footprint. 30-40 groups × $429/mo avg = $12,870-17,160/mo.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every physician's per-state license and CME cycle renews independently, every state CSR recurs on its own clock layered against the physician's single federal DEA registration, and every new state a group expands telehealth service into adds a fresh licensure-and-CSR obligation — a continuous, physician-and-footprint-driven compliance stream tied directly to the group's ability to keep prescribing legally across its entire service area.

**Biggest risk:** A small telehealth group licensed in only two or three states has a comparatively low-stakes tracking need a spreadsheet can still handle. Mitigate by qualifying leads for groups with physicians licensed across eight or more states, where the staggered per-state renewal-and-CSR matrix genuinely outgrows manual tracking.

**Growth path:** Side project → 12 groups ($5.1K/mo) → replaces-part-time-income → 28 groups ($12K/mo) → replaces-full-time-salary at 30-40 groups plus a state-board-inquiry-readiness export for groups facing a multi-state licensure audit.
