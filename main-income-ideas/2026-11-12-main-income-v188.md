# Main Income Ideas - 2026-11-12 (Session 188)

## Who this is for
A 29-year-old software engineer with data analytics skills who wants to replace a full-time salary ($8-15K+/mo) within 12-24 months by building solo, in evenings and weekends, with no outside funding. Every idea below must have a realistic path to first revenue inside 90 days and a named, reachable distribution channel.

## Lens for this session: Alex Hormozi
Built from the $100M Offers / $100M Leads frameworks:
- **Value equation:** Value = (Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood of Success) / (Time Delay x Effort & Sacrifice).
- **Grand Slam Offer:** performance-priced or guarantee-backed, with the fee drawn from money the client gets back or gains.
- **Lead efficiency:** stop paying for leads that cannot convert, and turn the leads already arriving into revenue.

Per standing feedback, none of these are compliance or regulatory tools. Each solves a revenue-leakage or growth problem.

## Ideas

### 1. Invalid-Lead Dispute Filer for Home-Service Companies Using Google Local Services Ads

**Problem:** Companies running Google Local Services Ads pay per lead, and a share of leads are wrong-service, out-of-area, spam or duplicates. Google credits those leads back if disputed with the right evidence, but the dispute process is tedious, so most owners never file.

**What to build:** Pull the account's lead list and call recordings or transcripts. Classify leads that look invalid (wrong service, outside the service area, no answer from the customer, duplicates), prepare the dispute with the required reasoning and track credit status. Show credits recovered per month.

**Grand Slam angle:** "We only take 25% of the credits we win you. No credit, no fee."

**Skill fit:** Lead classification, transcript analysis and workflow automation.

**MVP scope:** 3 weekends. Start with a manual upload of the lead report and recordings.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days through home-service marketing groups and agency partners.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** An account spending $3,000/mo on leads, with 15% disputable and 70% credited, recovers about $315/mo. 25% = $79/mo. That is small, so target accounts spending $8,000+/mo, which recover about $840/mo = $210/mo fee. With a $49 platform fee, about $260/mo. 45 accounts x $260 = **$11,700/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Credits recur every month the account runs ads, and the lead-quality data helps the owner tune spend.

**Biggest risk:** Platform policy and interface changes. Keep a manual-filing fallback and build around the platform's published dispute reasons.

**Growth path:** Local services ads, then agencies managing many accounts, then other pay-per-lead platforms.

### 2. Stock-Aware Ad Pausing for Small Shopify Stores

**Problem:** A small store runs ads on products that have sold out or have only a few units left. The ad money is wasted on clicks that cannot buy, and customers who find a sold-out page go to a competitor. The owner checks stock manually and misses it.

**What to build:** Connect the store inventory and ad accounts (Meta, Google Shopping). When a product falls below a set stock threshold, pause its ads or swap to an in-stock alternative, and resume when restocked. Report wasted ad spend avoided.

**Grand Slam angle:** "We find the ad spend wasted on out-of-stock products in the last 90 days, or the audit is free."

**Skill fit:** Shopify and ad-platform APIs and rules automation.

**MVP scope:** 3 weekends.

**Time to first $:** 30-60 days through Shopify seller groups.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $79/mo. A store spending $10K/mo on ads typically wastes 3-5%, so $300-500/mo is saved, which justifies the fee. 140 stores = **$11,060/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** It runs continuously on every SKU and ties into inventory forecasting.

**Biggest risk:** Ad-platform API limits. Start with Meta catalog ads, then add Google.

**Growth path:** Small stores, then agencies, then reorder forecasting and bundle suggestions.

### 3. Local Competitor Price and Promotion Monitor for Independent Retailers and Service Shops

**Problem:** An independent retailer or service shop sets prices without knowing what nearby competitors charge today. A competitor's sale goes unnoticed for weeks, or the shop leaves money on the table by pricing below the market.

**What to build:** Track a short list of competitor websites, listings and social posts for price and promo changes on the shop's key items. Send a weekly alert ("Competitor A dropped tire installation to $19.99"), plus a "you're 12% below the market on these 5 items" opportunity list. Log the owner's response and results.

**Grand Slam angle:** "We find at least $1,000 a year of pricing upside, or the first quarter is free."

**Skill fit:** Scraping, change detection and alerting.

**MVP scope:** 3 weekends.

**Time to first $:** 45-60 days through independent-retailer and trade groups.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $99/mo. 115 shops = **$11,385/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Price history builds a market database that gets more valuable over time.

**Biggest risk:** Sites that block scraping. Target competitors with public price lists and add manual price entry.

**Growth path:** Independent retailers, then tire and auto shops, florists, jewelers and pet stores.

### 4. Private-Dining and Catering Inquiry Converter for Restaurants

**Problem:** Restaurants get inquiries for birthdays, rehearsal dinners and corporate lunches by phone, email and DM, and answer them slowly or incompletely. Each private event is worth $1,500-6,000, many more than a regular night, and a slow reply loses it to a competitor.

**What to build:** A guided inquiry page and auto-reply: capture date, party size, budget and occasion, show available spaces and sample menus, send an instant proposal range and request a deposit. Follow up automatically and show the inquiry-to-booking rate and revenue.

**Grand Slam angle:** "A 60-day trial. Book one extra event or pay nothing."

**Skill fit:** Forms, proposal templates, deposit links and follow-up sequences.

**MVP scope:** 3 weekends.

**Time to first $:** 30-45 days through restaurant-owner groups.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $129/mo + 2% of deposits. A restaurant collecting $8,000 in deposits pays about $290/mo. 40 restaurants x $290 = **$11,600/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Private events are high-margin and repeat, and the owner can see the extra revenue.

**Biggest risk:** Restaurants with no private space. Target those with a room, patio or buyout option.

**Growth path:** Restaurants, then breweries, wineries, event barns and hotel bars.

### 5. Wholesale Reorder Portal for Small Makers Selling to Boutiques

**Problem:** A maker of candles, soaps, jewelry or prints sells to 30-100 boutiques through email and text. Reorders arrive randomly, order forms are PDFs and invoices are hand-written. Boutiques forget to reorder and the maker never nudges them.

**What to build:** A simple wholesale portal with a line sheet, minimum order rules, net-30 invoicing and one-click reorder of a store's last order. Remind boutiques to reorder based on their past cycle and flag accounts that have gone quiet. Show revenue per account and reorder rate.

**Grand Slam angle:** "We bring back lapsed wholesale accounts, and you pay 5% of wholesale revenue we process."

**Skill fit:** Order portal, invoicing, reorder reminders and account analytics.

**MVP scope:** 3-4 weekends.

**Time to first $:** 45-60 days through maker communities and craft-business groups.

**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $49/mo + 2% of wholesale order value. A maker processing $6,000/mo pays about $169/mo. 70 makers x $169 = **$11,830/mo**.

**Why this can go beyond side money:** Each maker's customer list sits in the tool, and reorder reminders directly raise their wholesale revenue.

**Biggest risk:** Competition from established wholesale marketplaces. Differentiate by letting makers keep their own boutique relationships and avoid marketplace commissions.

**Growth path:** Candle and soap makers, then jewelry, stationery, food makers and apparel brands.
