### 1. Retail Plant Nursery & Greenhouse Grower State Nursery License, Phytosanitary Certificate & Pesticide Applicator Compliance
**Problem:** Retail and wholesale plant nurseries and greenhouse growers must hold a state nursery/greenhouse dealer license or certificate — California's Nursery Stock license under the Food & Agricultural Code, administered by CDFA, and Florida's Division of Plant Industry Nursery Certificate under Chapter 581, among them — renewed annually and tied to a state inspector's periodic on-site inspection for regulated pests, plant diseases, and noxious weeds; a nursery found propagating or selling stock carrying a quarantine pest during that inspection faces an immediate stop-sale order on the affected stock, and a lapsed license discovered at inspection halts the nursery's ability to sell at all. Any nursery shipping stock across state lines or internationally needs a phytosanitary certificate issued per shipment by the state's plant regulatory official in coordination with USDA APHIS, which requires the specific stock being shipped to have passed inspection within a defined validity window before the certificate can be issued — a shipment sent without a current phytosanitary certificate is exactly the finding a destination-state agricultural inspection station is built to catch, and can result in the shipment being destroyed at the buyer's dock. Nursery staff who apply pesticides or fungicides to stock must also hold their own state pesticide applicator licenses — distinct from a pest-control company's technicians — and comply with EPA's Worker Protection Standard, including application records retained for two years and posted re-entry-interval notices; a nursery unable to produce those records during a state agriculture-department pesticide compliance inspection faces the same enforcement exposure as an unlicensed applicator. A small nursery running several growing houses and a rotating stock inventory manages state nursery license renewal, per-shipment phytosanitary certificate currency, and staff pesticide-applicator license/WPS documentation with no dedicated compliance function beyond a filing cabinet of inspection slips.
**What to build:** A state nursery/greenhouse license renewal tracker with an inspection-readiness checklist; a per-shipment phytosanitary certificate tracker confirming the relevant stock's inspection is within its validity window before a certificate is requested; and a per-employee pesticide applicator license and WPS application-record tracker.
**Skill fit:** State nursery licensing cycles and phytosanitary certificate validity windows are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-shipment record requirement, and the certificate-currency tracker plus the applicator-license tracker are the same deterministic "confirm the required record is on file before the action proceeds / block the action until the credential clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to an agricultural-retail trade most small nurseries currently track on a filing cabinet of inspection slips with no systematic cross-check between a specific shipment and the stock's underlying inspection currency.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the nursery license tracker, the phytosanitary certificate tracker, and the applicator/WPS tracker; land the first 5-8 nurseries through AmericanHort member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — AmericanHort's member directory and state nursery and landscape association meetings, where small growers already juggle phytosanitary certificate timing against a tight shipping calendar with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $229/mo base + $12/mo per pesticide-applicator employee tracked. Average client (5 employees) = $229 + $60 = $289/mo. 32-40 nurseries × $289/mo = **$9,248-11,560/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every outbound shipment opens its own phytosanitary certificate event, every applicator's license renews on its own cycle, and the nursery's own license renews annually — a continuous, per-shipment and per-employee compliance stream tied directly to the nursery's shipping volume and season.
**Biggest risk:** A shipment sent without a current phytosanitary certificate, or stock sold carrying a quarantine pest past a lapsed inspection, is exactly the finding a destination-state inspection station or a state agriculture-department audit is built to catch — the certificate tracker and the license-renewal checklist are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → nursery license tracker, phytosanitary tracker, and applicator/WPS tracker deployed, first 5-8 nursery retainers through AmericanHort outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 nurseries. Replaces-full-time-salary → 32-40 nurseries, pushing past $9.25-11.56K/mo.

### 2. Electrology (Licensed Permanent Hair Removal) Practice State Electrologist Licensure & Single-Use Sharps Compliance
**Problem:** Electrology practices performing needle-based permanent hair removal operate under a state electrologist licensing or registration regime entirely separate from the cosmetology board and the medical board — California's Electrolysis Act (Business & Professions Code Division 3, Chapter 6.5), administered through the Medical Board of California, and Florida's Board of Electrolysis Practitioners under Florida Statutes Chapter 478 among them — that requires completion of a board-approved training program, an individual license or registration renewed on a fixed cycle with continuing-education hours, and strict compliance with the single-use, pre-sterilized, disposable needle/probe requirement mandated in most states specifically to prevent bloodborne pathogen transmission through repeated skin puncture; a practitioner found reusing a disposable probe, or one working on a lapsed license, is exactly the finding a state health-department or licensing-board complaint investigation targets first, and repeat sharps violations can result in license revocation given the direct infection-control stakes of a needle-based skin-penetration procedure performed dozens of times a day. Every client session must also be documented with an informed-consent record and, in several states, a client intake health-history form flagging contraindications — certain medications, skin conditions, pacemakers near treatment sites — and a practice unable to produce a specific client's signed consent during a board audit faces the same exposure as a sharps violation. A solo or small multi-practitioner electrology studio running several rooms and a rotating client base manages per-practitioner license/CE currency, per-session sharps/single-use-probe documentation, and per-client consent and health-history records with no dedicated compliance function beyond a paper intake folder.
**What to build:** A per-practitioner state license/registration and CE-hour renewal tracker with a "cleared to treat" gate; a per-session single-use-probe/sharps compliance log confirming a new, sealed probe was opened and disposed of for that session; and a per-client informed-consent and health-history intake tracker flagging contraindications before a session is booked.
**Skill fit:** State electrology licensing cycles and single-use-probe mandates are public, structured regulatory data with a well-defined per-practitioner and per-session record requirement, and the treat-eligibility gate plus the sharps-compliance log are the same deterministic "block the action until the credential clears / require the record before the action proceeds" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a skin-penetration trade most small studios currently track on a paper intake folder with no systematic cross-check between a practitioner's license status and today's session log.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license/CE tracker with the treat-eligibility gate, the sharps compliance log, and the consent/health-history tracker; land the first 5-8 studios through American Electrology Association (AEA) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — American Electrology Association (AEA) member directory and state electrology board public license-holder lists, where small solo and multi-practitioner studios already juggle CE-hour renewal and per-session sharps documentation with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $249/mo base + $20/mo per practitioner tracked. Average client (1.5 practitioners) = $249 + $30 = $279/mo. 33-40 studios × $279/mo = **$9,207-11,160/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every session opens its own sharps-compliance and consent-documentation event, and every practitioner's license and CE hours renew on their own independent cycle — a continuous, per-session compliance stream tied directly to the studio's client volume.
**Biggest risk:** A probe reused past its single-use mandate, or a session performed without a signed informed-consent record on file, is exactly the finding a state board complaint investigation is built to catch — the sharps-compliance log and the consent tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → license/CE tracker, sharps log, and consent tracker deployed, first 5-8 studio retainers through AEA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 18-22 studios. Replaces-full-time-salary → 33-40 studios, pushing past $9.21-11.16K/mo.

### 3. Licensed Cannabis Cultivation Operation State Canopy-Tier, METRC Plant-Tag & Approved-Pesticide Compliance
**Problem:** State-licensed cannabis cultivators operate under a canopy-tiered license — a state cannabis control agency caps each license at a specific square footage of mature flowering canopy, with California's DCC cultivation tiers ranging from under 5,000 sq ft to over 22,000 sq ft — and a cultivator whose actual flowering canopy measured during a state compliance inspection exceeds the licensed tier is exactly the finding that triggers a stop-work order and can jeopardize the license entirely; the flowering footprint changes constantly as crops rotate through vegetative and flowering stages across multiple rooms, and a grower running several rooms has no easy way to confirm at any given moment whether the current flowering square footage is still under the licensed cap. Every individual plant must be tagged in the state's seed-to-sale system, most commonly METRC, from the clone or seedling stage through harvest, and a plant discovered without a current, correctly assigned tag during an inspection is treated as untracked and potentially diverted product — a far more serious finding than a paperwork gap. Cultivators are also restricted to a state-published list of pesticides approved for use on cannabis, since no product carries an EPA label specifically for a federally illegal crop and each state instead maintains its own approved-product list, and a pesticide application from outside that list — discovered either at inspection or through a failed residue test on the harvested batch — can result in the entire batch being destroyed rather than sold. A small licensed cultivation operation running multiple grow rooms through a rotating harvest schedule manages canopy-utilization tracking against the licensed cap, per-plant METRC tag currency, and pesticide-application compliance against the state's approved list with no dedicated compliance function beyond the state portal's own reporting screen.
**What to build:** A real-time canopy-utilization tracker computing current flowering square footage against the license's tier cap as rooms rotate through vegetative and flowering stages, flagging any room change that would push the operation over its licensed limit; a per-plant METRC tag audit tool cross-checking physical plant counts against tagged records; and a pesticide-application log restricted to the state's approved-product list, flagging any application using a non-approved product before a batch moves to harvest testing.
**Skill fit:** State cultivation canopy-tier rules and METRC's per-plant tagging requirements are public, structured state regulatory data with a well-defined per-room and per-plant record requirement, and the canopy-utilization tracker plus the approved-pesticide log are the same deterministic "compute the current state against the licensed cap / restrict the action to the approved list" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a cultivation trade most small growers currently track by exporting the state portal to a spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a specific room's flowering footprint and the license's tier cap.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the canopy-utilization tracker, the METRC tag audit tool, and the approved-pesticide log; land the first 5-8 cultivators through National Cannabis Industry Association (NCIA) cultivator-segment outreach and state cannabis growers associations.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — NCIA's cultivator-segment member directory and state cannabis growers association meetings, where small licensed cultivators already juggle real-time canopy tracking and per-plant METRC tagging across rotating grow rooms with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $349/mo base + $50/mo per grow room beyond the first 2. Average client (4 rooms) = $349 + $100 = $449/mo. 20-25 cultivators × $449/mo = **$8,980-11,225/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every grow-room rotation reopens the canopy-utilization check, every new plant opens its own METRC tag event, and every pesticide application requires its own approved-list verification — a continuous, per-room and per-plant compliance stream tied directly to the cultivator's harvest cycle.
**Biggest risk:** Flowering canopy that exceeds the licensed tier cap, or a plant found without a current METRC tag, is exactly the finding a state cultivation compliance inspection is built to catch — the canopy tracker and the tag-audit tool are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → canopy tracker, METRC tag audit tool, and pesticide log deployed, first 5-8 cultivator retainers through NCIA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 11-14 cultivators. Replaces-full-time-salary → 20-25 cultivators, pushing past $8.98-11.23K/mo.

### 4. Healthcare Per Diem/Travel Nurse Staffing Registry State Nurse Registry License & Multi-State License Verification Compliance
**Problem:** Healthcare staffing agencies and nurse registries that place per diem, travel, and PRN nurses and CNAs at hospitals, nursing homes, and home-health agencies operate under state-specific nurse registry or staffing-agency licensure — Florida's Nurse Registry license under Florida Statutes Chapter 400, Part II, administered by AHCA, is a distinct license from a home health agency's own license — and many hospital systems will only contract with registries holding Joint Commission Health Care Staffing Services (HCSS) certification; a registry placing a nurse at a facility without a current state registry license or accreditation on file is exactly the finding a facility's own vendor-compliance audit is built to catch, risking the registry's entire contract with that facility. Every placed clinician's professional license must be verified as active and unencumbered in the state where the assignment occurs — complicated by the Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC), which lets an RN with a compact license work across member states without a separate license in each, but only in states that have actually joined the compact, and a nurse dispatched to a non-compact state on compact privileges alone is working without a valid license in that state — and registries verify this against the NCSBN's Nursys database before every assignment. Facilities also require documented pre-placement health screening (TB test, required immunizations) and competency validation on file before a clinician can be dispatched, and a placement made without a current TB test or license verification on file is exactly the gap a facility's Joint Commission survey or a state agency's own registry audit is built to expose. A small registry running several dozen per diem clinicians across multiple facility contracts manages per-clinician multi-state license/compact-privilege verification, health-screening currency, and the registry's own state license/HCSS accreditation renewal with no dedicated compliance function beyond a shared spreadsheet.
**What to build:** A per-clinician license and NLC compact-privilege verification tracker cross-checked against the Nursys database with a "cleared to dispatch" gate scoped to the specific assignment state; a per-clinician health-screening (TB test, immunization) and competency-validation currency tracker; and a registry-level state license/Joint Commission HCSS accreditation renewal tracker.
**Skill fit:** State nurse registry licensing cycles, NLC compact-state lists, and Joint Commission HCSS renewal requirements are public regulatory and accrediting-body data with a well-defined per-clinician record requirement, and the dispatch-eligibility gate plus the health-screening tracker are the same deterministic "block the action until the compliance record clears" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a healthcare-logistics trade most small registries currently track on a shared spreadsheet with no systematic cross-check between a clinician's compact-privilege status and the specific state they're about to be dispatched to.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the license/compact-privilege tracker with the dispatch-eligibility gate, the health-screening tracker, and the registry accreditation tracker; land the first 5-8 registries through American Staffing Association's healthcare staffing section and National Association of Travel Healthcare Organizations (NATHO) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — NATHO's member directory and ASA's healthcare staffing section meetings, where small registries already juggle per-clinician compact-privilege verification and pre-placement health-screening currency across multiple facility contracts with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $8/mo per active clinician tracked. Average client (30 clinicians) = $299 + $240 = $539/mo. 17-21 registries × $539/mo = **$9,163-11,319/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every clinician's license and compact-privilege status is a continuously monitored record that changes with every renewal cycle and every new-state assignment, and every placement opens its own health-screening currency check — a continuous, per-clinician compliance stream tied directly to the registry's active roster and contract volume.
**Biggest risk:** A clinician dispatched to a non-compact state on compact privileges alone, or a placement made without a current TB test on file, is exactly the finding a facility vendor-compliance audit or a Joint Commission survey is built to catch — the dispatch-eligibility gate and the health-screening tracker are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → license/compact tracker, health-screening tracker, and accreditation tracker deployed, first 5-8 registry retainers through NATHO/ASA outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 9-11 registries. Replaces-full-time-salary → 17-21 registries, pushing past $9.16-11.32K/mo.

### 5. Ketamine & Esketamine (Spravato) Clinic FDA REMS Certification & DEA Controlled-Substance Compliance
**Problem:** Ketamine clinics offering intranasal esketamine (Spravato) for treatment-resistant depression operate under the FDA's Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program, which requires the clinic and every prescriber to be separately certified in the Spravato REMS, mandates that every dose be administered on-site with direct observation, and requires a documented minimum post-dose monitoring period — blood pressure checks at specific intervals and a dissociation-scale assessment — before the patient can be released; a dose administered or a patient released without the REMS-mandated monitoring documentation is exactly the finding a REMS program audit or a state medical board complaint investigation targets first, and non-compliance can result in a prescriber's REMS certification being revoked, cutting off the clinic's ability to dispense the drug at all. Off-label IV ketamine infusion, by contrast, isn't REMS-governed but instead operates under state medical board delegation rules — since the infusions are frequently administered by an RN or CRNA under a physician's standing order — and requires DEA registration for storing and administering a Schedule III controlled substance, with a controlled-substance administration log that must reconcile against the clinic's on-hand ketamine inventory; a discrepancy is exactly the finding a DEA audit of the clinic's controlled-substance handling is built to catch. Every patient, regardless of modality, must also complete a documented psychiatric screening — including suicide-risk and substance-use-history assessment — before their first session, since an undocumented intake screening is a direct patient-safety and licensing-board finding given ketamine's dissociative and abuse-potential profile. A small clinic running several treatment rooms and rotating prescribers manages per-prescriber REMS certification currency, per-session monitoring documentation, DEA inventory reconciliation, and per-patient intake-screening records with no dedicated compliance function.
**What to build:** A per-prescriber Spravato REMS certification tracker with a "cleared to dispense" gate; a per-session REMS monitoring checklist (vitals intervals, dissociation-scale assessment, minimum observation window) blocking a patient's discharge from being logged until the full monitoring window is documented; and a DEA controlled-substance (ketamine) inventory reconciliation log cross-checked against the administration log.
**Skill fit:** FDA REMS program requirements and DEA controlled-substance recordkeeping rules are public, structured regulatory data with well-defined per-prescriber and per-session record requirements, and the REMS monitoring checklist plus the inventory-reconciliation log are the same deterministic "require the record before the action proceeds / cross-check two records that must match" pattern this series builds repeatedly, applied to a fast-growing psychiatric-medicine trade most small clinics currently track on a paper monitoring sheet with no systematic cross-check between a specific session's monitoring window and the clinic's on-hand controlled-substance inventory.
**MVP scope:** 2-3 weekends to build the REMS certification tracker with the dispense-eligibility gate, the per-session monitoring checklist, and the DEA inventory-reconciliation log; land the first 5-8 clinics through American Society of Ketamine Physicians, Psychotherapists & Practitioners (ASKP3) member outreach.
**Time to first $:** 30-45 days — ASKP3's member directory and state psychiatric association ketamine-clinic member networks, where small clinics already juggle per-prescriber REMS certification and DEA inventory reconciliation with no dedicated compliance staff.
**Income ceiling (realistic, with math):** $299/mo base + $30/mo per prescriber tracked. Average client (3 prescribers) = $299 + $90 = $389/mo. 24-29 clinics × $389/mo = **$9,336-11,281/mo**.
**Why this can go beyond side money:** Every session opens its own REMS monitoring-documentation event, every prescriber's REMS certification renews on its own cycle, and every controlled-substance delivery reopens the DEA inventory-reconciliation check — a continuous, per-session and per-prescriber compliance stream tied directly to the clinic's treatment volume.
**Biggest risk:** A patient discharged without the full REMS-mandated monitoring window documented, or a ketamine inventory that doesn't reconcile against the administration log, is exactly the finding a REMS audit or a DEA controlled-substance audit is built to catch — the monitoring checklist and the inventory-reconciliation log are the core protections.
**Growth path:** Side project → REMS certification tracker, monitoring checklist, and inventory-reconciliation log deployed, first 5-8 clinic retainers through ASKP3 outreach. Replaces-part-time-income → 13-16 clinics. Replaces-full-time-salary → 24-29 clinics, pushing past $9.34-11.28K/mo.
